# 15. When to Attack

*Part V — Attack and Defence · draft for the second edition*

*This chapter is education, not advice. It names no trade for you, and it promises no result. Where it names a number, the number is arithmetic you can check on paper.*

## The Buyer

1. Security against defeat implies defensive tactics; ability to defeat the enemy means taking the offensive.

2. The seller has read the first half of that sentence for thirteen chapters. The buyer holds the second. Neither half is a whole army.

3. Standing on the defensive indicates insufficient strength; attacking, a superabundance of strength. The buyer's superabundance is not capital. It is price: an attack is strong only when the right to make it has been sold to him too cheaply.

4. There are five grounds on which the buyer may attack. Outside them, he is not an attacker. He is a donor.

5. The first ground is cheap fire: the option whose implied volatility sits below what the market has been delivering and the calendar is about to deliver. He does not buy movement. He buys movement for less than it costs.

6. The second ground is the dated door: a catalyst with a day on it, bought before the crowd has noticed the day, while the rent is still the rent of an ordinary week.

7. The third ground is the long night: the move the sellers price as impossible because they have not seen it for years. The far strike, cheap enough to be lost a hundred times, held for the one night that does not stay dark.

8. The fourth ground is the shield: the put bought behind a book of stock, the wing bought behind a book of short premium. Here he does not attack the market. He attacks his own ruin, and the price of that attack is a known, small, regular expense.

9. The fifth ground is the gap-proof ticket. When the enemy's fire is likely but its direction is not, the debit is the only position whose worst morning has already been paid for.

10. Rapidity is the essence of war: take advantage of the enemy's unreadiness, make your way by unexpected routes, and attack unguarded spots.

11. The seller's unreadiness is his comfort. After a long calm he prices tomorrow as if it were yesterday. That is the unguarded spot, and there is no other.

12. To see victory only when it is within the ken of the common herd is not the acme of excellence. The option the whole market is shouting about has already been priced by the shouting.

13. Do not swallow bait offered by the enemy. A large move on the screen is bait: it invites the buyer to pay for the next move at the price of the last one.

14. Measure before you attack. Read the inward spy, implied volatility, and set him beside the local spy: what price has actually travelled. If the quartermaster charges for a calmer road than the one the army is marching, attack. If he charges for a rougher one, stand aside.

15. Choose the shape of the attack by the ground. When the size of the move is unknown, the single option. When its size is roughly known, the debit spread, which sells the far wing to pay for the near one. When only the date is known, buy both doors and let the event choose.

16. Buy no more time than the catalyst needs, and no less. Too little and the fuse is cut short. Too much and he pays rent on months his reason does not need.

17. Size the attack as a raid, not an invasion: each debit small enough to be lost whole, many times in a row, without changing the size of the next.

18. Before paying, say the five sentences: the edge, the catalyst, the maximum loss, the time stop, and the breakeven win rate. If he cannot name the edge in one breath, as a price, he has no edge.

19. When the attack succeeds, divide the spoils at once. When the date passes and it has not succeeded, leave at once. Between those two, do nothing.

20. He wins his battles by making no mistakes. The buyer's mistakes are four, and none of them is about direction: paying the spike, buying without a date, sizing as if the debit could not go to zero, and rolling a reason that has died.

## The Seller

21. The seller reads this chapter as a list of the days he must not be found on the field.

22. When his wage falls below the fire the market is delivering, he is not being paid to hold the line. He is paying to hold it. On that ground the defender should become the attacker, or leave.

23. Before a dated door, the seller who sold the quiet week sold it to the buyer who arrived early. Let him know who stands on the other side of a cheap ticket.

24. The long night is the seller's catastrophe and the buyer's harvest. Every seller who keeps a wing has bought a little of the night. Every seller who does not has sold all of it.

25. Thus the skilful general is both: defended while the wage is fair, attacking when it is not, and never both on the same direction at once.

## Commentary

The general's chapter on the unbeatable position is usually read for its first half: make yourself invincible, then wait. Thirteen chapters of this book have read it that way, and for the seller that reading is right. But the general did not stop there. In the same chapter he wrote that security against defeat lies in defence, that the ability to defeat the enemy lies in attack, and that the commander skilled in attack "flashes forth from the topmost heights of heaven." He did not despise attack. He despised attack without advantage. The buyer's whole case rests on that word, and in this trade "advantage" has an exact meaning: a price.

That is why the first verse of the buyer's section in Chapter 1 asked for the edge, and why this chapter refuses every other kind. Conviction is not an edge; the seller on the other side of the ticket also has conviction, and he has been paid for his. A strong story is not an edge; the market has heard it. An edge, for the buyer, is a gap between what an option charges for and what is likely to come. It is either cheap fire, a dated event the market has not yet priced, or a tail the sellers have stopped charging for. Everything else in the chapter follows from refusing to attack anywhere the edge cannot be stated as a number.

**Cheap fire** can be checked on paper. A straddle at the money (the call and the put together) costs roughly 0.8 × price × implied volatility × the square root of the time left, in years. Take a stock at 100, implied volatility of 20% a year, and thirty days to expiry. The square root of thirty days over 365 is about 0.29. The straddle costs about 0.8 × 100 × 0.20 × 0.29, or 4.6. That is the size of move, up or down, the market is charging for. Now suppose the stock has actually been moving at 30% a year for the past month. The same formula, with 0.30 in place of 0.20, gives a typical move of about 6.9. The quartermaster is charging for a road a third calmer than the one the army is on.

That gap is the buyer's opening. It is not a promise. Realised volatility is a spy who reports the past, and a market that has been moving fast can go quiet the morning after the buyer pays. The honest point is narrower. On this ground the buyer is not paying the seller's wage; the seller is paying the buyer's. Most of the time, in most names, the gap runs the other way: implied above what is later delivered. That is the wage the seller has collected for thirteen chapters, and it is why most buyers who buy at the average price lose on average. Cheap fire is the exception, and the buyer's art is to wait for it.

**The dated door** works on time rather than on level. As a scheduled event approaches, the options that cover it grow more expensive, because more of the uncertainty in their remaining life is concentrated in a single day. The buyer who arrives early pays the rent of an ordinary week for a ticket that includes the event. If the event moves the market, he is paid. If the price of fear rises as the date approaches, he can also be paid without the move, by selling the ticket into the spike before the news, as Chapter 10 advised. The buyer who arrives late pays for the fear in full and then watches the crush take it away the minute the event is known. The two bought the same right. Only the date of purchase differed, and the date decided which of them was the seller's provisions.

**The long night** is convexity, and it is the ground most often misread. A put struck 20% below the price, on a stock at 100, may cost a tenth of a point when the market has been calm for a long time. If the stock falls to 70 before expiry, that put is worth 10, a hundred times what was paid. The breakeven win rate is one in a hundred. That is the whole of the arithmetic, and the arithmetic does not say that such a fall will come. It says what the ticket costs and what it pays. Bought every month for a year, the same ticket costs 1.20 for every 100 of stock, and in most years every one of the twelve expires worthless.

So the long night is not a raid. It is a garrison, and it must be funded for the length of the night, not for the brightness of the morning that ends it. Two honest limits belong in the same breath. First, the tail is not cheap everywhere. Puts on the broad index are, most of the time, the most expensive options on the board, because every institution that must protect a portfolio buys the same insurance. The tail is cheap only where the sellers have stopped charging for it, usually in a single name or a quiet corner after a long calm. Second, the buyer who cannot hold the garrison through a long run of expiries is worse off than one who never began, because he will abandon it in the year before it pays.

**The shield** is buying in defence, and Chapter 17 gives it a full reading. Here it is enough to say why it belongs among the grounds of attack. Insurance loses money on average by design, as it does on a house. The buyer of a shield is not attacking the market. He is attacking the one enemy that ends accounts: the large loss that resets the base. Chapter 12 gave the arithmetic: minus fifty needs plus a hundred. A small, regular expense that turns a possible minus fifty into a certain minus fifteen has bought not a profit but the survival of the sequence. The seller should recognise this ground at once. His defined-risk spread is a short option and a long one, and the long one is a shield. He has been a buyer since Chapter 4.

**The gap-proof ticket** is the quietest ground. There are days when the fire is likely and its direction is not: a decision pending, a market coiled in a range narrowing toward a date. On such days the seller's spread is capped, but it can reach its cap overnight, in a single gap, with no hour in which to hedge. The buyer's debit cannot be enlarged by any open. If both doors are bought, he has paid in advance for not knowing which one will open. That is not an edge by itself; the straddle is priced, and the price may be fair. But when it is fair, the buyer has the better shape, because his worst morning was settled before the night began.

**The shape of the attack** follows from what is known:

- **Single option:** when only the direction is known, because its payoff grows with the move.
- **Debit spread:** when the size of the move can be roughly estimated. It sells the far strike, the part of the move he does not expect, to pay for the near one. That lowers the debit, and so the win rate he needs: pay 2 for a spread 10 wide, and he must be right one time in five, not one in ten. It gives up the long night in exchange.
- **Straddle:** when only the date is known.

No shape is an edge. Each is a way of matching what he pays for to what he knows, so that he is not charged for a guess he never made.

The verses on **bait and the common herd** are the general's warning against the most common buy of all: the option bought after the move, on the day the move is on every screen. It feels like the safest attack, because the direction has just been shown. It is the most expensive, because everyone who saw the same screen is bidding for the same right, and the seller has raised the price of fire to match. To see victory only when the herd can see it is to pay the herd's price for it.

Last, **size and the exit**, which decide whether any of the five grounds can be held. A buyer whose breakeven is one win in five will lose ten in a row about once in every ten such runs, by plain arithmetic: 0.8 multiplied by itself ten times is just over a tenth. If ten total losses in a row change his size, his mind or his account, he was never on any of the five grounds. He was on desperate ground, which Chapter 11 said does not exist for the buyer, and he built it himself. The exit is the same discipline from the other end. When the attack succeeds, the option is worth mostly fire and time, and both leave quickly, so he takes part of it at once. When the date passes and the attack has not succeeded, the reason is gone, so the option is gone.

The seller's half of this chapter is short because his lesson is short. Every ground on which the buyer may attack is a day on which the seller is paid less than his risk: cheap fire, the early ticket on a dated door, the tail priced as impossible. On those days the defender can do three things. He can buy the wing he should have kept. He can stand aside. Or, if he has the discipline of both halves, he can cross the river and attack. He must not do two of these on the same direction at once: Chapter 7's rule still holds. A long call beside a short put on the same name is one bet in two costumes, whatever the trader calls himself.

The general wrote that the good fighter can secure himself against defeat but cannot make certain of defeating the enemy. That is the honest limit of this chapter. The five grounds do not make the buyer right. They make sure that when he is wrong, as he will be most of the time, he was wrong at a fair price, at a size he could repeat, on a ticket whose worst day he had already paid for. And when the seller has priced the river too calm, he is standing on the far bank when it floods.
