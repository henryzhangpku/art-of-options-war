# 4. The Unbeatable Position

*Part II: Shape and Force*

## The Seller

1. The good fighters of old first put themselves beyond the possibility of defeat, and then waited for an opportunity to defeat the enemy.

2. To be beyond defeat lies in your own hands. To defeat the enemy lies in the enemy's. The seller can make himself unbeatable; he cannot make the market give him a move.

3. Being unbeatable is a matter of defence. Defined risk is the only kind of position in this book, because it is the only kind whose worst day is printed on the ticket before the day begins: the seller's spread, whose width is its ceiling, and the buyer's debit, which is its own.

4. Sell a five-wide spread for one, and the maximum loss is four. Written. Known. Chosen. The market cannot enlarge it. Only the seller can, by selling more.

5. He who sells naked has no shape. He has a wage on ordinary days and a debt without a ceiling on the day that is not ordinary. A position without a ceiling is not a position. It is a wager on the weather.

6. Every policy carries a limit, written on its face before it is sold. In a credit spread the limit is the long wing, and the width less the credit is the most the policy can ever cost.

7. The extra premium earned by dropping the wing is the price of unlimited liability. No premium is large enough to pay that price. Never remove the wing to save its cost; it is the cheapest part of the policy, and the only part that is never wrong.

8. The deductible is the distance from the price to the short strike. A small deductible pays well and is claimed often; a large one pays little and is claimed rarely. Neither is better. The mistake is to forget which one you wrote.

9. The limit protects the account from the world. It does not protect the account from its own size. A limited policy, written too many times, is an unlimited book. The book's limit is the width times the size, and only that number counts.

10. Read the limit aloud before every sale: *the worst this can cost is the width less the credit, times the size.* A seller can be wrong about the weather. He must never be wrong about the limit.

11. The seller who is beyond defeat does not need to know the future. He needs only to have set his line where the future must work hard to arrive, and to have made his loss survivable if it does.

12. Standing on the defensive indicates insufficient strength; attacking, a superabundance of it. The seller has never had a superabundance. He has a small, repeatable wage. On the days his wage is fair, let him stand on the defensive and be content.

13. What the ancients called a clever fighter is one who not only wins, but wins with ease. The seller wins with ease when his line is far and his size is small; every trade that felt like a fight was a trade sized for a different day.

14. The consummate seller cultivates the Method and adheres to discipline. Thus it is in his power to control success.

15. The Method is: max loss first; size so that a maximum loss is an ordinary day, not a ruinous one; cut at the credit; never stack a second position on the same direction.

16. A victorious seller enters the trade having already survived it. A defeated seller enters, and then looks for a way to survive.

## The Buyer

17. The general did not stop at defence. *Security against defeat implies defensive tactics; ability to defeat the enemy means taking the offensive. Standing on the defensive indicates insufficient strength; attacking, a superabundance of strength. The general who is skilled in defence hides in the most secret recesses of the earth; he who is skilled in attack flashes forth from the topmost heights of heaven.*

18. Defence and attack are one sentence in the old text, not two doctrines. The seller has read its first half. The buyer holds the second, and Part V reads the whole of it.

19. The buyer's position is unbeatable from the moment he pays. The debit is his whole risk, printed on the ticket and paid before the day begins. No gap can enlarge it.

20. But unbeatable is not unharmed. A debit can go to zero every time, and a buyer who sizes each one as if it could not is beaten by arithmetic alone.

21. *Remove the firewood from under the pot* (第19计 釜底抽薪). To still a boiling pot, no one ladles the water. A man draws the firewood from beneath it, and the boil dies of itself.

22. A loss is the boiling; the size that feeds it is the firewood. To argue with the price after entry is to ladle, and the pot boils faster for it.

23. Before the first order, write the worst case in one sentence: *my worst case is three hundred dollars, because the debit is three.* Divide the risk budget by that number, and round down. If one contract is over the budget, take a narrower spread, a smaller underlying, or no trade. Never round up to one.

24. Size the debit so that losing all of it is ordinary. Then the buyer may lose many times in a row, which he will, and still be in the field on the day the door opens.

25. Risk one to make ten is a constraint before it is a hope. The one must be small enough to lose ten times in a row and still sit down to count the eleventh.

26. The buyer who is beyond defeat does not need to be right often. He needs to be small when wrong and present when right.

27. Attacking indicates a superabundance. The buyer is the attacker in this trade, and his superabundance is not capital. It is the number of attempts his size allows, and, when he is lucky, a price that has been sold to him too cheaply.

28. A victorious buyer enters having already accepted the loss of the whole debit. A defeated buyer enters, and then begins to hope.

## Commentary

This chapter is the reason the whole book is about defined risk and nothing else. The general's "invincible" has an exact translation: a position whose maximum loss is a number, not a hope. The seller of a vertical spread has read his worst day off the ticket. He may dislike that day, but he cannot be surprised by it, and surprise is the only thing in this trade that ends accounts.

The insurer calls the same thing a limit, and no sane insurer writes a policy without one. Selling an option naked is exactly that policy. Most days a naked short option behaves like a spread with a little more income, because the storm that would reach far beyond the strike does not come. The extra income is the market paying you to remove the limit. It is never enough, because the event it insures against is the one that ends companies, and an insurer is not paid for most days. He is paid for surviving the one day that matters.

The deductible is where the seller's real choice sits. A short strike close to the price is a policy with a small deductible: it is claimed often, and it pays a large premium for that reason. A strike far away is a large deductible: claimed rarely, paid little. Both are legitimate products with different claim rates. The error is mixing the two in your head: writing the near strike for its premium and then being surprised by the claims, or writing the far strike and sizing it as if it could never be claimed.

"First be beyond defeat, then wait" also fixes the order of operations that most traders reverse. They find a direction they believe in, then look for a way to size it. The seller does the opposite: he fixes the size such that the printed maximum loss is a normal, boring expense, and only then asks where the line should be. The line can be wrong; that is Tuesday. The size cannot be wrong, because the size is what decides whether Wednesday happens.

The fair reading of "standing on the defensive indicates insufficient strength" is that it is true, and that is fine. The seller's edge, where it exists, is a small, structural premium for holding a line that others must cross. It is not large enough to survive being attacked with. Whoever treats it as a war chest has misread the ledger. But the first edition of this book stopped reading the general at that line, and he did not stop writing there. In the next breath he says that defence and attack are two halves of one skill: one protects, the other completes the victory. The seller's half is complete in this chapter. The buyer's verse is placed beside it so that the sentence is read whole, and Part V returns to it.

The buyer has the general's invincibility for free: his worst day is the debit, paid in advance, and nothing the market does can make it larger. What he lacks is the seller's frequency of small wins, so his invincibility is tested in a different way, not by one catastrophe but by a long run of total losses on a low win rate. If a long run of debits is more than his account or his nerve can carry, his position was never unbeatable. Only his ticket was. Chapter 16 counts that run.

The firewood is the buyer's version of the limit, and the arithmetic is easy, which is why it is so often skipped. A 100 call bought and a 110 call sold for a debit of 3 has a maximum loss of 300 and a maximum gain of 700. If the risk budget is 1,000, three spreads cost 900 and a fourth would cost 1,200. The count is three. Rounding up to four does not make the risk 1,000. It makes the plan a fiction.

Mai's "risk one to make ten" is often quoted as though the ten were the point. In Schwager's interview the one comes first, and it is small. His bets were expected to fail, most of them, and a book built that way has to survive a run of failures to collect the one that does not. Ten bets at a tenth of the book each, all lost, is the whole book. That is not convexity. It is ten chances to be finished before the thesis arrives.

So the order of operations is the same at both ends of the ticket. The seller fixes the size so that a maximum loss is boring; the buyer fixes it so that a run of total losses is boring. Only then does either of them look for a line, or a door.
