# 3. Winning Without Fighting

*Part I: The Count*

## The Seller

1. In the practical art of trading, the best thing is to take the credit whole and unharmed; to fight for it against a moving market is second best.

2. To win a hundred directional battles is not the height of skill. To be paid without needing direction at all is the height of the seller's skill.

3. Thus the highest form of selling is to be right about where the market will *not* go. The next is to hedge when it goes. The next is to cut. The lowest is to hold to expiry and hope.

4. Ask a buyer what the market will do and he answers up, or down. He has named one door of four.

5. The market may rise far. It may rise and stall. It may fall. It may go nowhere at all. The buyer needs one door. The seller of the fence is paid by the other three.

6. Three doors of four is not three wins in four. The doors are not equal in width, and the one that hurts is wider than any that pays. The market prices them honestly. Take the three doors for what they are: a better shape to be wrong in.

7. The skilful seller subdues the enemy's direction without fighting it. He sets his line where the enemy must travel far and fast to reach it, and he is paid for every day the enemy does not.

8. He does not attack the trend. He sells beneath a rising market and above a falling one, and lets the trend defend his line for him.

9. The rule is: when the tape is calm, collect. When it moves, hedge into the move. When it runs through your line, cut the short and keep the wing. Do not besiege a moving market; a siege costs a season.

10. The highest form of winning without fighting is the fight declined. Underwriting is the art of saying no. A seller who writes every policy offered to him is not an insurer. He is a target.

11. Look at the risk, not the premium. The premium will always look attractive; that is how the bad risks are sold.

12. Some hazards are printed on the calendar: the rate decision, the inflation report, the earnings call, the election night. Writing cover across them at the ordinary rate is writing fire insurance on a house whose owner has already bought matches. A known hazard is excluded, priced higher, or written smaller.

13. Ask who is buying, and why now. When the market is suddenly eager for the exact cover you are selling, at the exact strike, the eagerness is information. The people most keen to buy fire cover are the ones who smell smoke.

14. Some ground is not insurable at any price: a market falling in a straight line, a fear gauge rising day after day, a gap that has not been filled. There the underwriter closes the book and waits.

15. Declining a policy costs nothing but the premium not collected. Writing a bad one can cost the company. Keep a book of the policies you declined, and read it after the storm.

16. Know the enemy and know yourself, and in a hundred trades you will not be in danger. Know yourself but not the enemy, and for every credit you take you will give one back. Know neither, and you will be in danger in every trade.

17. To know the enemy is to know that flat is the market's ordinary state, and that direction is the exception it is famous for.

18. To know yourself is to know the exact loss at which you will leave, and to have left at that loss before.

## The Buyer

19. The buyer cannot win without fighting. He must be right about direction, distance and date. Let him therefore fight only where two of the three are already decided.

20. Direction he borrows from the trend, as the seller does. Date he takes from the calendar, never from a feeling. Only distance is left to his judgement, and it is the one judgement he is paying for.

21. *Besiege Wei to rescue Zhao* (第2计 围魏救赵). When Wei besieged Handan, the relief did not ride to Handan. It rode to Daliang, the enemy's own capital, and the siege broke behind it.

22. The price is Handan: visible, loud, and not where the trade is won. The mispricing is Daliang. Do not attack the price. Attack the reason it is wrong, and say it as one sentence with three parts: *I expect this mispricing, because of this measurable reason, and after costs this structure wins if this condition holds.*

23. *Kill with a borrowed knife* (第3计 借刀杀人). The buyer's knife is borrowed, not forged: an event on a day, a mechanism by which it reprices the stock, and a chain that has not yet charged for either. A catalyst without a date is a rumour. A date without a mechanism is a coin.

24. The sharpest knife is a binary one: a ruling or a regulator's decision, where the stock has two futures and the chain prices one smooth curve. There the buyer need not know which future arrives. He needs the two futures to be priced as one.

25. The buyer names one door of four, and he knows it. His art is to choose the door the market has priced as narrow and the event will make wide.

26. One door in four is not one win in four. The buyer's door, when it opens, is wider than the three that close; that is what he pays for. The market prices this honestly too. His edge, where it exists, is a door priced narrower than it is.

27. The highest form of buying is to pay for a move the seller has forgotten to charge for. The next is to cheapen the debit by selling the far wing. The lowest is to buy the move the whole market is already shouting about.

28. The buyer declines too. A day without a trade that passes all five sentences is not a lost day. It is the buyer's version of the seller's declined book.

29. To know himself, the buyer must know how many debits in a row he can lose without changing his size or his mind. If he has never counted it, the tape will count it for him.

## Commentary

The general's most famous line, that supreme excellence is to win without fighting, is the seller's highest skill. The buyer of an option must be right three times: direction, distance and date. The seller of a spread must be right once: that price stays on his side of a line he chose. He has given away the arrow and kept the fence, and the fence is priced. He is not smarter than the buyer. He has declined the fight the buyer has chosen. The buyer's highest skill is the other half of the general's art, to attack only where the enemy is unguarded, and it is the subject of Part V.

The four doors are the honest version of this. Only a real move, far enough, in time, hurts the seller; stall, reverse and flat all pay him. But the honest half must be said in the same breath: the losing door is also the widest loss. Nothing about the shape of the bet promises a win rate, and a seller who hears "three in four" as "seventy-five percent" has already lost the count. The shape buys tractability, not edge.

The purest form of not fighting is not selling at all. Insurance law has a phrase for the duty the two sides of a policy owe each other, *uberrimae fidei*, utmost good faith, because the business only works if the risk written is the risk described. In the market no counterparty will describe the risk to you honestly. The calendar does it instead. A rate decision or an earnings report inside the term of a policy is a known hazard, and the seller who writes across it at the ordinary premium is not taking a calculated risk. He has not read the form.

Adverse selection is the subtler danger. On an ordinary day the people buying index protection are hedgers paying for sleep, and their buying is why premiums usually run rich. When demand for cover suddenly concentrates, everyone wanting the same puts at the same strikes at once, the buyers are no longer paying for sleep. They know, or fear, something specific. The book of declined policies is the cheapest education in the business. Most of the time it shows a little premium given up. Once in a while it shows the policy that would have been the year's worst claim, and that entry is what teaches a seller to keep saying no.

Knowing yourself, in this chapter, is not a mood. It is a number. The seller who cannot say the price at which he will leave does not know himself, however calm he feels at the open, because the tape will ask him the question when he is least calm.

Read from the other side, the same line is the buyer's warning. He must be right three times, so he cannot afford to be careless about any of the three. The disciplined buyer does not make three forecasts; he takes two of them from somewhere other than himself. Direction comes from the trend's structure, date from a scheduled event, and only distance remains a judgement.

The two stratagems are the buyer's answer to "win without fighting." He cannot avoid the fight, but he can choose where it is fought, and the relief of Handan is the lesson: ride to the place the price must return to, not to the place it is shouting from. Each clause of the three-part sentence can fail, and that is its use. Most ideas fail at the first clause, because "it goes up" names a direction and calls it a mispricing.

The binary knife is where the book's second voice first speaks. Jamie Mai, as he described his practice to Schwager in *Hedge Fund Market Wizards* and as Lewis tells it in *The Big Short*, attacked the shape of a price rather than the direction of a stock. Where a company's future hung on one decision, a court or a regulator or a question of solvency, the option chain still priced a smooth spread of outcomes, as if the stock could land anywhere along a gentle curve. In fact it had two futures. The cheap end of that curve could be bought without knowing which future would arrive, because the curve itself was wrong. That is the siege of Daliang: the pricing is the wall, and the stock's direction rescues itself. It is also the buyer's whole case in one picture. He does not win by forecasting better than the seller. He wins, when he wins, because the seller has priced a two-humped world as one hump.

The four doors work against the buyer and for him in the same breath. He is paid by one door only, but it is the widest in the house, and the market charges him for the width in advance. Nothing about the shape of a long option is an edge, any more than the shape of a spread is. The buyer's edge, when there is one, is that the door has been priced too narrow, by sellers who have watched it stay shut for so long that they have stopped charging for the day it opens.
