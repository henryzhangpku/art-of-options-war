# 3. Winning Without Fighting

*Part I: The Count*

1. In the practical art of trading, the best thing is to take the credit whole and unharmed; to fight for it against a moving market is second best.

2. To win a hundred directional battles is not the height of skill. To be paid without needing direction at all — that is the height of skill.

3. Thus the highest form of selling is to be right about where the market will *not* go. The next is to hedge when it goes. The next is to cut. The lowest is to hold to expiry and hope.

4. Ask a buyer what the market will do and he answers up, or down. He has named one door of four.

5. The market may rise far. It may rise and stall. It may fall. It may go nowhere at all. The buyer needs one door. The seller of the fence is paid by the other three.

6. Three doors of four is not three wins in four. The doors are not equal in width, and the one that hurts is wider than any that pays. The market prices them honestly. Take the three doors for what they are: a better shape to be wrong in.

7. The skilful seller subdues the enemy's direction without fighting it. He sets his line where the enemy must travel far and fast to reach it, and he is paid for every day the enemy does not.

8. He does not attack the trend. He sells beneath a rising market and above a falling one, and lets the trend defend his line for him.

9. The rule is: when the tape is calm, collect. When it moves, hedge into the move. When it runs through your line, cut the short and keep the wing. Do not besiege a moving market; a siege costs a season.

10. Know the enemy and know yourself, and in a hundred trades you will not be in danger. Know yourself but not the enemy, and for every credit you take you will give one back. Know neither, and you will be in danger in every trade.

11. To know the enemy is to know that flat is the market's ordinary state, and that direction is the exception it is famous for.

12. To know yourself is to know the exact loss at which you will leave, and to have left at that loss before.

## Commentary

The general's most famous line — that supreme excellence is to win without fighting — is the seller's whole thesis. The buyer of an option must be right three times: direction, distance and date. The seller of a spread must be right once: that price stays on his side of a line he chose. He has given away the arrow and kept the fence, and the fence is priced. He is not smarter than the buyer. He has simply refused the fight the buyer insists on.

The four doors are the honest version of this. Only a real move, far enough, in time, hurts the seller; stall, reverse and flat all pay him. But the honest half must be said in the same breath: the losing door is also the widest loss. Nothing about the shape of the bet promises a win rate, and a seller who hears "three in four" as "seventy-five percent" has already lost the count. The shape buys tractability, not edge.

Knowing yourself, in this chapter, is not a mood. It is a number. The seller who cannot say the price at which he will leave does not know himself, however calm he feels at the open, because the tape will ask him the question when he is least calm.
