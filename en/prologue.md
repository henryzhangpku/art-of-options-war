# Before the Temple Count

Two and a half thousand years ago a general wrote thirteen short chapters on how to win without being destroyed. He was not interested in glory. He was interested in the arithmetic of survival: which battles to fight, which to refuse, how to be paid by an enemy's mistakes instead of your own courage, and why the state that wins every battle can still lose the war.

Options are the same problem in a different uniform, and the uniform has two sides. The seller is paid to hold a line. He does not need to know where the enemy will go, only where the enemy will not arrive in time. He wins most engagements and can be ruined by one. The buyer pays for the right to be right. He loses most engagements, each one small and paid for in advance, and lives on the rare one that pays many times over. Every chapter that follows borrows the old general's structure, thirteen chapters in his order and on his subjects, and reads it from both ends of a defined-risk ticket: first the seller of the credit spread, then the buyer of the option or the debit spread, each of whom intends to still be trading next year.

The seller has an older trade than war to learn from. In 1688 Edward Lloyd kept a coffee house near the Thames, and the merchants who drank there did a strange thing. When a ship was about to sail, its owner wrote the voyage on a slip of paper, and men with money and nerve wrote their names underneath. Each took a share of the loss if the ship went down, and each was paid a premium for it. They were called underwriters because they wrote under. None of them knew which ship would sink. Every one of them knew, roughly, how many. That is the difference between a gambler and an insurer, and it runs through the seller's half of this book: he prices a fleet, writes many small shares, refuses the voyages he cannot price, keeps money aside for the ones that sink, and is paid for the arithmetic, not for being right.

The buyer has an older book than this one too. The Thirty-Six Stratagems, 三十六计, are tricks played on an enemy: show weakness where you are strong, offer a bait worth taking, let the enemy's own certainty do the work. A stratagem succeeds less because the enemy is foolish than because he sees what he expects to see. In trading, the enemy most often tricked is yourself. So the buyer's sections quote the stratagems where they fit, each cited by its number, and the whole thirty-six stand at the back of the book. Beside them runs a second voice: Jamie Mai, a buyer of cheap convexity, as told in Jack Schwager's *Hedge Fund Market Wizards* and Michael Lewis's *The Big Short*, together with the record of a test that tried to copy his method by machine and failed.

This second edition adds a fifth part, Attack and Defence, in five chapters. The general's thirteen teach each side to survive its own arithmetic. They do not answer the general's own question: when to defend, and when to attack. Part V does, and in it the buyer speaks first.

This book is education, not advice. It contains no recommendations for you personally, no signals and no promises. Options can lose money faster than stock. A defined-risk spread cannot lose more than its width, and a long option cannot lose more than its debit, but either can still be more than you can afford, and a run of them can still end an account. Insurers go bankrupt too; the ones that last are the ones that never forgot it. Nothing here is a performance claim. Where the text names a number, the number is arithmetic you can check on paper.

Read one chapter a day. Then read them again, in the order the general chose, because the order is the argument: count before you fight, know what a campaign costs, win without needing to be right, make yourself unbeatable before you look for a chance, value information above every other weapon, and only then choose whether today is a day to defend or to attack.
