# 5. Force and the Four Quarters

*Part II: Shape and Force*

## The Seller

1. The control of a large force is the same in principle as the control of a few: it is a matter of dividing it.

2. Divide the day's risk in four before the day divides it for you.

3. One quarter for the direction you took. One for the hedge you will need. One for the direction that returns. One for the fluctuation you must simply survive.

4. Full size on the first entry is not conviction. It is the absence of a plan for being wrong.

5. In all fighting the direct method is used for joining battle, and the indirect for securing victory. The direct method is the first spread, sold in the direction of the trend. The indirect is the second side, sold only when the first is hurt, which turns a bet into a condor.

6. The direct and the indirect lead on to each other in turn. It is like moving in a circle: you never come to an end. The trend read, the hedge, the trend again, the exit: the whole day is four turns of one wheel.

7. Energy is the bending of the crossbow; timing is the release of the trigger. The seller's energy is his credit already collected. His timing is the stop.

8. The quality of decision is like the well-timed swoop of a falcon which enables it to strike and destroy its victim. The stop is not a retreat. It is the swoop.

9. Division is false if the parts are the same part. A hundred policies on the same coast are one policy. The hurricane does not visit them one at a time.

10. Puts sold on the index, on the technology fund, and on the five largest companies in the index are three names and one coast. On a quiet day they move separately. On the day of the storm they move together. Correlation is a fair-weather word.

11. Add up the book by coast, not by ticket. Ask how much the whole book pays if the market falls five percent before the term ends. That number is the real size, and it gets a limit of its own.

12. Time is a coast too. Ten policies all expiring on the same afternoon are one claim waiting for one bad afternoon. Spread the terms.

13. A put spread and a call spread on the same index, sold together, cover two coasts that one move cannot reach at once. That is a real reduction in risk. But both are written on one sea, and the condor is one policy with two sides, not two policies.

14. Compounding is the momentum of a round stone rolled down a mountain. Ten in a hundred, kept and rolled thirty times, becomes a number that no longer looks like ten.

15. Double your money and lose half, and you are exactly where you began, having paid for the ride. The account does not remember your best day. It remembers the product of all of them.

16. Therefore the good seller seeks his result from the shape of the sequence, not from any single battle. He does not demand too much of one trade. He demands that the next trade be possible.

17. Small and repeatable outruns large and rare, and it does it quietly. Boring is the product.

## The Buyer

18. The buyer divides his force too, but by time rather than by side.

19. He does not buy the whole position at once. One part when the setup appears, one when the catalyst is near, one when the market confirms. A ladder, not a leap.

20. Each rung is sized so that if the trade ends at that rung, the loss is that rung's debit alone.

21. The buyer's energy is the asymmetry he has paid for; his timing is the catalyst. Bought too early, the energy leaks away as rent. Bought too late, it has already been spent by others.

22. *Clamour in the east, strike in the west* (第6计 声东击西). Drums in the east, the army crosses in the west. The noise is not a lie about the war. It is a lie about the place.

23. The edge at one level is not the edge at another. A finding about stocks remains a finding about stocks until it has been tested in the option that will carry it. The option adds a clock, a spread and a price of fear that the stock never had.

24. Strike where the edge lands. Sometimes the option is the cheap expression. Sometimes it is the shares, and the option only adds a clock the thesis does not need.

25. Five call spreads in five names that rise on the same rate and fall on the same fear are not five attacks. They are one attack bought five times, and they get one budget between them.

26. *Chain stratagems* (第35计 连环计). A trade is a chain: the calendar, the five sentences, the size, the alert, the log, the review. One weak link and the rest is cargo. A chain is not a mood; it is a routine that runs in the weeks you do not feel like running it.

27. Compounding is harsher on the buyer than on the seller, because his results arrive rarely and in large pieces. A long run of small losses shrinks the base on which the rare win must multiply.

28. Therefore the buyer's falcon is the time stop and the profit taken. A long option that has doubled is not yet money. Held to expiry, it often turns back into a lesson.

## Commentary

The general's chapter on energy is usually read as a chapter on momentum, and the seller's momentum is compounding. Returns multiply; they do not add. A plus-one-hundred followed by a minus-fifty is two times one half: exactly nothing, after a month of adrenaline. The same account taking a smaller figure without a reset ends the period on a number that looks like an error. This is not a claim about markets; it is what multiplication does, and it can be checked on paper in a minute.

The four quarters are the seller's division of force. The first quarter is the read: one spread, in the direction of the trend, at a quarter of what you are willing to lose today. The second is the hedge, sold only when the first side starts to hurt, which completes an iron condor instead of closing in fear or doubling in pride. The third is the re-entry when the turn comes, on better information than the open offered. The fourth buys nothing and earns nothing: it is the room to let an open position breathe against you without being closed by your broker or by yourself.

The insurer adds the warning that a division can be an illusion. Insurers learned accumulation the expensive way. A company could write thousands of home policies, each one modest, and discover after a single hurricane that most of them stood within forty miles of the same shoreline. Every policy passed the individual size check. The book as a whole had written one enormous policy on one storm. The seller's coasts are less visible and just as real: a put spread on the index, another on the technology fund and a third on a large technology stock behave like three positions on an ordinary day and like one on the day the market falls hard. The question that replaces the count of tickets is a single sentence, asked before every new position: *what else in the book pays out on the same day, for the same reason?*

The Thirty-Six Stratagems have the same picture at Red Cliffs. The northern fleet lashed its ships together so that they would ride steady in the swell, and the steadiness was real. So was the price: one fire, set at one end, reached every ship. A book of policies chained by one factor is that fleet. It rides more smoothly than a scattered one in calm water, and it burns all at once.

Nothing in this chapter says you will earn ten percent at any interval, ever. It says only that if you ever do have a repeatable process, what decides where you finish is not the size of your best result. It is whether the sequence stays unbroken, and the stop, the falcon's swoop, is what keeps it so.

The buyer's division is by time. A ladder of entries, the first small at the setup, the next as the catalyst approaches, the last only on confirmation, lets him pay less for being early and commit more only once the market has agreed. It also keeps the first loss, which is the most common one, the smallest.

The sixth stratagem is the buyer's warning about where his force lands. A study can find a real effect in stocks, a group of names that doubled far more often than names picked at random, and the effect can vanish in the options. In one such test, of 87 cases where a call spread could have expressed the idea, only 10 were tradeable at all, and one of those ten won. The direction was right and the structure still lost, because the move came late, or small, or already paid for. That is not a weak version of a strong stock result. It is a different trade wearing the same name. So the buyer asks where his edge actually lands before he chooses the instrument, and sometimes the honest answer is the shares, or nothing.

The chain is what makes the ladder possible. No single link is impressive: an hour on Sunday with the calendar, the five sentences said before each entry, the size divided down, the exit written while the head is cool, the log kept in facts rather than verdicts, the week reviewed. Together they turn a rare winner into something findable, and they hold in the weeks when the trader does not feel like holding them. A size rounded up once undoes every calculation before it, which is the whole meaning of the image.

Compounding is the buyer's harder lesson. His returns come rarely and in large pieces, and multiplication punishes the gaps between them. Twenty debits of one percent each, all lost, leave the account at about eighty-two percent of itself; the win that follows is measured against that smaller base, not the original. The arithmetic can be checked on paper, and it tells the buyer what it tells the seller: the sequence decides, and the sequence is protected by size.
