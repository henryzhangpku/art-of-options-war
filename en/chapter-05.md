# 5. Force and the Four Quarters

*Part II: Shape and Force*

1. The control of a large force is the same in principle as the control of a few: it is a matter of dividing it.

2. Divide the day's risk in four before the day divides it for you.

3. One quarter for the direction you took. One for the hedge you will need. One for the direction that returns. One for the fluctuation you must simply survive.

4. Full size on the first entry is not conviction. It is the absence of a plan for being wrong.

5. In all fighting the direct method is used for joining battle, and the indirect for securing victory. The direct method is the first spread, sold in the direction of the trend. The indirect is the second side, sold only when the first is hurt, which turns a bet into a condor.

6. The direct and the indirect lead on to each other in turn. It is like moving in a circle: you never come to an end. The trend read, the hedge, the trend again, the exit — the whole day is four turns of one wheel.

7. Energy is the bending of the crossbow; timing is the release of the trigger. The seller's energy is his credit already collected. His timing is the stop.

8. The quality of decision is like the well-timed swoop of a falcon which enables it to strike and destroy its victim. The stop is not a retreat. It is the swoop.

9. Compounding is the momentum of a round stone rolled down a mountain. Ten in a hundred, kept and rolled thirty times, becomes a number that no longer looks like ten.

10. Double your money and lose half, and you are exactly where you began, having paid for the ride. The account does not remember your best day. It remembers the product of all of them.

11. Therefore the good seller seeks his result from the shape of the sequence, not from any single battle. He does not demand too much of one trade. He demands that the next trade be possible.

12. Small and repeatable outruns large and rare, and it does it quietly. Boring is the product.

## Commentary

The general's chapter on energy is usually read as a chapter on momentum, and the seller's momentum is compounding. Returns multiply; they do not add. A plus-one-hundred followed by a minus-fifty is two times one half — exactly nothing, after a month of adrenaline. The same account taking a smaller figure without a reset ends the period on a number that looks like an error. This is not a claim about markets; it is what multiplication does, and it can be checked on paper in a minute.

The four quarters are the seller's division of force. The first quarter is the read: one spread, in the direction of the trend, at a quarter of what you are willing to lose today. The second is the hedge, sold only when the first side starts to hurt, which completes an iron condor instead of closing in fear or doubling in pride. The third is the re-entry when the turn comes, on better information than the open offered. The fourth buys nothing and earns nothing: it is the room to let an open position breathe against you without being closed by your broker or by yourself.

Nothing in this chapter says you will earn ten percent at any interval, ever. It says only that if you ever do have a repeatable process, what decides where you finish is not the size of your best result. It is whether the sequence stays unbroken — and the stop, the falcon's swoop, is what keeps it so.
