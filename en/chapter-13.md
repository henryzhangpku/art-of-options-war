# 13. Spies

*Part IV: Ground and Fire*

## The Seller

1. What enables the wise sovereign and the good general to strike and conquer, and achieve things beyond the reach of ordinary men, is foreknowledge.

2. Foreknowledge cannot be elicited from spirits; it cannot be obtained inductively from experience, nor by any deductive calculation. Knowledge of the enemy's dispositions can only be obtained from other men.

3. Forecasts are spirits. The seller does not consult them. He consults the market's dispositions: what it has done, where it is leaning, what it has priced. These are obtained from the tape, not from the oracle.

4. There are five classes of spies: local spies, inward spies, converted spies, doomed spies, and surviving spies.

5. The seller has five spies, and he pays them differently.

6. Local spies are price itself: the pivots, the swings, the structure. They are cheap, they are everywhere, and they are honest. They tell you what the market has done and, by its shape, what it has decided.

7. Inward spies are volatility: the market's own officials, reporting what it fears. Implied volatility is the enemy's quartermaster telling you how much protection is being bought and at what price. Read him before every trade; he sets the wage.

8. Converted spies are positioning: the dealer's hedges, the walls of gamma, the levels where the enemy's own soldiers must buy or sell against their will. A converted spy works for the enemy and tells you where the enemy is forced to go. Of all five, he is the most valuable, and the one most traders never recruit.

9. Doomed spies are the news: information sent out to be believed, and to be acted upon by those who do not check it against the other four. Every headline is a doomed spy. Let him carry his message to the crowd.

10. Surviving spies are those who bring back news from the enemy's camp. The seller's surviving spy is his own journal: the record of every trade, the reason it was taken, the price at which it was cut or held, and what happened after. He is the only spy who has never lied, and he is the only one most traders refuse to keep.

11. Hence it is that with none in the whole army are more intimate relations maintained than with spies; none should be more liberally rewarded. Reward the journal with honesty. Write the trade down before it is over. Write the reason, not the outcome.

12. Spies cannot be usefully employed without a certain intuitive sagacity; they cannot be properly managed without benevolence and straightforwardness. Nor can a journal be usefully kept by a man who edits it to feel better.

13. The surviving spy's report fits on one line. An insurance company is judged by one number, the combined ratio: claims paid plus the costs of doing business, divided by the premium earned. Below one hundred, the company made money underwriting. Above one hundred, it paid its customers to be its customers.

14. The seller's costs are many and small: the commission on every fill, the spread between bid and ask, the slippage of a stop in a fast market. Each is a cent. Together they are often the whole margin. The cheapest cost to cut is the trade not made.

15. Keep the ratio monthly, from the journal, not from the account balance. The balance mixes luck with skill. The ratio separates premium from claims, and claims from costs.

16. A ratio below one hundred in a quiet month proves very little; the storm has not been counted yet. Judge it over a year that includes bad weather. A ratio above one hundred is not a reason to write more. It is a reason to stop and find which line is wrong: the price, the size, the selection, or the claims.

17. If one kind of policy carries the whole book's losses, stop writing that kind. Insurers drop lines of business every year; traders rarely drop a setup. The insurers are right.

18. Whether the object be to crush an army, to storm a city, or to assassinate an individual, it is always necessary to begin by finding out the names of the attendants and the sentries. Before the line is sold, find out the names: the expected move, the wall, the floor, the event, the volatility rank. These are the sentries. The trade that begins without them begins blind.

19. Hence it is only the enlightened ruler and the wise general who will use the highest intelligence of the army for purposes of spying, and thereby they achieve great results. Spies are a most important element in trading, because upon them depends the seller's ability to move at all.

20. The seller who has sold a thousand lines and written down none of them has fought a thousand battles and learned the lesson of one. The seller who has written down a hundred has a spy in every future camp.

## The Buyer

21. The buyer keeps the same five spies, and trusts the inward spy most.

22. Implied volatility tells the seller his wage and tells the buyer his price. A buyer who does not read it before every trade does not know whether he is buying a bargain or the seller's best day.

23. The converted spy tells the buyer where the dealers will be forced to chase: when the wall breaks, the enemy's own hedges push price in the buyer's direction.

24. *Let the enemy's spy sow discord* (第33计 反间计). A letter left on the desk for the spy to steal has done its work before it is read; the hand that kills is the enemy's own.

25. The option chain is the enemy's spy in the buyer's camp, reporting every day what others fear. The implied move is his price, skew is his accent, and the term structure is his calendar: next week's fear and next year's fear, priced apart.

26. An intercepted report is useful twice: for what it says, and for why someone wished it said. Stale data is a double agent. It answers with yesterday's map and charges today's price.

27. The deepest secret in the report is its shape: a smooth curve laid over an event that can only go two ways. Read where the spy's map is wrong, and let the enemy's own intelligence point the road.

28. The buyer's surviving spy is the journal too, and for every trade it holds the five sentences, written before entry and never after.

29. Without the journal the buyer remembers the one option that rose tenfold and forgets the forty that expired. With it, he can count whether the forty and the one together paid. That count is his combined ratio: the debits lost and the costs paid, set against what the few winners returned.

## Commentary

The general ends with spies because everything before, the count, the ground, the fire, depends on knowing, and knowing comes from people rather than from prayer. The seller's translation is that forecasts are the spirits the old text forbids consulting, and the market's dispositions are the men. Price tells him what has been decided. Volatility tells him what protection costs and therefore what he will be paid. Positioning tells him where the other side is forced to act against its will, which is the single most useful fact a seller can own about a line. The news tells the crowd what to think, and he lets it.

The surviving spy is the chapter's real subject. A journal that records the reason for every trade, at the time it was taken, is the only instrument that can tell a seller whether his losses came from bad ground, bad size, or the Commander's five faults. Without it he remembers his best trades and his worst, and nothing in between, and the pattern that is killing him is always in between. It must be written before the outcome is known, and it must be kept by a man willing to read it.

The insurer gives the surviving spy a single number to report. Take the premium collected on every spread over a month. Take the cost of every claim, the losses on positions that were tested and closed. Add every cost of trading: commissions, the bid-ask spread paid on entry and exit, the slippage of stops filled in fast markets. Divide claims plus costs by premium. If a month collected one thousand in premium, paid seven hundred in claims and one hundred and twenty in costs, the ratio is eighty-two: eighteen of every hundred collected stayed in the business. The costs are where most sellers quietly lose. A spread that collects a small credit and is opened, adjusted and closed several times can pay most of its premium away in tolls before the market has done anything at all. The costs never appear as one large loss, so they are rarely noticed, and the ratio is the only place they become visible. The cheapest improvement most books can make is not a better forecast but fewer round trips. And when every loss in the journal comes from the same setup, the same hour or the same event week, the seller has learned what an insurer would act on at once: drop the line.

For the buyer the inward spy matters most. Implied volatility is the price of the right he wants, and the whole of his edge, where it exists, is a gap between that price and the move that follows. The journal matters as much to him as to the seller, and for a sharper reason: a buyer's results are dominated by rare large wins, and memory keeps those and drops the long run of small losses that paid for them. Only a written count can say whether the one paid for the forty.

The thirty-third stratagem is the buyer's way of recruiting the seller's own spy. The stratagem is not bribery. It is making the other side's judgement do your work. The chain is a record of what other people paid to be afraid of, and its use is to show where that fear is priced too high or too low. Its deepest report is the two-humped world priced as one hump, which Chapter 3 read with Mai: the spy's map is wrong at its edges, and that is where it is read.

The last verse of the general's thirteen is the book's own sentence about itself. Thirteen chapters, in a general's order, on a single trade: count before you fight; know the cost of a campaign; win without needing to be right; be unbeatable before you look for a chance; divide your force in four; respond, do not predict; march slowly and cut fast; know the roads not to take; read the signs; refuse the wrong ground; know which of the nine situations you are in; survive fire; and keep the spy who never lies. None of it will make you right. It makes being wrong survivable, and survival is the only thing that compounds.

The buyer reads the same thirteen chapters and arrives at the mirror of the same sentence: say the five sentences before you pay; know the rent; fight only where direction and date are already decided; be small enough to lose many times; buy by a ladder, not a leap; buy what is cheap, not what is loud; march slowly and take profit fast; leave when the date passes; buy the coil, not the press; refuse the spike before the event; know that the middle of the trade is where the rent is paid; let fire come to you rather than pay for it in the blaze; and keep the journal that counts the forty losers beside the one winner.

Neither side is the right side. Each is a way of being wrong that can be survived, if it is counted. But the general did not end his career at Spies, and this book does not end here. Spies come before the choice of stance because a trader cannot choose between attack and defence until he has read the inward spy and knows what the fire costs today. Part V makes that choice.
