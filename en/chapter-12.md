# 12. Fire

*Part IV: Ground and Fire*

## The Seller

1. There are five ways of attacking with fire. The first is to burn soldiers in their camp; the second is to burn stores; the third is to burn baggage trains; the fourth is to burn arsenals and magazines; the fifth is to hurl dropping fire amongst the enemy.

2. Volatility is fire. There are five ways the fire reaches the seller, and he must know each by its smoke.

3. The first burns the camp: the gap at the open, when the line set last night is already behind the price. There is no defence in the position; the defence was the size.

4. The second burns the stores: the slow bleed of a chopping market that crosses the line, retreats, and crosses again, charging a cut each time. This fire does not kill. It starves.

5. The third burns the baggage: the margin call, when a spread's marked loss exceeds the fourth quarter and the broker closes what the seller would have held. It is the fire the seller lights himself, by sizing for the outcome he hoped for.

6. The fourth burns the arsenal: the volatility spike that makes every strike expensive at once, so the hedge that was cheap in the morning costs half the credit in the afternoon. The seller who did not hedge when hedging was cheap has lost his arsenal to the same fire that made him need it.

7. The fifth is dropping fire: the news that arrives while the position is open, a headline in the middle of serious ground. Against this there is only the wing.

8. In order to carry out an attack with fire, the means must always be ready. In order to survive one, the means must be ready sooner: the stop set in the temple, the wing already bought, the fourth quarter untouched.

9. When fire breaks out inside the enemy's camp, respond at once with an attack from without. When volatility breaks out inside your position, respond at once: the hedge into reversal if the move is young, the cut of the short if it is not. Fire waits for no one, and the seller who waits to see if it spreads is the one it spreads through.

10. Beyond the five fires is the sixth, which burns every camp at once. Insurers do not go broke on ordinary claims. They go broke on catastrophes: the one event that sends every claim in the book on the same morning.

11. The market calls these events rare. It has delivered one every few years. On one Monday in October 1987 the index fell by a fifth in a single session. In February 2018 the market's fear gauge more than doubled in a day, and products built on its calm were closed for good. In February and March 2020 the index lost about a third in a few weeks.

12. A catastrophe is not a large ordinary day. It is a different kind of day: the gaps skip the stops, the quotes widen, the exits close, and every coast is hit together.

13. Run the storm in advance. Ask: if the index gaps five percent overnight, what does the book owe in the morning? If it falls ten percent in a week? Write the answers while the sun is shining. If the paper storm closes the account, the size is wrong today, not on the day it comes.

14. A stop is a plan for an ordinary day. In a catastrophe the market opens beyond it. Only the wing is still where you left it.

15. The worst storms come after the longest calm, because the calm is when everyone writes the most cover at the thinnest premium. Do not try to predict the catastrophe. Its date is unknowable. Its existence is certain.

16. Unhappy is the fate of one who tries to win his battles without cultivating the spirit of enterprise; for the result is waste of time and general stagnation. Unhappier is the seller who sells every day without cultivating the discipline of the stop; for the result is a hundred wages and one fire that takes them all.

17. Move not unless you see an advantage; use not your troops unless there is something to be gained; fight not unless the position is critical. Sell not unless the count says sell. Hedge not unless hurt. Cut not unless the line is broken — and then, cut.

18. No ruler should put troops into the field merely to gratify his own spleen; no general should fight a battle simply out of pique. No seller should sell because he is angry at the market for the last trade. Anger has never been a read.

19. A kingdom that has once been destroyed can never come again into being; nor can the dead ever be brought back to life. An account that has been halved needs a double to return, and one that has lost four-fifths needs five times itself. Every large loss does not merely subtract; it resets the base that everything afterward multiplies.

20. Hence the enlightened ruler is heedful, and the good general full of caution. This is the way to keep an account at peace and a sequence intact.

## The Buyer

21. Fire burns the seller and warms the buyer. Volatility is the buyer's ally, but only the volatility he paid for before it arrived.

22. The buyer who buys during the fire pays the fire's price. Options are dearest while the smoke is rising, and their value falls the moment it clears.

23. *Loot a burning house* (第5计 趁火打劫). The looter does not light the fire. He stands at the door and takes what the fire throws out. But he measures the fire first.

24. Divide the at-the-money straddle by the share price; that is the move the chain expects from the event. Set it against the last eight to twelve moves the stock actually made after the same event. Well above that history, with volatility high in its range, the fire is overpriced and the buyer is the one being looted. Well below it, with volatility near its lows, the event is being sold cheap. Between the two there is no trade, only a price.

25. There is a second fire, and it is not in the chain: bad news that has pushed a price below what the business can survive at. There the work is not volatility but solvency, and it is read in the filings. Two fires, two measures. If you cannot say which one you are looting, you are standing in the third, the one you lit yourself.

26. *Watch the fire from across the river* (第9计 隔岸观火). The house on the far bank is burning. The general does not cross to loot it. He lets the fire finish its argument, and counts what is left when the smoke clears.

27. A priced event is a settled argument. Watching is a position: flat has a profit and loss of zero and keeps the quiver full. "I have no view" is not the same as "there is no edge", and sometimes the edge is the refusal to pay for one.

28. Dropping fire, the news in the middle of the trade, is the buyer's one free gift. He may receive it; he must not pay for it in advance at full price.

29. The buyer's own fire is the crush: the event comes, moves the market a little, and leaves the option worth less than before. He was right about the direction and paid for a larger move than came.

30. The seller's catastrophe is the buyer's harvest, and the one fire the buyer may buy in advance at a low price, because it is the one the sellers have stopped charging for. Part V gives it a chapter.

## Commentary

The chapter on fire is the general's chapter on the weapon that does not care about skill, and for the seller that weapon is volatility. It arrives as a gap that puts the line behind the price before the first order can be placed; as a spike that makes the hedge unaffordable in the exact hour it becomes necessary; as a headline in the middle of a working trade. None of these can be predicted. All of them can be survived, and survival is arranged in advance: the size that makes a gap an ordinary expense, the wing that is always present because the spread always contains one, the reserve that keeps the broker's hand off the position.

The two fires the seller lights himself deserve naming. The margin call is size. The starving fire, the chop that charges a cut, then a re-entry, then a cut, is frequency: the chapter on manoeuvring returned as a slow burn. Neither is the market's fault, and both are ended by the same two rules that were already on the page: size so that the maximum loss is boring, and give the day a structural ceiling on how many times it can open.

The insurer's word for the sixth fire is catastrophe, and the industry learned it at great cost. When Hurricane Andrew struck Florida in 1992, the losses left several insurers insolvent. Most of them had priced their ordinary policies reasonably well. What they had not done was add up what the whole book would owe if one storm hit the whole coast in one night. The lesson the surviving industry took was not to forecast hurricanes better. It was to run the catastrophe in advance, on paper, and to refuse any size the paper storm could not survive. The option seller's catastrophes are part of the public record, and they come more often than the word *rare* suggests. In each of them, sellers who had sized for ordinary days met a day that was not ordinary, and the claims arrived together, at the open, through the stops. That is why this book insists on the wing. A stop is an instruction for a market that trades continuously; in a gap, the market opens past it and the fill comes wherever liquidity returns. The wing is not an instruction but a contract, and it is the one protection that is where you left it on the morning after. Its premium is the cost of making the catastrophe a number rather than a surprise.

The last verses are the arithmetic of ruin, stated by a general who had seen kingdoms end. Minus fifty needs plus one hundred. Minus eighty needs plus four hundred. This is not a warning about markets; it is what multiplication does to a sequence with a hole in it. It is also why the stop, the subject of nearly every chapter, is the only rule in the book with no exceptions. Every later chapter that needs this arithmetic points back here.

For the buyer, fire is the reason for the trade. A long option is a bet that volatility will arrive, and its value is a claim on the fire. But the claim must be bought before the smoke, at a price set by people who do not expect a fire. A buyer who pays during the spike buys a claim the market has already marked up, and the crush that follows the event, volatility falling as soon as the news is known, can take his profit even when the move went his way.

The two stratagems divide the buyer's fires into the one he may loot and the one he should watch. The looter's rule is a measurement that fits on the back of a card: the straddle divided by the price is the move the chain expects, and the stock's own history after the same kind of event is the move it has usually made. A wide gap either way is information; a narrow one is a fair price, and a fair price is not a trade, however strong the opinion. The second fire, the business sold below what it can be worth, is not a volatility trade: no screen on the chain finds it; it is read in the balance sheet. The watcher's rule is the default for everything else. In one count across thirty-eight rate decisions, the median move after the announcement was smaller than the move the day's straddle had priced, and the direction was close to a coin flip. The median is not the mean, and a few decisions produced moves far larger than their price, so the finding says that most such events are priced, not that all are. What survives is procedural. Watching costs nothing and keeps the right to act. Crossing the river costs the straddle and then the decay, and it must be justified by a mispricing written down before the event, with the number that would show it wrong.

So the same two rules hold for the buyer. Size so that losing the whole debit is boring, and give the day a ceiling on how many debits it can spend. The fire that ruins a buyer is not one catastrophe. It is the slow burn of paying, again and again, for fires that do not come.
