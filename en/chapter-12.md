# 12. Fire

*Part IV: Ground and Fire*

1. There are five ways of attacking with fire. The first is to burn soldiers in their camp; the second is to burn stores; the third is to burn baggage trains; the fourth is to burn arsenals and magazines; the fifth is to hurl dropping fire amongst the enemy.

2. Volatility is fire. There are five ways the fire reaches the seller, and he must know each by its smoke.

3. The first burns the camp: the gap at the open, when the line set last night is already behind the price. There is no defence in the position; the defence was the size.

4. The second burns the stores: the slow bleed of a chopping market that crosses the line, retreats, and crosses again, charging a cut each time. This fire does not kill. It starves.

5. The third burns the baggage: the margin call, when a spread's marked loss exceeds the fourth quarter and the broker closes what the seller would have held. It is the fire the seller lights himself, by sizing for the outcome he hoped for.

6. The fourth burns the arsenal: the volatility spike that makes every strike expensive at once, so the hedge that was cheap in the morning costs half the credit in the afternoon. The seller who did not hedge when hedging was cheap has lost his arsenal to the same fire that made him need it.

7. The fifth is dropping fire: the news that arrives while the position is open, a headline in the middle of serious ground. Against this there is only the wing.

8. In order to carry out an attack with fire, the means must always be ready. In order to survive one, the means must be ready sooner: the stop set in the temple, the wing already bought, the fourth quarter untouched.

9. When fire breaks out inside the enemy's camp, respond at once with an attack from without. When volatility breaks out inside your position, respond at once: the hedge into reversal if the move is young, the cut of the short if it is not. Fire waits for no one, and the seller who waits to see if it spreads is the one it spreads through.

10. Unhappy is the fate of one who tries to win his battles without cultivating the spirit of enterprise; for the result is waste of time and general stagnation. Unhappier is the seller who sells every day without cultivating the discipline of the stop; for the result is a hundred wages and one fire that takes them all.

11. Move not unless you see an advantage; use not your troops unless there is something to be gained; fight not unless the position is critical. Sell not unless the count says sell. Hedge not unless hurt. Cut not unless the line is broken — and then, cut.

12. No ruler should put troops into the field merely to gratify his own spleen; no general should fight a battle simply out of pique. No seller should sell because he is angry at the market for the last trade. Anger has never been a read.

13. A kingdom that has once been destroyed can never come again into being; nor can the dead ever be brought back to life. An account that has been halved needs a double to return, and one that has lost four-fifths needs five times itself. Every large loss does not merely subtract; it resets the base that everything afterward multiplies.

14. Hence the enlightened ruler is heedful, and the good general full of caution. This is the way to keep an account at peace and a sequence intact.

## Commentary

The chapter on fire is the general's chapter on the weapon that does not care about skill, and for the seller that weapon is volatility. It arrives as a gap that puts the line behind the price before the first order can be placed; as a spike that makes the hedge unaffordable in the exact hour it becomes necessary; as a headline in the middle of a working trade. None of these can be predicted. All of them can be survived, and survival is arranged in advance: the size that makes a gap an ordinary expense, the wing that is always present because the spread always contains one, the reserve that keeps the broker's hand off the position.

The two fires the seller lights himself deserve naming. The margin call is size. The starving fire — the chop that charges a cut, then a re-entry, then a cut — is frequency, the chapter on manoeuvring returned as a slow burn. Neither is the market's fault, and both are ended by the same two rules that were already on the page: size so that the maximum loss is boring, and give the day a structural ceiling on how many times it can open.

The last verses are the arithmetic of ruin, stated by a general who had seen kingdoms end. Minus fifty needs plus one hundred. Minus eighty needs plus four hundred. This is not a warning about markets; it is what multiplication does to a sequence with a hole in it. It is also why the stop — the subject of nearly every chapter — is the only rule in the book with no exceptions.
