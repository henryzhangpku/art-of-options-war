# 10. Terrain

*Part IV: Ground and Fire*

## The Seller

1. We may distinguish six kinds of terrain: accessible ground, entangling ground, temporising ground, narrow passes, precipitous heights, and positions at a great distance from the enemy.

2. The market has six kinds of ground, and the seller must know which he is standing on before he sells a line.

3. Accessible ground is the clean trend: pivots holding, structure intact, volatility calm. On accessible ground, sell in the direction of the trend, beneath it or above it, and let the trend defend you.

4. Entangling ground can be abandoned but is hard to re-occupy. It is the range that looks like a trend for an hour. The seller who sells a directional line here is trapped when the range reasserts; the condor was the right shape from the start, and only on this ground is it sold without being hurt first.

5. Temporising ground is when neither side gains by making the first move. It is the hour before the number, the day before the decision. On temporising ground, do not sell. Whoever moves first is charged for the information the other side is about to receive for free.

6. Narrow passes are the event days: the print, the meeting, the expiry of the giant. If you can occupy them first — sell after the event, into the collapse of volatility — do so. If the enemy holds the pass — sell before the event, into the spike — do not follow him in, however rich the credit looks.

7. Precipitous heights are the gap: the market that has opened far from where it closed. Occupy the sunny side — sell the range the gap has made and the market must now travel far to unmake — and do not chase the gap itself.

8. Positions at a great distance are far expiries. The credit is larger, the wage is slower, and the enemy has more days to arrive. The seller who does not know why he is far from the enemy will find that distance is not safety; it is time, and time is the market's ally too when the line is far off.

9. These six are the principles connected with ground. The general who has attained a responsible post must be careful to study them.

10. When troops flee, are insubordinate, collapse, are disorganised, or are routed, it must be due to natural causes in the general. So with the seller: when a spread is chased, held, doubled, or abandoned in fear, the cause is not the ground. It is the Commander, who did not know which ground he was on.

11. The natural formation of the ground is the soldier's best ally; but a power of estimating the adversary, of controlling the forces of victory, and of shrewdly calculating difficulties, dangers and distances, constitutes the test of a great general.

12. If fighting is sure to result in victory, then you must fight, even though the ruler forbid it. If the count says sell and the mood says wait, sell. If the count says wait and the credit tempts you, wait. The credit is the ruler; the count is the general.

13. Know the enemy and know yourself, and your victory will not stand in doubt. Know the ground and know the sky, and you may make your victory complete.

## The Buyer

14. The buyer has his own map of the six grounds, and it is nearly the seller's map turned over.

15. On accessible ground, the clean trend, the buyer may follow. But options on a calm trend are cheap for a reason, and the buyer must be content with the trend's pace.

16. Temporising ground — the hour before the number — is where the buyer is most tempted and most often charged. The option is richest just before the event and falls the moment the event is known. He who buys into the spike pays for the information and receives the crush.

17. Narrow passes are the buyer's ground only if he arrived first: bought the move days before the event, while the rent was cheap, and sold into the spike.

18. Positions at a great distance — far expiries — are the buyer's friend. They charge less rent per day and give the move time to arrive. A near expiry on a slow thesis is a fuse cut too short.

## Commentary

Terrain is a chapter about refusal. Of the six grounds, the seller fights well on one — the clean trend — and adequately on two, the range and the post-gap market, provided he changes shape to suit them. The other three are grounds to leave alone: the hour before a scheduled number, the volatility spike into an event, and a far expiry sold without a reason. A trader who sells every day has decided that all six grounds are the same, and the market will charge him tuition on the three that are not.

The entangling ground deserves the closest reading, because it is where the condor is honestly sold at the open. Everywhere else in this book the second side is a hedge, sold only when the first side is hurt, because on trending ground a scheduled condor is a bet against the trend with twice the risk. But a market that has proven itself a range — that has already turned back from both edges, more than once, on ordinary volume — has told the seller what shape to take. The rule is not "never sell both sides at once." The rule is "know why you are."

The last verse is the summary of the book so far: yourself, the enemy, the ground and the sky. The Commander's discipline; the market's ordinary flatness and exceptional direction; the six grounds and which to refuse; and time, which decays in the seller's favour every hour he is not fighting it.

For the buyer the six grounds sort differently. The event pass the seller refuses to enter before the news is the one the buyer is most likely to enter at the worst price: implied volatility peaks just before the print and collapses just after it, so the buyer who pays at the top must be right by more than the market already expected merely to break even. The disciplined buyer arrives at the pass early, while the rent is low, or not at all.

Distance favours him where it disfavours the seller. A far expiry costs more in total but less per day, and its value falls slowly while the thesis matures. The buyer's mistake is the reverse of the seller's: not distance without a reason, but nearness without one — the cheap weekly option bought on a thesis that needs a month.
