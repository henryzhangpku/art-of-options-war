# 1. Counting in the Temple

*Part I: The Count*

## The Seller

1. The trade is a matter of vital importance to the account. It is the ground of survival or ruin. It cannot be entered on a whim.

2. Therefore, before the market opens, weigh the trade by five constants, and compare your side with the enemy's.

3. These are: the Sky, the Ground, the Commander, the Method, and the Cause.

4. The Sky is time. It is the option's expiry, the hours that remain, the decay that runs in one direction only. The Sky favours the seller, and the seller must never fight it.

5. The Ground is the terrain of prices: where the strikes stand, how wide the spread is, where the walls of dealer hedging rise and where the floors lie. The Ground decides how far the enemy must travel to reach you.

6. The Commander is the trader: his patience, his discipline, his willingness to take a small loss, his refusal to double a bet. All five constants are weighed, but the Commander is the one who fails.

7. The Method is the plan for money: how the day's risk is divided, when the hedge is sold, when the short leg is cut, when the wing is left to ride. A Method that exists only in the head does not exist.

8. The Cause is why you trade at all. If it is to be right, you will lose. If it is to be paid for standing behind a line, you may endure.

9. He who knows these five will prevail; he who does not will be surprised, and surprise is the seller's only enemy.

10. Therefore, before entering, ask: which side has the Sky? Whose Ground is easier to defend? Which Commander is calmer? Whose Method has a rule for being wrong? By these, the outcome is known.

11. All trading is based on positioning. When you are willing to sell, seem uninterested. When the market screams direction, remember that it is offering you a price for the fence, not a prophecy.

12. The seller who wins first counts in the temple. He knows his maximum loss before he knows his profit. He knows what he will do if the line is tested before it is tested. He knows the day can turn four times, and he has kept powder for each turn.

13. The seller who counts once wins once. The seller who counts every morning is still here in the afternoon, and in the year after.

14. An option is a policy. The buyer pays a premium to be covered against a move; the seller is the insurer. The count in the temple is the reading of the policy before it is signed.

15. Every policy has five terms, and the seller reads all five off the ticket. The premium is the credit, his only income. The strike is the attachment point, where the policy begins to pay; everything between the price and the strike is the deductible the buyer carries himself. The width is the limit, the most the policy can ever pay. The expiry is the term. The size is the number of policies written.

16. Four of the five terms are set by the market and the chain. The fifth, the size, is set by the seller alone, and it is the one most often written in panic or in greed.

17. The buyer of the policy is not a fool. He pays to sleep at night, to protect a book larger than yours, to hedge what he cannot sell. Do not despise the customer. Price him.

18. An insurer does not know which house will burn. He knows how often houses burn, and he asks to be paid more than that. The seller does not need to know where the market will go. He needs to know how often it reaches his strike, and to be paid more than that costs.

## The Buyer

19. The buyer counts in the same temple, by the same five constants, and finds them arranged against him.

20. The Sky is his enemy. Every hour the move does not come, he pays. He cannot fight time; he can only choose how much of it to buy, and buy no more than the catalyst needs.

21. The Ground, for the buyer, is distance: how far price must travel to reach his strike, and how far beyond it to pay him. A strike the market cannot reach before expiry is not cheap. It is a donation.

22. The Commander who buys must be patient in the opposite direction from the seller: patient through many small losses, each of which looks like proof that he is wrong.

23. The Method, for the buyer, is the debit: written, paid and final. He sizes so that losing the whole of it is a loss he would not mention at dinner.

24. The Cause, for the buyer, is a mispricing with a date on it. If he buys because he feels a move coming, he is paying rent to a feeling.

25. *Cross the sea under camouflage* (第1计 瞒天过海). The emperor feared the sea, so a general built him a house upon the water, with painted walls and a floor that did not move, and the emperor crossed believing he sat on land.

26. So the hope trade enters the account: dressed as conviction, furnished like a plan, and no one aboard asks what water runs beneath the floor. The camouflage is not aimed at the enemy. It is aimed at the passenger, and the passenger is the buyer.

27. The test of the floor is five sentences, each sayable in one breath: the edge, the catalyst, the maximum loss, the time stop, and the win rate he needs to break even. A trade that cannot say all five is a hope trade. Chapter 15 says them in full.

28. Conviction belongs to the rules and to the thesis. It has no business in the size. A size that grows with feeling is the sea showing through the paint.

29. The policy has five terms and the buyer reads them too, from the other side of the table. The premium is his whole risk. The strike is the point at which his cover begins. The width, if he buys a spread, is the most he can be paid. The expiry is the date his reason must arrive by. The size is the only term he chooses alone, and it is chosen in the temple or not at all.

## Commentary

The old text begins with counting because the temple is the only place a battle can be lost without cost. For the option seller the temple count is one sentence, said out loud before the order: *this is a five-wide spread sold for one, the maximum loss is four, and the loss at which I cut is one.* If the sentence cannot be said, the trade cannot be taken.

Of the five constants, only the Sky is reliably yours. Time decays in one direction, and a seller who does not fight it is paid every hour he is not wrong. The Ground can be chosen but not controlled. The Method can be written and then ignored. And the Commander is where every account dies: not from being wrong about direction, which is ordinary, but from refusing to be wrong at a small size.

The Cause is placed last because it is the quietest. A trader who needs to be right will hold a broken spread to prove a point. A trader who is paid to hold a line simply moves the line, or leaves the field. This book follows both men, the one who is paid to hold the line and the one who pays to cross it, and in Part V it says which of them to be, and when.

The insurer's reading of the same count is the policy read aloud: *I am paid one to cover four; the limit is the width; the term ends Friday; I can pay the full claim.* Each of the five terms is a piece of the spread. The credit is the premium. The short strike is the attachment point, and the distance from the price to it is the deductible the market must cover before the seller owes anything. The long wing is the limit. The expiry is the term. And the size is the one term no chain prints for you, which is why so many of the chapters that follow come back to it. An insurer who writes cover he cannot honour is not taking more risk than his rivals. He is in a different business, one that ends the first time the storm arrives.

The buyer reads the same five constants and finds the Sky reversed. Time decays in one direction, and he is on the wrong side of it; every quiet hour is charged to him. That does not make buying a mistake. It makes it a different trade, one whose count must name the date on which the move is due and the price at which that move is not already paid for. A buyer who cannot name a catalyst has bought time without a reason to use it.

His temple sentence is the seller's, read from the other end of the ticket: *I pay one for a five-wide spread, the maximum loss is one, the maximum gain is four, and I need to be right one time in five to break even.* The arithmetic is the same arithmetic. What changes is which mistake is fatal. The seller dies of one large loss refused. The buyer dies of a thousand small ones accepted without a reason, or of one debit sized as if it could not go to zero.

The first stratagem is placed here because the hope trade is the buyer's first enemy and the one he meets every morning. The house on the water was real enough to walk in and false in everything that mattered. The hope trade is built to the same plan: a thesis with a grain of truth, a chart that agrees with it, a size that feels like commitment, and no floor under any of it. The five sentences are the cheapest test of the floor there is. Each one closes a door the hope trade would otherwise walk through, and the fourth, the time stop, is the one most often painted shut, because a time stop costs nothing to promise and something to keep. They are named here and only named. Chapter 15, where the buyer attacks, says each of them in full.
