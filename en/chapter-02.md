# 2. The Cost of the Campaign

*Part I: The Count*

## The Seller

1. When you take the field, know what it costs to stay there.

2. The buyer of options pays rent every day for the right to be right later. The seller collects the rent. This is the seller's advantage, and it is not small; but it is a wage, not a treasure, and on some grounds the buyer rents at a discount.

3. A wage is earned by staying alive to collect it. A treasure is lost in one campaign.

4. There is no instance of an account benefiting from a prolonged war with a broken position. What was sold to expire quietly, and did not, must not be fed with hope.

5. The cost of a long siege is not the loss on the ticket. It is the capital pinned under a position that cannot win, the trades not taken while it is held, and the Commander's judgement, which rots in a siege.

6. Therefore the wise seller does not raise a second levy. He does not add to a losing spread. He does not sell a fresh line beside the one that is failing. He ends the engagement, and the cost is one collection, not four.

7. The clever general forages on the enemy. The clever seller is paid by the buyer's need for protection, which is constant, and by the buyer's love of direction, which is expensive.

8. One credit taken from the market is worth twenty predicted.

9. In war, then, let your object be a quick, quiet settlement. In a spread, let your object be to collect and leave, or to cut and leave, but in either case to leave.

10. The seller who understands the cost of the campaign never asks how much he can make. He asks how many days he can stay wrong, and prices the trade so the answer is more than one.

11. Speed has never been the enemy of the seller. Only the slow exit is.

12. One policy is a bet. Many policies, each small, are a business. The law of large numbers works only for the seller who obeys both halves of that sentence.

13. A seller who writes one large spread a month is not running a book. He is running a coin, and calling it a company. Twelve tosses a year is too few for any margin to show through the noise, and each toss is too large for one bad month to be survived.

14. Size each policy so that the worst claim it can make is a bad week, never a closed company. Size before frequency: trading more often does not make a book safer; writing each policy smaller does.

15. Do not raise the size after a quiet month; the quiet did not make the storm smaller, it only made you forget it. Do not raise it after a bad month to win it back; that is the gambler's chair, entered through the back door.

16. The army in the field needs a reserve. Keep a fixed share of the account unused by any position, every day, in every market. Set it in the quiet and keep it in the storm.

17. Measure the reserve in losses, not in dollars: how many full claims can the account pay before it stops? That number is the account's real distance from ruin.

18. Margin is the broker's minimum, not the seller's standard. The broker will let you sell until every dollar is committed. The broker is not the one who closes your company.

19. When a claim is paid, refill the reserve before writing again. The size of the reserve is the size of the seller's patience.

## The Buyer

20. The buyer pays the rent. Let him know it before he signs: the daily decay, multiplied by the days he expects to wait, is the price of his patience.

21. For the buyer, the long siege is the debit held after the catalyst has passed. The event came, the move did not, and what remains is an option paying rent on a reason that has already expired.

22. Therefore the buyer sets a time stop as the seller sets a price stop. When the date passes without the move, he leaves, and the cost is what is left of one debit, not the whole of it.

23. *Wait at leisure for the exhausted enemy* (第4计 以逸待劳). The general does not chase. He holds the pass, keeps his men in the shade, and lets the enemy's march do half the work.

24. For the buyer the march is time, and it does not tire the enemy. It tires the buyer. Every day held, the debit is eaten by an opponent who never sleeps and never asks for terms. So his patience has a deadline, written before entry: *I leave on this date if the thesis has not confirmed, regardless of price.*

25. Buy enough time, or set a tight stop. Short time and a loose stop is the one combination with no defence: the thesis is given no room to arrive, and the loss is given all the room in the world.

26. The buyer forages on the seller's comfort: the quiet market where protection is priced as if nothing will ever happen again. That is the only field where rent is cheap.

27. One move paid for in advance at a fair price is worth a hundred guessed at after the news, at the news's price.

28. The buyer keeps a reserve too, and his is the cash he did not spend on the last ten ideas. The law of large numbers works for him only if he lives long enough to reach the large number.

29. The buyer who understands the cost of the campaign does not ask how much he can make if he is right. He asks how many times he can afford to be wrong before the time he is right, and buys small enough that the answer is many.

## Commentary

The chapter on waging war is a chapter about expense, and in this trade the expense is well hidden. The credit looks like income; the risk does not appear on any statement until the morning it does. A seller who holds a failing spread believes he is being patient. In the old general's arithmetic he is feeding an army that has already lost, and the provisions come from the treasury that was supposed to fund the next hundred trades.

The number that matters is the ratio of the loss you allow to the credit you took. A five-wide spread sold for one carries a maximum loss of four. Cut at one and the campaign cost a single collection. Refuse, and it costs four: the earnings of four quiet days, spent to be stubborn on one loud one. Theta pays the seller a wage precisely because it is small and repeated; a single maximum loss is the treasure that the wage can never rebuy.

Foraging on the enemy has a plain meaning here. Someone must sell protection to a market that must hedge. Someone must sell direction to a buyer who wants it. The seller's provisions are the other side's needs, and those needs renew every session. His own provisions, his capital and his temper, do not.

The insurer adds two things the general took for granted: a large number and a reserve. The law of large numbers is the reason insurance exists at all. One house is an unpredictable thing; ten thousand houses are a statistic. The margin is invisible on any single policy and reliable across the book, but only if the book is large and the policies are small. Remove either condition and the law stops working. The arithmetic is done before the order: a five-wide spread sold for one has a limit of four per contract, so ten contracts is forty. If forty is a bad week for the account, the size is right. If forty is the account's whole quarter, the size is a bet, whatever the probability on the screen says. The probability tells you how often the claim will come. Only the size tells you what it will cost when it does.

The reserve is the bridge between the premiums, which arrive in good weeks, and the claims, which arrive in clusters. Measure it in claims rather than dollars and it cannot be talked around: if the largest position can lose forty and the reserve is two hundred, the account can pay five full claims before it is out of business. The reserve also has a reward, which Chapter 12 and the last chapter of the book come back to. After a storm, premiums are rich and many sellers are busy paying claims. Only the one who kept money back has anything to write with.

The buyer's campaign has the opposite expense. He pays a little every day to hold the right, and the amount looks negligible until it is added up. An option losing a twentieth of what is left of it each day is about half gone after two weeks of waiting, whether or not the thesis was right. The buyer's siege is therefore not a broken line but a stale reason: the option held past the date it was bought for, because selling it would make the loss real.

That is why the fourth stratagem is the buyer's chapter here. The general at leisure is not idle; he has already chosen the pass, the day and the order in which his men will stand. The buyer at leisure has chosen his date of retreat before he paid, and he chose it from the catalyst, not from his comfort: the day after the last event that could confirm the thesis, or the day the money must go elsewhere, whichever comes first. A one-year thesis in a thirty-day option is not early; it is the wrong instrument. A thirty-day thesis with a loose stop is not patient; it is rent paid on a conclusion already reached.

His foraging ground is the seller's comfort. When the market has been calm long enough, sellers price protection as if calm were permanent, and the rent falls. A buyer who pays rent only on that ground, and only for a dated reason, has turned the seller's wage into his own entry price. A buyer who pays it everywhere is the provisions the seller forages on.
