# 2. The Cost of the Campaign

*Part I: The Count*

1. When you take the field, know what it costs to stay there.

2. The buyer of options pays rent every day for the right to be right later. The seller collects the rent. This is the whole of the seller's advantage, and it is not small; but it is a wage, not a treasure.

3. A wage is earned by staying alive to collect it. A treasure is lost in one campaign.

4. There is no instance of an account benefiting from a prolonged war with a broken position. What was sold to expire quietly, and did not, must not be fed with hope.

5. The cost of a long siege is not the loss on the ticket. It is the capital pinned under a position that cannot win, the trades not taken while it is held, and the Commander's judgement, which rots in a siege.

6. Therefore the wise seller does not raise a second levy. He does not add to a losing spread. He does not sell a fresh line beside the one that is failing. He ends the engagement, and the cost is one collection, not four.

7. The clever general forages on the enemy. The clever seller is paid by the buyer's need for protection, which is constant, and by the buyer's love of direction, which is expensive.

8. One credit taken from the market is worth twenty predicted.

9. In war, then, let your object be a quick, quiet settlement. In a spread, let your object be to collect and leave, or to cut and leave, but in either case to leave.

10. The seller who understands the cost of the campaign never asks how much he can make. He asks how many days he can stay wrong, and prices the trade so the answer is more than one.

11. Speed has never been the enemy of the seller. Only the slow exit is.

## Commentary

The chapter on waging war is a chapter about expense, and in this trade the expense is well hidden. The credit looks like income; the risk does not appear on any statement until the morning it does. A seller who holds a failing spread believes he is being patient. In the old general's arithmetic he is feeding an army that has already lost, and the provisions come from the treasury that was supposed to fund the next hundred trades.

The number that matters is the ratio of the loss you allow to the credit you took. A five-wide spread sold for one carries a maximum loss of four. Cut at one and the campaign cost a single collection. Refuse, and it costs four — the earnings of four quiet days, spent to be stubborn on one loud one. Theta pays the seller a wage precisely because it is small and repeated; a single maximum loss is the treasure that the wage can never rebuy.

Foraging on the enemy has a plain meaning here. Someone must sell protection to a market that must hedge. Someone must sell direction to a buyer who wants it. The seller's provisions are the other side's needs, and those needs renew every session. His own provisions — his capital and his temper — do not.
