# 17. The Wing in the Baggage Train

*Part V: Attack and Defence*

## The Buyer

1. An army without its baggage train is lost; without provisions it is lost; without bases of supply it is lost.

2. Not every option is bought to attack. Some are bought to make sure the army is still there to attack tomorrow. These are carried in the baggage train, and they are paid for like provisions: every day, without expecting a battle.

3. The art of war teaches us to rely not on the likelihood of the enemy's not coming, but on our own readiness to receive him; not on the chance of his not attacking, but on the fact that we have made our position unassailable.

4. There are three wings in the baggage train. The put behind a book of stock. The wing behind a book of short premium. And the far tail, bought as fire insurance for the whole account.

5. Insurance loses money on average, by design. The house that does not burn has paid its premium for nothing, every year but one. Say this plainly before buying, or the first calm year will talk you out of the policy.

6. The insurance does not buy a profit. It buys the survival of the sequence. Chapter 12 counted the hole: a large loss resets the base that everything afterward multiplies. A small, regular expense that removes the large loss pays for the sequence, not for the trade.

7. Carry as much insurance as turns your worst morning into a bad one, and no more. Cover larger than the book it protects is not insurance. It is a new position, and it must be counted as an attack.

8. Insurance is cheap where the sellers have forgotten the tail: a single name after a long calm, a corner nobody hedges. It is dear where every institution buys the same put on the same index. Buy where it is cheap if the risk is there; pay the dear price only for the risk you cannot carry.

9. Buy it in the calm. Insurance bought after the storm is expensive, and it is bought at the moment it feels most necessary, which is the moment it is priced to be least worth having.

10. Judge the insurance on the sequence, not on its own ledger line. Its cost appears every month. Its value appears once in ten years.

11. *The golden cicada sheds its shell* (第21计 金蝉脱壳). The cicada does not die when it leaves the shell. The husk stays on the branch, whole and empty, and the bird strikes it.

12. A position carried into expiry is that husk. Leave before the last days, not on them. Know what settles and how: broad index options settle in cash, some at the morning print and some at the close; options on a fund or a stock are claims on shares, and shares can be assigned.

13. A wing that expires is a wing that has stopped guarding. Roll the insurance on a schedule written in the calm, not on the morning the house is already warm.

## The Seller

14. Every serious insurer buys insurance for itself. It is called reinsurance, and it is the reason the company is still writing after the storm.

15. In a credit spread the long wing is reinsurance bought at the moment the policy is written: part of the premium is handed on, and the largest claim is capped.

16. A wing far away is cheap reinsurance with a high attachment point. A wing near the short strike is expensive reinsurance that caps the claim sooner. Choose the cover that keeps the worst claim inside the reserve, not the cover that looks cheapest.

17. When a short option has fallen to a few cents, buy it back. The few cents are the cheapest reinsurance in the market: they close a policy that can still be claimed for its whole limit. Do not hold a nearly expired policy to collect the last penny; the last penny is paid for by the whole limit.

18. A small, permanent tail cover for the whole book, a little protection far below the market, is the insurer's catastrophe treaty. It loses money most years. It is meant to.

19. The seller who refuses to pay for his own cover keeps a little more premium every year, and gives all of it back in one.

20. Cede enough to sleep, and keep enough to earn.

21. Leave a wing in every spread. That was the seller's rule in Chapter 7. It is also a buyer's rule, and now the seller knows which one he was following.

## Commentary

Part V has so far been about buying to attack. This chapter is about buying in defence, which is the oldest and most respectable reason anyone ever bought an option. A farmer bought the right to sell his harvest at a fixed price long before anyone bought a call to bet on a stock. The baggage train is the image the general gives for it: the provisions that do not fight, that slow the march, that cost every day, and without which the army is lost.

The arithmetic is the reason to carry them, and it can be done on paper. Take a book of stock worth 100. Suppose a put struck at 90, three months out, costs 1. Carried for a year, rolled each quarter, the insurance costs about 4, and in a calm year that 4 is simply gone. Now suppose the year is not calm, and the book falls to 60. Without the put, the loss is 40, and Chapter 12's arithmetic says the book needs to rise by two-thirds to return. With the put, the loss is the 10 down to the strike plus what the year's puts have cost, somewhere between 11 and 14, and the book needs to rise by about an eighth to a sixth. The insurance did not make money in that year either. It turned a hole that takes years to fill into one that takes months, and that is the whole of what it is for. The numbers are an example, not a price list. Real puts cost what the market says on the day, and the market charges most for the insurance everyone wants.

That is why where the insurance is bought matters as much as whether. Puts on the broad index are usually the dearest options on the board: every pension fund, every manager with a mandate, every dealer short the same risk is buying the same protection, and the sellers charge accordingly. The skew, the extra price charged for the downside strikes, is the sign of that crowd. Insurance is cheap in the places the crowd is not: in a single name where nobody hedges, after a long calm in which the sellers have stopped charging for the tail. Mark Spitznagel's *Safe Haven* (2021) makes a related argument at the scale of a whole portfolio: that insurance should be judged by what it does to the compound rate of the whole, not by its own line in the ledger. That is the same lesson as verse 10, told across decades rather than seasons.

How much to carry has a plain answer, and it is the same answer for buyer and seller. Carry enough to turn the worst morning into a bad one, and no more. Insurance larger than the risk it covers has stopped being insurance. It is a bet on the catastrophe, and it must be counted as one, with its own five sentences and its own size. The buyer who loads his baggage train with more puts than he has stock has not become safer. He has become an attacker on the long night, and Chapter 15 has his rules.

The insurer's chapter on reinsurance is the seller's half of the same lesson. A primary insurer writes thousands of policies, then cedes a slice of the premium to a reinsurer who pays the claims above some level. The primary keeps less in a good year and is still open in a bad one. No large insurer operates without it, and none regards its cost as wasted. The option seller already buys reinsurance on every policy: the wing. The choice of where to place it is the reinsurance buyer's choice: far and cheap, with a large claim still possible but capped; near and dear, with the claim capped early. Neither is correct in general. The right wing is the one that keeps the worst claim inside the reserve of Chapter 2.

The buy-back at a few cents is the reinsurance most sellers skip. A short option that has decayed to almost nothing still carries the full liability of the policy until it expires. Buying it back for those cents closes the liability; holding it to earn them keeps the whole limit at risk for almost no reward. Insurers cancel policies whose remaining premium no longer pays for their remaining risk, and the habit costs pennies to copy.

The twenty-first stratagem belongs here because insurance has an expiry too. A wing left to expire stops guarding the hour it expires, and a protective put carried into its last week is a husk: the shape is still on the screen, the protection is mostly gone, and an illiquid strike may be pinned against the price with nowhere to go. The settlement terms matter more for insurance than for any other position, because insurance is held for the bad day, and the bad day is when an assignment, a cash settlement at an unexpected print, or a short stock position nobody planned does the most damage. So the roll of the insurance is written in the calm, on a schedule, like a premium payment, and not decided on the morning the house is already warm.

The seller should finish this chapter with one recognition. Every defined-risk seller in this book has owned a long option on every trade: the wing. He bought it with part of his credit, he kept it after the cut, and on the day the line broke it was the only thing on the board that paid. He has been a buyer since Chapter 4. Seeing himself as a buyer of the far wing is what makes this one book and not two.
