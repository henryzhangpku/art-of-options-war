# The Art of Options War
*Sun Tzu for Both Sides of the Options Ticket · Second Edition*
*Henry Zhang*

## Before the Temple Count

Two and a half thousand years ago a general wrote thirteen short chapters on how to win without being destroyed. He was not interested in glory. He was interested in the arithmetic of survival: which battles to fight, which to refuse, how to be paid by an enemy's mistakes instead of your own courage, and why the state that wins every battle can still lose the war.

Options are the same problem in a different uniform, and the uniform has two sides. The seller is paid to hold a line. He does not need to know where the enemy will go, only where the enemy will not arrive in time. He wins most engagements and can be ruined by one. The buyer pays for the right to be right. He loses most engagements, each one small and paid for in advance, and lives on the rare one that pays many times over. Every chapter that follows borrows the old general's structure, thirteen chapters in his order and on his subjects, and reads it from both ends of a defined-risk ticket: first the seller of the credit spread, then the buyer of the option or the debit spread, each of whom intends to still be trading next year.

The seller has an older trade than war to learn from. In 1688 Edward Lloyd kept a coffee house near the Thames, and the merchants who drank there did a strange thing. When a ship was about to sail, its owner wrote the voyage on a slip of paper, and men with money and nerve wrote their names underneath. Each took a share of the loss if the ship went down, and each was paid a premium for it. They were called underwriters because they wrote under. None of them knew which ship would sink. Every one of them knew, roughly, how many. That is the difference between a gambler and an insurer, and it runs through the seller's half of this book: he prices a fleet, writes many small shares, refuses the voyages he cannot price, keeps money aside for the ones that sink, and is paid for the arithmetic, not for being right.

The buyer has an older book than this one too. The Thirty-Six Stratagems, 三十六计, are tricks played on an enemy: show weakness where you are strong, offer a bait worth taking, let the enemy's own certainty do the work. A stratagem succeeds less because the enemy is foolish than because he sees what he expects to see. In trading, the enemy most often tricked is yourself. So the buyer's sections quote the stratagems where they fit, each cited by its number, and the whole thirty-six stand at the back of the book. Beside them runs a second voice: Jamie Mai, a buyer of cheap convexity, as told in Jack Schwager's *Hedge Fund Market Wizards* and Michael Lewis's *The Big Short*, together with the record of a test that tried to copy his method by machine and failed.

This second edition adds a fifth part, Attack and Defence, in five chapters. The general's thirteen teach each side to survive its own arithmetic. They do not answer the general's own question: when to defend, and when to attack. Part V does, and in it the buyer speaks first.

This book is education, not advice. It contains no recommendations for you personally, no signals and no promises. Options can lose money faster than stock. A defined-risk spread cannot lose more than its width, and a long option cannot lose more than its debit, but either can still be more than you can afford, and a run of them can still end an account. Insurers go bankrupt too; the ones that last are the ones that never forgot it. Nothing here is a performance claim. Where the text names a number, the number is arithmetic you can check on paper.

Read one chapter a day. Then read them again, in the order the general chose, because the order is the argument: count before you fight, know what a campaign costs, win without needing to be right, make yourself unbeatable before you look for a chance, value information above every other weapon, and only then choose whether today is a day to defend or to attack.

## Part I: The Count

The general began in the temple, before a single soldier moved. Whoever counts more carefully wins; whoever counts less, loses; whoever does not count at all does not deserve to be surprised.

The first three chapters are the count. What the campaign will be judged on, what it costs to keep an army in the field, and the seller's highest skill: to win without fighting, to be paid without having to be right. The buyer's highest skill is the other half of the general's art, to attack only where the enemy is unguarded, and it is the subject of Part V.

### 1. Counting in the Temple

## The Seller

1. The trade is a matter of vital importance to the account. It is the ground of survival or ruin. It cannot be entered on a whim.

2. Therefore, before the market opens, weigh the trade by five constants, and compare your side with the enemy's.

3. These are: the Sky, the Ground, the Commander, the Method, and the Cause.

4. The Sky is time. It is the option's expiry, the hours that remain, the decay that runs in one direction only. The Sky favours the seller, and the seller must never fight it.

5. The Ground is the terrain of prices: where the strikes stand, how wide the spread is, where the walls of dealer hedging rise and where the floors lie. The Ground decides how far the enemy must travel to reach you.

6. The Commander is the trader: his patience, his discipline, his willingness to take a small loss, his refusal to double a bet. All five constants are weighed, but the Commander is the one who fails.

7. The Method is the plan for money: how the day's risk is divided, when the hedge is sold, when the short leg is cut, when the wing is left to ride. A Method that exists only in the head does not exist.

8. The Cause is why you trade at all. If it is to be right, you will lose. If it is to be paid for standing behind a line, you may endure.

9. He who knows these five will prevail; he who does not will be surprised, and surprise is the seller's only enemy.

10. Therefore, before entering, ask: which side has the Sky? Whose Ground is easier to defend? Which Commander is calmer? Whose Method has a rule for being wrong? By these, the outcome is known.

11. All trading is based on positioning. When you are willing to sell, seem uninterested. When the market screams direction, remember that it is offering you a price for the fence, not a prophecy.

12. The seller who wins first counts in the temple. He knows his maximum loss before he knows his profit. He knows what he will do if the line is tested before it is tested. He knows the day can turn four times, and he has kept powder for each turn.

13. The seller who counts once wins once. The seller who counts every morning is still here in the afternoon, and in the year after.

14. An option is a policy. The buyer pays a premium to be covered against a move; the seller is the insurer. The count in the temple is the reading of the policy before it is signed.

15. Every policy has five terms, and the seller reads all five off the ticket. The premium is the credit, his only income. The strike is the attachment point, where the policy begins to pay; everything between the price and the strike is the deductible the buyer carries himself. The width is the limit, the most the policy can ever pay. The expiry is the term. The size is the number of policies written.

16. Four of the five terms are set by the market and the chain. The fifth, the size, is set by the seller alone, and it is the one most often written in panic or in greed.

17. The buyer of the policy is not a fool. He pays to sleep at night, to protect a book larger than yours, to hedge what he cannot sell. Do not despise the customer. Price him.

18. An insurer does not know which house will burn. He knows how often houses burn, and he asks to be paid more than that. The seller does not need to know where the market will go. He needs to know how often it reaches his strike, and to be paid more than that costs.

## The Buyer

19. The buyer counts in the same temple, by the same five constants, and finds them arranged against him.

20. The Sky is his enemy. Every hour the move does not come, he pays. He cannot fight time; he can only choose how much of it to buy, and buy no more than the catalyst needs.

21. The Ground, for the buyer, is distance: how far price must travel to reach his strike, and how far beyond it to pay him. A strike the market cannot reach before expiry is not cheap. It is a donation.

22. The Commander who buys must be patient in the opposite direction from the seller: patient through many small losses, each of which looks like proof that he is wrong.

23. The Method, for the buyer, is the debit: written, paid and final. He sizes so that losing the whole of it is a loss he would not mention at dinner.

24. The Cause, for the buyer, is a mispricing with a date on it. If he buys because he feels a move coming, he is paying rent to a feeling.

25. *Cross the sea under camouflage* (第1计 瞒天过海). The emperor feared the sea, so a general built him a house upon the water, with painted walls and a floor that did not move, and the emperor crossed believing he sat on land.

26. So the hope trade enters the account: dressed as conviction, furnished like a plan, and no one aboard asks what water runs beneath the floor. The camouflage is not aimed at the enemy. It is aimed at the passenger, and the passenger is the buyer.

27. The test of the floor is five sentences, each sayable in one breath: the edge, the catalyst, the maximum loss, the time stop, and the win rate he needs to break even. A trade that cannot say all five is a hope trade. Chapter 15 says them in full.

28. Conviction belongs to the rules and to the thesis. It has no business in the size. A size that grows with feeling is the sea showing through the paint.

29. The policy has five terms and the buyer reads them too, from the other side of the table. The premium is his whole risk. The strike is the point at which his cover begins. The width, if he buys a spread, is the most he can be paid. The expiry is the date his reason must arrive by. The size is the only term he chooses alone, and it is chosen in the temple or not at all.

## Commentary

The old text begins with counting because the temple is the only place a battle can be lost without cost. For the option seller the temple count is one sentence, said out loud before the order: *this is a five-wide spread sold for one, the maximum loss is four, and the loss at which I cut is one.* If the sentence cannot be said, the trade cannot be taken.

Of the five constants, only the Sky is reliably yours. Time decays in one direction, and a seller who does not fight it is paid every hour he is not wrong. The Ground can be chosen but not controlled. The Method can be written and then ignored. And the Commander is where every account dies: not from being wrong about direction, which is ordinary, but from refusing to be wrong at a small size.

The Cause is placed last because it is the quietest. A trader who needs to be right will hold a broken spread to prove a point. A trader who is paid to hold a line simply moves the line, or leaves the field. This book follows both men, the one who is paid to hold the line and the one who pays to cross it, and in Part V it says which of them to be, and when.

The insurer's reading of the same count is the policy read aloud: *I am paid one to cover four; the limit is the width; the term ends Friday; I can pay the full claim.* Each of the five terms is a piece of the spread. The credit is the premium. The short strike is the attachment point, and the distance from the price to it is the deductible the market must cover before the seller owes anything. The long wing is the limit. The expiry is the term. And the size is the one term no chain prints for you, which is why so many of the chapters that follow come back to it. An insurer who writes cover he cannot honour is not taking more risk than his rivals. He is in a different business, one that ends the first time the storm arrives.

The buyer reads the same five constants and finds the Sky reversed. Time decays in one direction, and he is on the wrong side of it; every quiet hour is charged to him. That does not make buying a mistake. It makes it a different trade, one whose count must name the date on which the move is due and the price at which that move is not already paid for. A buyer who cannot name a catalyst has bought time without a reason to use it.

His temple sentence is the seller's, read from the other end of the ticket: *I pay one for a five-wide spread, the maximum loss is one, the maximum gain is four, and I need to be right one time in five to break even.* The arithmetic is the same arithmetic. What changes is which mistake is fatal. The seller dies of one large loss refused. The buyer dies of a thousand small ones accepted without a reason, or of one debit sized as if it could not go to zero.

The first stratagem is placed here because the hope trade is the buyer's first enemy and the one he meets every morning. The house on the water was real enough to walk in and false in everything that mattered. The hope trade is built to the same plan: a thesis with a grain of truth, a chart that agrees with it, a size that feels like commitment, and no floor under any of it. The five sentences are the cheapest test of the floor there is. Each one closes a door the hope trade would otherwise walk through, and the fourth, the time stop, is the one most often painted shut, because a time stop costs nothing to promise and something to keep. They are named here and only named. Chapter 15, where the buyer attacks, says each of them in full.

### 2. The Cost of the Campaign

## The Seller

1. When you take the field, know what it costs to stay there.

2. The buyer of options pays rent every day for the right to be right later. The seller collects the rent. This is the seller's advantage, and it is not small; but it is a wage, not a treasure, and on some grounds the buyer rents at a discount.

3. A wage is earned by staying alive to collect it. A treasure is lost in one campaign.

4. There is no instance of an account benefiting from a prolonged war with a broken position. What was sold to expire quietly, and did not, must not be fed with hope.

5. The cost of a long siege is not the loss on the ticket. It is the capital pinned under a position that cannot win, the trades not taken while it is held, and the Commander's judgement, which rots in a siege.

6. Therefore the wise seller does not raise a second levy. He does not add to a losing spread. He does not sell a fresh line beside the one that is failing. He ends the engagement, and the cost is one collection, not four.

7. The clever general forages on the enemy. The clever seller is paid by the buyer's need for protection, which is constant, and by the buyer's love of direction, which is expensive.

8. One credit taken from the market is worth twenty predicted.

9. In war, then, let your object be a quick, quiet settlement. In a spread, let your object be to collect and leave, or to cut and leave, but in either case to leave.

10. The seller who understands the cost of the campaign never asks how much he can make. He asks how many days he can stay wrong, and prices the trade so the answer is more than one.

11. Speed has never been the enemy of the seller. Only the slow exit is.

12. One policy is a bet. Many policies, each small, are a business. The law of large numbers works only for the seller who obeys both halves of that sentence.

13. A seller who writes one large spread a month is not running a book. He is running a coin, and calling it a company. Twelve tosses a year is too few for any margin to show through the noise, and each toss is too large for one bad month to be survived.

14. Size each policy so that the worst claim it can make is a bad week, never a closed company. Size before frequency: trading more often does not make a book safer; writing each policy smaller does.

15. Do not raise the size after a quiet month; the quiet did not make the storm smaller, it only made you forget it. Do not raise it after a bad month to win it back; that is the gambler's chair, entered through the back door.

16. The army in the field needs a reserve. Keep a fixed share of the account unused by any position, every day, in every market. Set it in the quiet and keep it in the storm.

17. Measure the reserve in losses, not in dollars: how many full claims can the account pay before it stops? That number is the account's real distance from ruin.

18. Margin is the broker's minimum, not the seller's standard. The broker will let you sell until every dollar is committed. The broker is not the one who closes your company.

19. When a claim is paid, refill the reserve before writing again. The size of the reserve is the size of the seller's patience.

## The Buyer

20. The buyer pays the rent. Let him know it before he signs: the daily decay, multiplied by the days he expects to wait, is the price of his patience.

21. For the buyer, the long siege is the debit held after the catalyst has passed. The event came, the move did not, and what remains is an option paying rent on a reason that has already expired.

22. Therefore the buyer sets a time stop as the seller sets a price stop. When the date passes without the move, he leaves, and the cost is what is left of one debit, not the whole of it.

23. *Wait at leisure for the exhausted enemy* (第4计 以逸待劳). The general does not chase. He holds the pass, keeps his men in the shade, and lets the enemy's march do half the work.

24. For the buyer the march is time, and it does not tire the enemy. It tires the buyer. Every day held, the debit is eaten by an opponent who never sleeps and never asks for terms. So his patience has a deadline, written before entry: *I leave on this date if the thesis has not confirmed, regardless of price.*

25. Buy enough time, or set a tight stop. Short time and a loose stop is the one combination with no defence: the thesis is given no room to arrive, and the loss is given all the room in the world.

26. The buyer forages on the seller's comfort: the quiet market where protection is priced as if nothing will ever happen again. That is the only field where rent is cheap.

27. One move paid for in advance at a fair price is worth a hundred guessed at after the news, at the news's price.

28. The buyer keeps a reserve too, and his is the cash he did not spend on the last ten ideas. The law of large numbers works for him only if he lives long enough to reach the large number.

29. The buyer who understands the cost of the campaign does not ask how much he can make if he is right. He asks how many times he can afford to be wrong before the time he is right, and buys small enough that the answer is many.

## Commentary

The chapter on waging war is a chapter about expense, and in this trade the expense is well hidden. The credit looks like income; the risk does not appear on any statement until the morning it does. A seller who holds a failing spread believes he is being patient. In the old general's arithmetic he is feeding an army that has already lost, and the provisions come from the treasury that was supposed to fund the next hundred trades.

The number that matters is the ratio of the loss you allow to the credit you took. A five-wide spread sold for one carries a maximum loss of four. Cut at one and the campaign cost a single collection. Refuse, and it costs four: the earnings of four quiet days, spent to be stubborn on one loud one. Theta pays the seller a wage precisely because it is small and repeated; a single maximum loss is the treasure that the wage can never rebuy.

Foraging on the enemy has a plain meaning here. Someone must sell protection to a market that must hedge. Someone must sell direction to a buyer who wants it. The seller's provisions are the other side's needs, and those needs renew every session. His own provisions, his capital and his temper, do not.

The insurer adds two things the general took for granted: a large number and a reserve. The law of large numbers is the reason insurance exists at all. One house is an unpredictable thing; ten thousand houses are a statistic. The margin is invisible on any single policy and reliable across the book, but only if the book is large and the policies are small. Remove either condition and the law stops working. The arithmetic is done before the order: a five-wide spread sold for one has a limit of four per contract, so ten contracts is forty. If forty is a bad week for the account, the size is right. If forty is the account's whole quarter, the size is a bet, whatever the probability on the screen says. The probability tells you how often the claim will come. Only the size tells you what it will cost when it does.

The reserve is the bridge between the premiums, which arrive in good weeks, and the claims, which arrive in clusters. Measure it in claims rather than dollars and it cannot be talked around: if the largest position can lose forty and the reserve is two hundred, the account can pay five full claims before it is out of business. The reserve also has a reward, which Chapter 12 and the last chapter of the book come back to. After a storm, premiums are rich and many sellers are busy paying claims. Only the one who kept money back has anything to write with.

The buyer's campaign has the opposite expense. He pays a little every day to hold the right, and the amount looks negligible until it is added up. An option losing a twentieth of what is left of it each day is about half gone after two weeks of waiting, whether or not the thesis was right. The buyer's siege is therefore not a broken line but a stale reason: the option held past the date it was bought for, because selling it would make the loss real.

That is why the fourth stratagem is the buyer's chapter here. The general at leisure is not idle; he has already chosen the pass, the day and the order in which his men will stand. The buyer at leisure has chosen his date of retreat before he paid, and he chose it from the catalyst, not from his comfort: the day after the last event that could confirm the thesis, or the day the money must go elsewhere, whichever comes first. A one-year thesis in a thirty-day option is not early; it is the wrong instrument. A thirty-day thesis with a loose stop is not patient; it is rent paid on a conclusion already reached.

His foraging ground is the seller's comfort. When the market has been calm long enough, sellers price protection as if calm were permanent, and the rent falls. A buyer who pays rent only on that ground, and only for a dated reason, has turned the seller's wage into his own entry price. A buyer who pays it everywhere is the provisions the seller forages on.

### 3. Winning Without Fighting

## The Seller

1. In the practical art of trading, the best thing is to take the credit whole and unharmed; to fight for it against a moving market is second best.

2. To win a hundred directional battles is not the height of skill. To be paid without needing direction at all is the height of the seller's skill.

3. Thus the highest form of selling is to be right about where the market will *not* go. The next is to hedge when it goes. The next is to cut. The lowest is to hold to expiry and hope.

4. Ask a buyer what the market will do and he answers up, or down. He has named one door of four.

5. The market may rise far. It may rise and stall. It may fall. It may go nowhere at all. The buyer needs one door. The seller of the fence is paid by the other three.

6. Three doors of four is not three wins in four. The doors are not equal in width, and the one that hurts is wider than any that pays. The market prices them honestly. Take the three doors for what they are: a better shape to be wrong in.

7. The skilful seller subdues the enemy's direction without fighting it. He sets his line where the enemy must travel far and fast to reach it, and he is paid for every day the enemy does not.

8. He does not attack the trend. He sells beneath a rising market and above a falling one, and lets the trend defend his line for him.

9. The rule is: when the tape is calm, collect. When it moves, hedge into the move. When it runs through your line, cut the short and keep the wing. Do not besiege a moving market; a siege costs a season.

10. The highest form of winning without fighting is the fight declined. Underwriting is the art of saying no. A seller who writes every policy offered to him is not an insurer. He is a target.

11. Look at the risk, not the premium. The premium will always look attractive; that is how the bad risks are sold.

12. Some hazards are printed on the calendar: the rate decision, the inflation report, the earnings call, the election night. Writing cover across them at the ordinary rate is writing fire insurance on a house whose owner has already bought matches. A known hazard is excluded, priced higher, or written smaller.

13. Ask who is buying, and why now. When the market is suddenly eager for the exact cover you are selling, at the exact strike, the eagerness is information. The people most keen to buy fire cover are the ones who smell smoke.

14. Some ground is not insurable at any price: a market falling in a straight line, a fear gauge rising day after day, a gap that has not been filled. There the underwriter closes the book and waits.

15. Declining a policy costs nothing but the premium not collected. Writing a bad one can cost the company. Keep a book of the policies you declined, and read it after the storm.

16. Know the enemy and know yourself, and in a hundred trades you will not be in danger. Know yourself but not the enemy, and for every credit you take you will give one back. Know neither, and you will be in danger in every trade.

17. To know the enemy is to know that flat is the market's ordinary state, and that direction is the exception it is famous for.

18. To know yourself is to know the exact loss at which you will leave, and to have left at that loss before.

## The Buyer

19. The buyer cannot win without fighting. He must be right about direction, distance and date. Let him therefore fight only where two of the three are already decided.

20. Direction he borrows from the trend, as the seller does. Date he takes from the calendar, never from a feeling. Only distance is left to his judgement, and it is the one judgement he is paying for.

21. *Besiege Wei to rescue Zhao* (第2计 围魏救赵). When Wei besieged Handan, the relief did not ride to Handan. It rode to Daliang, the enemy's own capital, and the siege broke behind it.

22. The price is Handan: visible, loud, and not where the trade is won. The mispricing is Daliang. Do not attack the price. Attack the reason it is wrong, and say it as one sentence with three parts: *I expect this mispricing, because of this measurable reason, and after costs this structure wins if this condition holds.*

23. *Kill with a borrowed knife* (第3计 借刀杀人). The buyer's knife is borrowed, not forged: an event on a day, a mechanism by which it reprices the stock, and a chain that has not yet charged for either. A catalyst without a date is a rumour. A date without a mechanism is a coin.

24. The sharpest knife is a binary one: a ruling or a regulator's decision, where the stock has two futures and the chain prices one smooth curve. There the buyer need not know which future arrives. He needs the two futures to be priced as one.

25. The buyer names one door of four, and he knows it. His art is to choose the door the market has priced as narrow and the event will make wide.

26. One door in four is not one win in four. The buyer's door, when it opens, is wider than the three that close; that is what he pays for. The market prices this honestly too. His edge, where it exists, is a door priced narrower than it is.

27. The highest form of buying is to pay for a move the seller has forgotten to charge for. The next is to cheapen the debit by selling the far wing. The lowest is to buy the move the whole market is already shouting about.

28. The buyer declines too. A day without a trade that passes all five sentences is not a lost day. It is the buyer's version of the seller's declined book.

29. To know himself, the buyer must know how many debits in a row he can lose without changing his size or his mind. If he has never counted it, the tape will count it for him.

## Commentary

The general's most famous line, that supreme excellence is to win without fighting, is the seller's highest skill. The buyer of an option must be right three times: direction, distance and date. The seller of a spread must be right once: that price stays on his side of a line he chose. He has given away the arrow and kept the fence, and the fence is priced. He is not smarter than the buyer. He has declined the fight the buyer has chosen. The buyer's highest skill is the other half of the general's art, to attack only where the enemy is unguarded, and it is the subject of Part V.

The four doors are the honest version of this. Only a real move, far enough, in time, hurts the seller; stall, reverse and flat all pay him. But the honest half must be said in the same breath: the losing door is also the widest loss. Nothing about the shape of the bet promises a win rate, and a seller who hears "three in four" as "seventy-five percent" has already lost the count. The shape buys tractability, not edge.

The purest form of not fighting is not selling at all. Insurance law has a phrase for the duty the two sides of a policy owe each other, *uberrimae fidei*, utmost good faith, because the business only works if the risk written is the risk described. In the market no counterparty will describe the risk to you honestly. The calendar does it instead. A rate decision or an earnings report inside the term of a policy is a known hazard, and the seller who writes across it at the ordinary premium is not taking a calculated risk. He has not read the form.

Adverse selection is the subtler danger. On an ordinary day the people buying index protection are hedgers paying for sleep, and their buying is why premiums usually run rich. When demand for cover suddenly concentrates, everyone wanting the same puts at the same strikes at once, the buyers are no longer paying for sleep. They know, or fear, something specific. The book of declined policies is the cheapest education in the business. Most of the time it shows a little premium given up. Once in a while it shows the policy that would have been the year's worst claim, and that entry is what teaches a seller to keep saying no.

Knowing yourself, in this chapter, is not a mood. It is a number. The seller who cannot say the price at which he will leave does not know himself, however calm he feels at the open, because the tape will ask him the question when he is least calm.

Read from the other side, the same line is the buyer's warning. He must be right three times, so he cannot afford to be careless about any of the three. The disciplined buyer does not make three forecasts; he takes two of them from somewhere other than himself. Direction comes from the trend's structure, date from a scheduled event, and only distance remains a judgement.

The two stratagems are the buyer's answer to "win without fighting." He cannot avoid the fight, but he can choose where it is fought, and the relief of Handan is the lesson: ride to the place the price must return to, not to the place it is shouting from. Each clause of the three-part sentence can fail, and that is its use. Most ideas fail at the first clause, because "it goes up" names a direction and calls it a mispricing.

The binary knife is where the book's second voice first speaks. Jamie Mai, as he described his practice to Schwager in *Hedge Fund Market Wizards* and as Lewis tells it in *The Big Short*, attacked the shape of a price rather than the direction of a stock. Where a company's future hung on one decision, a court or a regulator or a question of solvency, the option chain still priced a smooth spread of outcomes, as if the stock could land anywhere along a gentle curve. In fact it had two futures. The cheap end of that curve could be bought without knowing which future would arrive, because the curve itself was wrong. That is the siege of Daliang: the pricing is the wall, and the stock's direction rescues itself. It is also the buyer's whole case in one picture. He does not win by forecasting better than the seller. He wins, when he wins, because the seller has priced a two-humped world as one hump.

The four doors work against the buyer and for him in the same breath. He is paid by one door only, but it is the widest in the house, and the market charges him for the width in advance. Nothing about the shape of a long option is an edge, any more than the shape of a spread is. The buyer's edge, when there is one, is that the door has been priced too narrow, by sellers who have watched it stay shut for so long that they have stopped charging for the day it opens.

## Part II: Shape and Force

Chapters four to six are about shape. The old text says that the skilful fighter first makes himself invincible and then waits for the enemy to become vulnerable. Invincibility is a matter of defence and lies with you; vulnerability is a matter of the enemy and does not.

For the premium seller, the defined-risk spread is the invincible position; the hedge into reversal is the response to the enemy's move; compounding is the force that a well-shaped position releases over time.

For the buyer, the debit is the invincible position, because it is the whole risk paid in advance. The catalyst is the opening the enemy gives, and the cheap price of fire is the vulnerability he waits for. Compounding is the arithmetic that punishes him for the gaps between rare wins.

### 4. The Unbeatable Position

## The Seller

1. The good fighters of old first put themselves beyond the possibility of defeat, and then waited for an opportunity to defeat the enemy.

2. To be beyond defeat lies in your own hands. To defeat the enemy lies in the enemy's. The seller can make himself unbeatable; he cannot make the market give him a move.

3. Being unbeatable is a matter of defence. Defined risk is the only kind of position in this book, because it is the only kind whose worst day is printed on the ticket before the day begins: the seller's spread, whose width is its ceiling, and the buyer's debit, which is its own.

4. Sell a five-wide spread for one, and the maximum loss is four. Written. Known. Chosen. The market cannot enlarge it. Only the seller can, by selling more.

5. He who sells naked has no shape. He has a wage on ordinary days and a debt without a ceiling on the day that is not ordinary. A position without a ceiling is not a position. It is a wager on the weather.

6. Every policy carries a limit, written on its face before it is sold. In a credit spread the limit is the long wing, and the width less the credit is the most the policy can ever cost.

7. The extra premium earned by dropping the wing is the price of unlimited liability. No premium is large enough to pay that price. Never remove the wing to save its cost; it is the cheapest part of the policy, and the only part that is never wrong.

8. The deductible is the distance from the price to the short strike. A small deductible pays well and is claimed often; a large one pays little and is claimed rarely. Neither is better. The mistake is to forget which one you wrote.

9. The limit protects the account from the world. It does not protect the account from its own size. A limited policy, written too many times, is an unlimited book. The book's limit is the width times the size, and only that number counts.

10. Read the limit aloud before every sale: *the worst this can cost is the width less the credit, times the size.* A seller can be wrong about the weather. He must never be wrong about the limit.

11. The seller who is beyond defeat does not need to know the future. He needs only to have set his line where the future must work hard to arrive, and to have made his loss survivable if it does.

12. Standing on the defensive indicates insufficient strength; attacking, a superabundance of it. The seller has never had a superabundance. He has a small, repeatable wage. On the days his wage is fair, let him stand on the defensive and be content.

13. What the ancients called a clever fighter is one who not only wins, but wins with ease. The seller wins with ease when his line is far and his size is small; every trade that felt like a fight was a trade sized for a different day.

14. The consummate seller cultivates the Method and adheres to discipline. Thus it is in his power to control success.

15. The Method is: max loss first; size so that a maximum loss is an ordinary day, not a ruinous one; cut at the credit; never stack a second position on the same direction.

16. A victorious seller enters the trade having already survived it. A defeated seller enters, and then looks for a way to survive.

## The Buyer

17. The general did not stop at defence. *Security against defeat implies defensive tactics; ability to defeat the enemy means taking the offensive. Standing on the defensive indicates insufficient strength; attacking, a superabundance of strength. The general who is skilled in defence hides in the most secret recesses of the earth; he who is skilled in attack flashes forth from the topmost heights of heaven.*

18. Defence and attack are one sentence in the old text, not two doctrines. The seller has read its first half. The buyer holds the second, and Part V reads the whole of it.

19. The buyer's position is unbeatable from the moment he pays. The debit is his whole risk, printed on the ticket and paid before the day begins. No gap can enlarge it.

20. But unbeatable is not unharmed. A debit can go to zero every time, and a buyer who sizes each one as if it could not is beaten by arithmetic alone.

21. *Remove the firewood from under the pot* (第19计 釜底抽薪). To still a boiling pot, no one ladles the water. A man draws the firewood from beneath it, and the boil dies of itself.

22. A loss is the boiling; the size that feeds it is the firewood. To argue with the price after entry is to ladle, and the pot boils faster for it.

23. Before the first order, write the worst case in one sentence: *my worst case is three hundred dollars, because the debit is three.* Divide the risk budget by that number, and round down. If one contract is over the budget, take a narrower spread, a smaller underlying, or no trade. Never round up to one.

24. Size the debit so that losing all of it is ordinary. Then the buyer may lose many times in a row, which he will, and still be in the field on the day the door opens.

25. Risk one to make ten is a constraint before it is a hope. The one must be small enough to lose ten times in a row and still sit down to count the eleventh.

26. The buyer who is beyond defeat does not need to be right often. He needs to be small when wrong and present when right.

27. Attacking indicates a superabundance. The buyer is the attacker in this trade, and his superabundance is not capital. It is the number of attempts his size allows, and, when he is lucky, a price that has been sold to him too cheaply.

28. A victorious buyer enters having already accepted the loss of the whole debit. A defeated buyer enters, and then begins to hope.

## Commentary

This chapter is the reason the whole book is about defined risk and nothing else. The general's "invincible" has an exact translation: a position whose maximum loss is a number, not a hope. The seller of a vertical spread has read his worst day off the ticket. He may dislike that day, but he cannot be surprised by it, and surprise is the only thing in this trade that ends accounts.

The insurer calls the same thing a limit, and no sane insurer writes a policy without one. Selling an option naked is exactly that policy. Most days a naked short option behaves like a spread with a little more income, because the storm that would reach far beyond the strike does not come. The extra income is the market paying you to remove the limit. It is never enough, because the event it insures against is the one that ends companies, and an insurer is not paid for most days. He is paid for surviving the one day that matters.

The deductible is where the seller's real choice sits. A short strike close to the price is a policy with a small deductible: it is claimed often, and it pays a large premium for that reason. A strike far away is a large deductible: claimed rarely, paid little. Both are legitimate products with different claim rates. The error is mixing the two in your head: writing the near strike for its premium and then being surprised by the claims, or writing the far strike and sizing it as if it could never be claimed.

"First be beyond defeat, then wait" also fixes the order of operations that most traders reverse. They find a direction they believe in, then look for a way to size it. The seller does the opposite: he fixes the size such that the printed maximum loss is a normal, boring expense, and only then asks where the line should be. The line can be wrong; that is Tuesday. The size cannot be wrong, because the size is what decides whether Wednesday happens.

The fair reading of "standing on the defensive indicates insufficient strength" is that it is true, and that is fine. The seller's edge, where it exists, is a small, structural premium for holding a line that others must cross. It is not large enough to survive being attacked with. Whoever treats it as a war chest has misread the ledger. But the first edition of this book stopped reading the general at that line, and he did not stop writing there. In the next breath he says that defence and attack are two halves of one skill: one protects, the other completes the victory. The seller's half is complete in this chapter. The buyer's verse is placed beside it so that the sentence is read whole, and Part V returns to it.

The buyer has the general's invincibility for free: his worst day is the debit, paid in advance, and nothing the market does can make it larger. What he lacks is the seller's frequency of small wins, so his invincibility is tested in a different way, not by one catastrophe but by a long run of total losses on a low win rate. If a long run of debits is more than his account or his nerve can carry, his position was never unbeatable. Only his ticket was. Chapter 16 counts that run.

The firewood is the buyer's version of the limit, and the arithmetic is easy, which is why it is so often skipped. A 100 call bought and a 110 call sold for a debit of 3 has a maximum loss of 300 and a maximum gain of 700. If the risk budget is 1,000, three spreads cost 900 and a fourth would cost 1,200. The count is three. Rounding up to four does not make the risk 1,000. It makes the plan a fiction.

Mai's "risk one to make ten" is often quoted as though the ten were the point. In Schwager's interview the one comes first, and it is small. His bets were expected to fail, most of them, and a book built that way has to survive a run of failures to collect the one that does not. Ten bets at a tenth of the book each, all lost, is the whole book. That is not convexity. It is ten chances to be finished before the thesis arrives.

So the order of operations is the same at both ends of the ticket. The seller fixes the size so that a maximum loss is boring; the buyer fixes it so that a run of total losses is boring. Only then does either of them look for a line, or a door.

### 5. Force and the Four Quarters

## The Seller

1. The control of a large force is the same in principle as the control of a few: it is a matter of dividing it.

2. Divide the day's risk in four before the day divides it for you.

3. One quarter for the direction you took. One for the hedge you will need. One for the direction that returns. One for the fluctuation you must simply survive.

4. Full size on the first entry is not conviction. It is the absence of a plan for being wrong.

5. In all fighting the direct method is used for joining battle, and the indirect for securing victory. The direct method is the first spread, sold in the direction of the trend. The indirect is the second side, sold only when the first is hurt, which turns a bet into a condor.

6. The direct and the indirect lead on to each other in turn. It is like moving in a circle: you never come to an end. The trend read, the hedge, the trend again, the exit: the whole day is four turns of one wheel.

7. Energy is the bending of the crossbow; timing is the release of the trigger. The seller's energy is his credit already collected. His timing is the stop.

8. The quality of decision is like the well-timed swoop of a falcon which enables it to strike and destroy its victim. The stop is not a retreat. It is the swoop.

9. Division is false if the parts are the same part. A hundred policies on the same coast are one policy. The hurricane does not visit them one at a time.

10. Puts sold on the index, on the technology fund, and on the five largest companies in the index are three names and one coast. On a quiet day they move separately. On the day of the storm they move together. Correlation is a fair-weather word.

11. Add up the book by coast, not by ticket. Ask how much the whole book pays if the market falls five percent before the term ends. That number is the real size, and it gets a limit of its own.

12. Time is a coast too. Ten policies all expiring on the same afternoon are one claim waiting for one bad afternoon. Spread the terms.

13. A put spread and a call spread on the same index, sold together, cover two coasts that one move cannot reach at once. That is a real reduction in risk. But both are written on one sea, and the condor is one policy with two sides, not two policies.

14. Compounding is the momentum of a round stone rolled down a mountain. Ten in a hundred, kept and rolled thirty times, becomes a number that no longer looks like ten.

15. Double your money and lose half, and you are exactly where you began, having paid for the ride. The account does not remember your best day. It remembers the product of all of them.

16. Therefore the good seller seeks his result from the shape of the sequence, not from any single battle. He does not demand too much of one trade. He demands that the next trade be possible.

17. Small and repeatable outruns large and rare, and it does it quietly. Boring is the product.

## The Buyer

18. The buyer divides his force too, but by time rather than by side.

19. He does not buy the whole position at once. One part when the setup appears, one when the catalyst is near, one when the market confirms. A ladder, not a leap.

20. Each rung is sized so that if the trade ends at that rung, the loss is that rung's debit alone.

21. The buyer's energy is the asymmetry he has paid for; his timing is the catalyst. Bought too early, the energy leaks away as rent. Bought too late, it has already been spent by others.

22. *Clamour in the east, strike in the west* (第6计 声东击西). Drums in the east, the army crosses in the west. The noise is not a lie about the war. It is a lie about the place.

23. The edge at one level is not the edge at another. A finding about stocks remains a finding about stocks until it has been tested in the option that will carry it. The option adds a clock, a spread and a price of fear that the stock never had.

24. Strike where the edge lands. Sometimes the option is the cheap expression. Sometimes it is the shares, and the option only adds a clock the thesis does not need.

25. Five call spreads in five names that rise on the same rate and fall on the same fear are not five attacks. They are one attack bought five times, and they get one budget between them.

26. *Chain stratagems* (第35计 连环计). A trade is a chain: the calendar, the five sentences, the size, the alert, the log, the review. One weak link and the rest is cargo. A chain is not a mood; it is a routine that runs in the weeks you do not feel like running it.

27. Compounding is harsher on the buyer than on the seller, because his results arrive rarely and in large pieces. A long run of small losses shrinks the base on which the rare win must multiply.

28. Therefore the buyer's falcon is the time stop and the profit taken. A long option that has doubled is not yet money. Held to expiry, it often turns back into a lesson.

## Commentary

The general's chapter on energy is usually read as a chapter on momentum, and the seller's momentum is compounding. Returns multiply; they do not add. A plus-one-hundred followed by a minus-fifty is two times one half: exactly nothing, after a month of adrenaline. The same account taking a smaller figure without a reset ends the period on a number that looks like an error. This is not a claim about markets; it is what multiplication does, and it can be checked on paper in a minute.

The four quarters are the seller's division of force. The first quarter is the read: one spread, in the direction of the trend, at a quarter of what you are willing to lose today. The second is the hedge, sold only when the first side starts to hurt, which completes an iron condor instead of closing in fear or doubling in pride. The third is the re-entry when the turn comes, on better information than the open offered. The fourth buys nothing and earns nothing: it is the room to let an open position breathe against you without being closed by your broker or by yourself.

The insurer adds the warning that a division can be an illusion. Insurers learned accumulation the expensive way. A company could write thousands of home policies, each one modest, and discover after a single hurricane that most of them stood within forty miles of the same shoreline. Every policy passed the individual size check. The book as a whole had written one enormous policy on one storm. The seller's coasts are less visible and just as real: a put spread on the index, another on the technology fund and a third on a large technology stock behave like three positions on an ordinary day and like one on the day the market falls hard. The question that replaces the count of tickets is a single sentence, asked before every new position: *what else in the book pays out on the same day, for the same reason?*

The Thirty-Six Stratagems have the same picture at Red Cliffs. The northern fleet lashed its ships together so that they would ride steady in the swell, and the steadiness was real. So was the price: one fire, set at one end, reached every ship. A book of policies chained by one factor is that fleet. It rides more smoothly than a scattered one in calm water, and it burns all at once.

Nothing in this chapter says you will earn ten percent at any interval, ever. It says only that if you ever do have a repeatable process, what decides where you finish is not the size of your best result. It is whether the sequence stays unbroken, and the stop, the falcon's swoop, is what keeps it so.

The buyer's division is by time. A ladder of entries, the first small at the setup, the next as the catalyst approaches, the last only on confirmation, lets him pay less for being early and commit more only once the market has agreed. It also keeps the first loss, which is the most common one, the smallest.

The sixth stratagem is the buyer's warning about where his force lands. A study can find a real effect in stocks, a group of names that doubled far more often than names picked at random, and the effect can vanish in the options. In one such test, of 87 cases where a call spread could have expressed the idea, only 10 were tradeable at all, and one of those ten won. The direction was right and the structure still lost, because the move came late, or small, or already paid for. That is not a weak version of a strong stock result. It is a different trade wearing the same name. So the buyer asks where his edge actually lands before he chooses the instrument, and sometimes the honest answer is the shares, or nothing.

The chain is what makes the ladder possible. No single link is impressive: an hour on Sunday with the calendar, the five sentences said before each entry, the size divided down, the exit written while the head is cool, the log kept in facts rather than verdicts, the week reviewed. Together they turn a rare winner into something findable, and they hold in the weeks when the trader does not feel like holding them. A size rounded up once undoes every calculation before it, which is the whole meaning of the image.

Compounding is the buyer's harder lesson. His returns come rarely and in large pieces, and multiplication punishes the gaps between them. Twenty debits of one percent each, all lost, leave the account at about eighty-two percent of itself; the win that follows is measured against that smaller base, not the original. The arithmetic can be checked on paper, and it tells the buyer what it tells the seller: the sequence decides, and the sequence is protected by size.

### 6. Weak Points and Strong

## The Seller

1. Whoever is first in the field and awaits the enemy will be fresh for the fight; whoever comes second and has to hasten to battle will arrive exhausted.

2. The seller who sets his line while the tape is calm chooses his ground. The seller who chases a move that has already happened is sold the worst price of the day, and calls it conviction.

3. By holding out advantages to him, you can cause the enemy to approach of his own accord. The market approaches your line of its own accord; you do not go to meet it.

4. You can be sure of succeeding in your attacks if you only attack places which are undefended. The seller attacks undefended ground: the strike the market must travel far to reach, beyond the wall where dealer hedging leans against it.

5. You can ensure the safety of your defence if you only hold positions that cannot be attacked. A line far enough, with a wing behind it, cannot be attacked by an ordinary day. It can only be attacked by the exceptional one, and for that, the wing.

6. Do not repeat the tactics which have gained you one victory, but let your methods be regulated by the infinite variety of circumstances. The line that held yesterday is not the line for today. The walls move.

7. Military tactics are like water; for water in its natural course runs away from high places and hastens downwards. So in trading: avoid what is strong and strike at what is weak. Avoid the direction the crowd has paid for; sell the range the crowd has abandoned.

8. Water shapes its course according to the nature of the ground over which it flows. The seller shapes his position according to the enemy he is facing.

9. Weak and strong have a price. Every policy has two: the premium the buyer pays, and the loss cost, which is what the policy will pay out on average. The business lives in the gap between them.

10. The premium is the market's fear. In an option it is called implied volatility: the size of move the buyers are paying to be covered against. The loss cost is the market's truth: the move that actually comes, realised volatility, measured after the fact.

11. When fear is larger than truth, the seller is paid for writing. When truth is larger than fear, every policy written is a gift to the buyer.

12. Fear is usually larger than truth, because people pay more to sleep than the storm will cost them. That is not a law of nature. It is a habit of buyers, and habits break.

13. Keep your own table. An insurer prices from his own claims history, not from the salesman's brochure. The seller's table is a record of how far the market actually travelled over the terms he writes, and how often it reached the strikes he sells.

14. If one week in ten the market reached the strike, and the average claim when it did was three, the loss cost is three tenths. A premium of three tenths is a policy written for nothing. A premium of a half is a business.

15. Never price a policy from the premium alone. A rich premium on a risk that is about to happen is not rich. It is accurate. And when premium is thin, the right size is small, and the right number is often none.

16. Therefore the seller does not predict. He responds. The first spread is a read. The hedge is a response. The cut is a response. The wing left to ride is a response. Nothing in the book is a forecast.

17. He who can modify his tactics in relation to his opponent and thereby succeed in winning may be called a heaven-born captain. He who can turn a directional spread into a condor, and a condor into a lone wing riding a trend, without ever adding to what is losing, may be called a seller.

18. The five elements are not always equally predominant; the four seasons make way for each other in turn. The market has its seasons too: flat is its ordinary one, and direction is a treat.

## The Buyer

19. The buyer, too, should be first in the field. Buy while the tape is calm and the rent is cheap; do not buy after the move, when everyone who is late is bidding for the same right.

20. Attack undefended places. The buyer attacks the move the sellers have stopped defending: the option whose implied volatility sits below what the calendar is likely to deliver.

21. Avoid what is strong. The strong place for the buyer is the expensive option, whose price already contains the move he expects.

22. *Take the goat in passing* (第12计 顺手牵羊). The column passes the village at dusk; a goat stands by the road; a soldier leads it away without breaking step. The seller's goat is the volatility premium, and most days it stands by his road, not the buyer's.

23. The goat is the buyer's only when it is untended: after a crush, when implied sits below realised and a dated event still lies ahead. Quiet is not cheap. A stock at a three-year low in realised volatility is a description, not a signal.

24. *Hide a dagger behind a smile* (第10计 笑里藏刀). The far weekly smiles: a few cents on the screen buy a fortune at the far strike. The smile is the price. The dagger is the probability.

25. The far strike must be crossed twice, once by the stock and once by the clock. The first is unlikely. The second is certain. Cheapness is not a gift. It is a quote for a thing that almost never happens, made by people who have counted.

26. *Secretly cross at Chencang* (第8计 暗渡陈仓). The crowd repairs the plank road of the earnings date in plain sight and looks only at the gap. The drift that follows is the road nobody watches, and it is bought cheaper after the print, when the fear has left the price.

27. *Create something from nothing* (第7计 无中生有). Straw men on the wall drew the besiegers' arrows. A backtest turned until it rises is a straw man at dusk. Write the rule, the exits and the test that would kill it before you look, and date the page.

28. Water runs away from high places. The buyer's water runs toward cheap volatility and away from rich.

29. The trader who held the wing left by a seller's cut was already a buyer, whether or not he called himself one. That wing is the one long option in this book bought for nothing, because the credit paid for it.

30. Therefore the buyer does not predict either. He prices. The question is not whether the market will move, but whether it will move more than the option's price says.

## Commentary

Weak points and strong is the chapter of the hedge. The old general's water finds the low ground; the seller's position finds the side of the market that is not moving. When the first spread, sold in the direction of the trend, begins to hurt, the answer is not to defend it and not to double it. It is to sell the other side. The bet becomes a condor, the credit doubles, and a bad morning becomes a flat afternoon. The second side is a reaction to being hurt, never a schedule; a condor sold at the open is a different trade with twice the risk and none of the information.

From the condor there are two futures. If the market goes flat, both credits are collected and the day is ordinary. If the trend resumes and runs through a line, the seller does not cut the losing vertical whole. He buys back the short leg only and keeps the wing, which is cheap, already paid for, and now the only thing in the book that profits if the move continues. Paired with the winning credit spread on the other side, it is a net long riding the very move that hurt him.

"Do not repeat the tactics that won" has a dull, exact meaning: the strikes that were safe yesterday are not information about today. The walls of dealer positioning move overnight, the expected move changes with volatility, and the line must be chosen again every morning, in the temple, before the first order.

The insurer gives "weak and strong" a number. Every insurer in history has been in one business: collect slightly more in premium than will be paid out in claims, across enough policies for the difference to become reliable. The option seller's version of that margin has a name, the gap between implied and realised volatility, and a long-documented habit: over long stretches, buyers of index protection have usually paid more than the moves they were protected against turned out to cost. The habit is the seller's wage. It is not guaranteed, and it is thinnest exactly when the market feels calmest.

The table in verse 14 is the actuarial sum in its simplest form, and it can be redone with your own numbers. If the market reached the strike one week in ten, and the average loss on the spread when it did was three, each policy costs three tenths on average. Sell it for three tenths and you have built a machine that pays your commissions. Sell it for a half and the margin is two tenths a policy, before costs. The danger is that the table is made of the past. A calm month makes every premium look generous, and a seller who prices from the last month alone writes his cheapest policies just before his most expensive claims. The cure is a long table, an honest one, and a willingness to write nothing.

For the buyer, weak and strong are measured in the same price from the other side. The weak point is the option priced for a calmer future than the calendar promises; the strong point is the one priced for exactly the move he expects, or more. Buying the strong point is not a mistake about direction. It is paying full price for a right the seller has already charged for, and it is the most common way to be right about the market and still lose money.

The Counted Trade's second set of stratagems is a single lesson about reading the other side's price, and four of them belong here. The goat is the variance premium: most days it is the seller's, and the buyer who pays for calm is buying fair-weather insurance from someone who knows the weather. The exception has a shape, not a slogan: implied below realised, after a crush, with a dated event still ahead. The dagger is the far weekly, cheap in dollars because it is unlikely, not because its volatility is cheap; the smile on the volatility surface is the honest name of that cheapness. Chencang is the quiet road after the event, where the same directional thesis is bought without paying for the gate. And the straw men are the backtest that was turned until it rose. A pre-registered test does not create an edge. It makes the absence of one visible, which is worth more than most edges, and Chapter 15 records one such test that failed.

The wing that survives the seller's cut is where the two halves of this book meet. It was bought as insurance, paid for by the credit, and on the day the line breaks it becomes the only long option on the board that cost nothing to hold. A seller who has learned to let that wing ride has learned the buyer's whole art in one position: a small, paid-for right, held only while the move that justifies it is still under way.

## Part III: The March

Armies lose more men on the march than in battle. Chapters seven to nine are about movement: the cost of hurry, the rules that must bend and the rules that must not, and how to read the ground and the enemy from the signs they leave.

In the market, the march is the trading day. Most accounts are not lost to one great battle. They are lost to marching too fast, trading too often, and reading omens where there are only signs.

### 7. Manoeuvring

## The Seller

1. There is nothing harder than manoeuvring. The difficulty consists in turning the devious into the direct, and misfortune into gain. 以迂为直，以患为利。

2. The seller's manoeuvre is the hedge: the devious route by which a losing morning is turned into a flat afternoon. It is slow, it is unglamorous, and it is the whole of the skill.

3. To take a long and circuitous route, and though starting after the enemy, to reach the goal before him, shows knowledge of the artifice of deviation. The seller who takes the small loss today reaches next year before the one who would not.

4. If you march your army in haste to seize an advantage, you will arrive with a tenth of your force. The trader who chases every move arrives at the important one with no capital and no temper.

5. An army without its baggage train is lost. A seller without his fourth quarter, the room to sit through a fluctuation, is closed out by his own broker in the hour before the trade resolves.

6. We cannot enter into alliance until we are acquainted with the designs of our neighbours. We cannot sell a line until we are acquainted with the walls of the day, the expected move, and the event on the calendar.

7. Let your rapidity be that of the wind, your compactness that of the forest. Be rapid in cutting. Be compact in size.

8. A claim is not a failure. It is the reason the premium was paid. A seller who is outraged by claims is in the wrong business.

9. Decide the settlement before the sale: at what price the short leg is closed, and how much the claim is allowed to cost. A claim settled by rule is a cost. A claim settled by hope is a lawsuit with the market.

10. Settle small and early. The cheapest claim is the one paid while it is still small. Do not argue with the claim; the market does not read your reasons, and the delay it charges for arguing is always larger than the claim.

11. Do not write the next policy to make back the last claim. The next policy has its own price, and the last claim is already paid.

12. In raiding, divide your spoils. In taking a credit, take it: the seller who waits for the last tenth of a credit is paid a tenth and risks the whole.

13. He who knows the art of the direct and the indirect will win. The direct is the trend spread. The indirect is the condor. Neither is an addition to the other; each is a substitution.

14. The seller who sells a second spread beside a losing one has not manoeuvred. He has doubled. A long wing and a credit sold with the trend are the same directional bet in two costumes; count the net direction, not the legs.

15. Never stack a second position on the same direction. Size over frequency. One exposure, hedged or cut, never repeated.

16. Do not trade non-stop. When the condor's balance breaks, when a short is cut or a hedge is sold, that is a resolution, not an invitation. An engine that re-enters every time its shape changes has no ceiling, and a trader without a ceiling has no floor.

17. A whole army may be robbed of its spirit; a commander may be robbed of his presence of mind. The tape robs the seller of presence of mind by moving quickly. His defence is that his decisions were made in the temple, slowly.

18. Do not pursue an enemy who simulates flight. Do not chase a reversal that has not confirmed. Do not attack soldiers whose temper is keen; do not sell into a panic that is still accelerating. Leave a way of escape to a surrounded enemy; leave a wing in every spread.

## The Buyer

19. The buyer's manoeuvre is patience before the catalyst and speed after it.

20. If the buyer marches in haste, buying every flicker of movement, he arrives at the real move with his debits spent and his nerve gone.

21. The buyer's baggage train is the cash he did not spend on the last ten ideas. Without it the eleventh, which is the right one, must be passed by.

22. *To catch, first let go* (第16计 欲擒故纵). The net is opened on one side, not from mercy, but so that the quarry runs into the side that is closed.

23. A plan made at entry has targets in it. A target named after the price has moved is a wish with a number attached. Write both lines before entry: the price at which part is released, and the date at which all of it is.

24. In raiding, divide your spoils. When the long option has paid, release part at the first target: the trade can no longer cost the whole debit, and what has come back is the right to choose again.

25. Sell everything at the first target and the buyer has bought a wage, not an asymmetry. Sell nothing and he holds a lottery ticket with a thesis attached. The remainder, released on a date and not on a feeling, is the only part that can pay for the losers.

26. Do not stack the same direction in two costumes. A call bought and a put sold on the same name are one bet; so is a call bought beside stock already owned. Count the net direction.

27. Do not pursue an enemy who simulates flight. Do not buy the reversal that has not confirmed. Do not buy the option after the gap; buy before it, or not at all.

## Commentary

Manoeuvring is the chapter about frequency, and frequency is a risk dial that most traders do not know they are turning. The old general loses his baggage by marching too fast; the seller loses his fourth quarter by trading too often. Every additional entry in a day is a levy on the reserve that was supposed to let an open position breathe. By noon the fast marcher is full size in one direction with nothing left for the hedge, and the first ordinary fluctuation closes him out.

The first line of the chapter is the oldest statement of an idea that has since travelled far. 以迂为直, make the devious route the direct: take the long way round, accept a cost or a delay now, and arrive before the enemy who marched straight. Mark Spitznagel's *The Dao of Capital* (2013) is built on this idea, under a German name, the *Umweg*, the roundabout path. His name for it comes from the Austrian economists. The idea is Sun Tzu's, and this is where he wrote it down, in the chapter on 军争, the contest of armies. Spitznagel applies it to capital across decades: the investor who accepts small losses and an indirect route to arrive in a stronger position later. This book applies it to the single trade and the single season: the seller whose hedge turns a losing morning into a flat afternoon, the small claim settled early so that the account reaches next year intact. The horizons are different and the source is the same, and it should be credited to the general.

The insurer reads the same chapter as claims handling. Insurers that fail usually fail in claims before they fail in capital. Delaying, disputing and hoping a claim goes away feels cheaper in the moment and is always more expensive in the end. The seller's version is the tested spread held past its stop because the market *should* turn: the argument is with a counterparty that does not listen, and the cost of losing it grows with every point the price travels into the policy. The adjustment that is a legitimate part of claims handling, rather than a new bet, is the condor built in the moment of injury: when one side is tested, the other side of the same index is usually far away and cheap to write, and the new premium reduces the cost of the claim. It is written because one side is already in claim, not as a habit, which is why the rule is to hedge only when hurt.

The door the gambler comes back through is the next policy. After a claim, it wants to be larger, or nearer, or written on a hunch, so that the account can be made whole by Friday. That policy is no longer priced by the table in Chapter 6. It is priced by the loss, and a seller whose prices are set by his last claim has stopped underwriting and started chasing.

Two rules in this chapter are absolute. The first is never to stack on the same direction. It is easy to believe that a second credit spread, sold with the trend, is "pairing" with the wing that survived a cut. But a long wing and a spread sold in the same direction are the same bet, and pairing them is doubling on the side that has just run through a strike. Read a book by its net exposure, not its leg count. The second is the ceiling: the position's own state must limit how many times it can open, and "usually stops re-entering" is not a rule.

Rapid as the wind is about one action only: the cut. Everything else in this trade is meant to be slow: the temple count, the line chosen while the tape is calm, the hedge sold only when hurt. Speed kept for the exit, and nowhere else, is what separates manoeuvre from panic.

For the buyer the danger of the march is the same and the cost is more visible. Every debit spent on a move that did not come is gone; it does not come back as a hedge or a re-entry. A buyer who trades often spends his reserve on noise, and the reserve is exactly what he needed on the day a real catalyst arrived with a real mispricing. For the seller, frequency is a risk dial. For the buyer, it is a bill.

Rapid as the wind, for the buyer, is the taking of profit, and the sixteenth stratagem is its plan. A long option that has paid is now worth mostly volatility and time, and both leave quickly once the move is over. A scale-out written before entry does one specific thing: it removes the chance that a trade which was right for a while becomes a total loss, because part of the debit has already come back. What remains is a smaller position with a defined date. The plan must be written before entry, because after entry the mind negotiates. A target invented afterwards always fits the current price, and a date invented afterwards always allows one more week. The buyer who sells part at once and lets the rest run on a stop has kept the spoils. The one who waits for the whole move has lent them back to the seller.

### 8. Variation

## The Seller

1. There are roads which must not be followed, armies which must not be attacked, towns which must not be besieged, positions which must not be contested, commands of the sovereign which must not be obeyed.

2. There are trades which must not be taken. There are lines which must not be defended. There are losses which must not be held. There are signals which must not be followed, however good the source. There are opinions of your own which must not be obeyed.

3. The general who thoroughly understands the advantages that accompany variation of tactics knows how to handle his troops. He who does not understand these may be well acquainted with the ground, and yet not be able to turn his knowledge to account.

4. Ground is the setup. Variation is what you do when the setup is wrong. Most traders study ground for years and variation for never.

5. The wise seller considers both advantage and disadvantage in one thought. In the advantage — the credit — he keeps the disadvantage in view: the four he can lose. In the disadvantage — the stop — he keeps the advantage in view: the sequence it protects.

6. The art of war teaches us to rely not on the likelihood of the enemy's not coming, but on our own readiness to receive him. The art of selling teaches us to rely not on the likelihood that the line holds, but on the plan for the day it does not.

7. There are five dangerous faults which may affect a general: recklessness, cowardice, a hasty temper, a delicacy of honour, and over-solicitude for his men.

8. There are five dangerous faults which may affect a seller. Recklessness, which sells too wide and too much. Cowardice, which closes a working spread at the first fluctuation and never collects. A hasty temper, which trades back at the market that hurt it. A delicacy of honour, which will not take a small loss because a small loss admits a small error. And over-solicitude for the position, which holds a broken line out of loyalty to the morning's opinion.

9. These are the five besetting sins of a seller, ruinous to the conduct of a campaign. When an account is overturned and its trader slain, the cause will surely be found among these five.

10. Of the five, the fourth is the deadliest. The trader who cannot cut does not have a strategy. He has a hostage.

11. Therefore the rule for variation is one rule: when the reason for the trade is gone, the trade is gone. Not the money — the reason. The money follows.

## The Buyer

12. The buyer has five dangerous faults too, and they are the seller's turned inside out.

13. Recklessness, which buys too much of a cheap thing because it is cheap. Cowardice, which sells the winner at the first profit and holds every loser to zero. A hasty temper, which buys the next move to win back the last debit. A delicacy of honour, which will not admit that the catalyst has passed. And over-solicitude for the idea, which buys next month when this month expires worthless.

14. Of the five, the last is the deadliest for the buyer. Rolling a dead thesis forward is not patience. It is paying rent twice on a house that has burned.

15. There are options which must not be bought: the one already priced for the move, the one whose reason has no date, and the one whose debit is larger than a loss he would accept without comment.

16. The rule of variation is the same for the buyer: when the reason is gone, the option is gone. The buyer's reason is a date, and when the date passes, so does the reason.

17. *Sacrifice the plum for the peach* (第11计 李代桃僵). A worm came to the peach root, and the plum tree beside it took the bite and stiffened in its place. The peach lived.

18. The far strike is the plum. Sell it, and the near strike is bought cheaper; the debit that remains is the whole risk. The plum sold is the tail beyond the far strike, the part of the move the buyer has agreed not to be paid for.

19. Buy the 100 call, sell the 110 call, pay 3. The ticket states the count before the market moves: maximum loss 3, maximum gain 7, breakeven at 103. The win rate needed to break even is the debit divided by the width: three in ten.

20. A long call bought at ten to one keeps the whole tail and needs one win in eleven. The spread needs three in ten, and a move to 103. Know which tree you are in. If the thesis is a doubling, the spread caps it; if the thesis is a move to 108, the single leg is a donation.

21. Variation is choosing the tree after the thesis is known, never before. A spread sized like a lottery ticket, or a lottery ticket held like a spread, is not a variation. It is a muddle.

## Commentary

The chapter on variation is the general's warning that rules without exceptions are for men who do not intend to survive contact. The seller's exceptions are the roads not to follow: the event day when the expected move is a lie, the line that would be right if only the wall had not moved overnight, the signal that arrives from a trusted source and is wrong anyway. Knowing the setup is the ground; knowing when the setup no longer applies is the variation, and it is the harder study.

The five faults translate almost without effort, and the translation is unkind. Recklessness is size. Cowardice is the seller who never lets a credit mature. Hasty temper is revenge trading — selling back at the market within minutes of a cut, on no read at all. Delicacy of honour is the deadliest because it is invisible: it feels like conviction, and it is nothing but the refusal to be wrong at a size that would not matter. Over-solicitude is the loyalty a trader feels toward the opinion he had at 9:31, long after the market has stopped sharing it.

The single rule of variation is that the reason ends the trade, not the price. A spread sold because the trend was up, in a market whose structure has just broken to the downside, has lost its reason whether or not it has yet lost money. Wait for the money to confirm what the reason already said and you will have waited exactly as long as the market needs to charge you for it.

The buyer's five faults are easy to recognise because each is the seller's fault reversed. The seller's cowardice closes winners too early and never collects; the buyer's does the same to his rare winners, which is worse, because the rare winners are the whole of his income. The seller's delicacy of honour will not cut a broken spread; the buyer's will not admit that the event has come and gone. And the seller's loyalty to a morning opinion becomes, for the buyer, the roll: the expired call replaced with next month's, at a fresh debit, on a reason that has already been tested and failed.

Variation for the buyer comes down to the time stop. The seller's trade ends when price crosses a line; the buyer's ends when the calendar crosses a date. Both stops are set in the temple, and both are broken the same way, by a man who decides on the tape that today is the exception.

The eleventh stratagem is the buyer's chapter on variation because it is the choice of shape, and shape is where a buyer most often varies without knowing he has. The sacrifice is exact. A ten-wide spread bought for 3 has a maximum loss of 3, a maximum gain of 7 and a breakeven at 103, and needs to be right three times in ten. Pay 2.50 instead and it risks 2.50 to make 7.50, three to one, and needs one win in four. The single call bought at ten to one needs only one win in eleven, which looks easier until the event is named: the ten-to-one ticket needs something near a doubling, and the spread needs a move of three points. The spread has traded the tenfold tail for a breakeven a modest move can reach. Which is better depends on what the thesis actually predicts, not on which payoff is prettier. This is the arithmetic behind Mai's practice of risking one to make ten, as Schwager records it: the buyer who keeps the tail accepts many losses, and the one who sells it accepts that the doubling will be paid to someone else. Both are coherent. Mixing them is not.

The seller has his own plum and peach, and they are the two legs of the broken spread. When the line is run through, the short leg is the plum: it is bought back at a loss, on the rule, and it takes the bite. The peach is the account, and the wing that stays behind to guard it. Cut the short leg, keep the wing. The sacrifice is chosen in the temple, when it is cheap to choose; on the tape every plum looks like a peach, and the trader who cannot give one up loses the orchard.

### 9. The March

## The Seller

1. Camp in high places, facing the sun. Do not climb heights in order to fight. So much for mountain warfare.

2. Sell your line on the high ground: beyond the wall where dealer hedging leans against price, on the side of the market that must climb to reach you. Do not sell into a climb to prove you can. So much for placing a strike.

3. After crossing a river, get far away from it. Do not linger near the level the market has just crossed; a level just broken is the one it comes back to test.

4. In crossing salt marshes, your sole concern should be to get over them quickly. The chop between two levels is a marsh. Do not sell a line inside it; the seller in the marsh is nibbled from both sides and paid for neither.

5. All armies prefer high ground to low, and sunny places to dark. For the seller, high ground is distance from price and sunny places are calm volatility. The low ground and the dark belong to the other bank of the river, where the buyer camps on purpose.

6. When the enemy is close at hand and remains quiet, he is relying on the natural strength of his position. When the tape is quiet near your line, it is not friendship. It is a market gathering.

7. Movement amongst the trees of a forest shows the enemy is advancing. The rise of birds in flight is the sign of an ambuscade. The market's signs are the same: a pivot taken out, a change of character in the swing, a trailing stop that has flipped. The structure changes before the price does.

8. The seller reads the trend by its structure, not by his hope. A higher low held is a market that has not changed its mind. A higher low broken is a market that has, whatever the last candle says.

9. When the ATR trail flips, the trend has spoken. Sell puts beneath a market that ratchets up. Sell calls above a market that ratchets down. Sell neither while it is deciding.

10. Dust rising in a high column is the sign of chariots; dust low and wide, of infantry. Volume surging at a level is the sign of a wall; volume dying into a move is the sign of a move that has run out of enemy.

11. If the enemy's troops march up angrily and remain facing ours for a long time without either joining battle or taking themselves off, the situation demands great vigilance. A market that presses your line for an hour and does not break it is the most dangerous market there is, because it looks like safety.

12. If soldiers are punished before they have grown attached to you, they will not prove submissive. If you cut a spread before it has ever been allowed to work, you will learn nothing and collect nothing. Let the position breathe inside the fourth quarter; cut it at the line you chose, not at the first breath.

13. The seller marches by the clock. He is paid first and pays claims later. The money held in between is float, the oldest advantage of the insurer.

14. When a spread is sold, the credit arrives that morning. It is not yet profit. It is premium held in trust for a policy that has not ended, and it is earned only as the term passes. The decay of an option is the insurer's earning, hour by hour, of money he already holds.

15. Do not count unearned premium as income, and do not size the next policy on it. Float is not capital.

16. The last days of a term earn the most premium and carry the sharpest claims. Near the end, a policy is all reward or all claim within hours. Decide in the temple which policies are held to the end and which are closed before it; in the last afternoon there is no time left to decide.

17. He who exercises no forethought but makes light of his opponents is sure to be captured by them.

## The Buyer

18. The buyer camps in the low places and waits for the sun. His ground is calm volatility that is about to stop being calm.

19. The buyer reads the same signs as the seller and acts on the opposite ones. A pivot broken is the seller's warning and the buyer's invitation.

20. When the ATR trail flips, the buyer may follow it: calls behind a market that has turned up, puts behind one that has turned down. Neither while it is deciding; deciding is where rent is paid for nothing.

21. Volume dying into a quiet range is the sign of a coiled market. That is the buyer's high ground: the move nobody is paying for yet.

22. A market that presses a level for an hour without breaking it is the seller's danger and the buyer's patience. He does not buy the press. He buys the break, small, with his stop at the level.

23. *Beat the grass to startle the snake* (第13计 打草惊蛇). The beater strikes the grass, not the snake. The rustle tells him where the snake was, and the snake, startled, tells him more than any map.

24. A paper trade is grass beaten at no price. Thirty to fifty forward paper trades on one setup, each written down before money touches it, will show the ground: whether the strike fills, how wide the spread sits at the open, whether the move arrives on the day.

25. The first probe with money is the smallest position the market will carry. A probe that costs a real share of the risk budget is not a probe. It is a position wearing a probe's coat.

26. The seller's float is the buyer's bill. Every day the seller earns a little of what he holds, the buyer pays a little of what he paid. The buyer marches by the catalyst; the clock only charges him.

## Commentary

The army on the march is the chapter about reading signs, and the seller's signs are structural. Direction is not read from a feeling or a headline but from the market's own pivots: whether the last swing low held or broke, whether the character of the swing has changed, whether a trailing stop that ratchets with volatility has flipped from one side of price to the other. This is the read that decides which side is sold. Bull sells puts. Bear sells calls. Undecided sells nothing and waits, which is also a position.

High ground and river crossings have exact counterparts. The high ground is distance from price, placed beyond the level where dealer hedging leans: the wall that pins a rising market or the floor that catches a falling one. The river is a level just crossed, and the general's advice to get far from it is the seller's rule never to sell the strike the market has just broken through; it returns to test what it broke. The marsh is the chop between two levels, and no line inside it is defensible. The low ground is not a mistake; it is a different camp. The first edition called a seller on the low ground a buyer who had not noticed. Better to say it plainly: the low ground is where the buyer camps on purpose, and a seller found there has wandered into the other army's country.

The sign that matters most is the quiet one. A market pressing against your line without breaking it is not evidence the line holds; it is the enemy standing close at hand and remaining quiet, relying on the strength of his position. Vigilance here means having already decided what the cut price is, so that when the quiet ends you are executing a decision rather than making one.

The insurer marches by a different sign, the clock. Float is why great insurers have often been great investors: they hold large sums of other people's premium for long stretches before the claims come due. The seller's float is small and short, a credit held for days or weeks, but the principle and the trap are the same. The credit sitting in the account looks like profit. Until the policy expires, it is a liability with a balance attached. A spread sold for one with ten days to expiry has not made one on the first day; it has made whatever the clock has released, and the rest is held against a claim. Treating the whole credit as earned is how a book ends up sizing new policies on money it may have to give back. And because decay quickens near expiry, the final days release the most premium at the same moment that price near the strike swings the claim hardest. The seller is paid by the clock. The trader wants to be paid by the move. They are different trades.

The buyer reads the same signs from the other bank of the river. The quiet market the seller treats as safety, the buyer treats as stored energy: realised volatility falling, volume drying up, a range narrowing toward a dated event. That is where options are cheapest, and where a buyer pays the least rent for the most possible movement.

The thirteenth stratagem is usually read as a warning not to alert the enemy. For the buyer it is better read as a method. The snake is the market's behaviour around a setup, and the only way to see it is to disturb the grass and watch. The disturbance must be cheap, or it is not observation but commitment. Paper is the cheapest grass, and thirty to fifty forward trades is not a magic number: it is large enough that a single lucky week does not become a method, and small enough that a working setup is not buried under a decade of waiting. The first money is a probe at minimum size. What must not happen is the probe growing before the count is complete. A probe that becomes a position has stopped asking questions.

The signs of a turning trend are the same signs for both. When the structure breaks, the higher low lost and the trail flipped, the seller moves his line and the buyer takes his entry. Neither acts while the market is deciding. The seller is not paid there, and the buyer pays rent there for nothing.

## Part IV: Ground and Fire

The last four chapters are the hardest ground. Terrain that must not be fought on. The nine situations an army can find itself in, and what each demands. Fire, the weapon that destroys faster than any sword. And finally spies — because the general who knows wins before he moves.

For the seller: the regimes to refuse, the stages of a trade from first entry to the desperate ground where only the stop remains, volatility as fire, and the journal as the only spy who has never lied to you. For the buyer: the grounds where rent is cheap and the ones where it is a trap, the long middle of a trade where the option bleeds, volatility as the fire he must buy before the smoke, and the same journal, which alone can count the losers beside the winner.

### 10. Terrain

## The Seller

1. We may distinguish six kinds of terrain: accessible ground, entangling ground, temporising ground, narrow passes, precipitous heights, and positions at a great distance from the enemy.

2. The market has six kinds of ground, and the seller must know which he is standing on before he sells a line.

3. Accessible ground is the clean trend: pivots holding, structure intact, volatility calm. On accessible ground, sell in the direction of the trend, beneath it or above it, and let the trend defend you.

4. Entangling ground can be abandoned but is hard to re-occupy. It is the range that looks like a trend for an hour. The seller who sells a directional line here is trapped when the range reasserts; the condor was the right shape from the start, and only on this ground is it sold without being hurt first.

5. Temporising ground is when neither side gains by making the first move. It is the hour before the number, the day before the decision. On temporising ground, do not sell. Whoever moves first is charged for the information the other side is about to receive for free.

6. Narrow passes are the event days: the print, the meeting, the expiry of the giant. If you can occupy them first — sell after the event, into the collapse of volatility — do so. If the enemy holds the pass — sell before the event, into the spike — do not follow him in, however rich the credit looks.

7. Precipitous heights are the gap: the market that has opened far from where it closed. Occupy the sunny side — sell the range the gap has made and the market must now travel far to unmake — and do not chase the gap itself.

8. Positions at a great distance are far expiries. The credit is larger, the wage is slower, and the enemy has more days to arrive. The seller who does not know why he is far from the enemy will find that distance is not safety; it is time, and time is the market's ally too when the line is far off.

9. These six are the principles connected with ground. The general who has attained a responsible post must be careful to study them.

10. When troops flee, are insubordinate, collapse, are disorganised, or are routed, it must be due to natural causes in the general. So with the seller: when a spread is chased, held, doubled, or abandoned in fear, the cause is not the ground. It is the Commander, who did not know which ground he was on.

11. The natural formation of the ground is the soldier's best ally; but a power of estimating the adversary, of controlling the forces of victory, and of shrewdly calculating difficulties, dangers and distances, constitutes the test of a great general.

12. If fighting is sure to result in victory, then you must fight, even though the ruler forbid it. If the count says sell and the mood says wait, sell. If the count says wait and the credit tempts you, wait. The credit is the ruler; the count is the general.

13. Know the enemy and know yourself, and your victory will not stand in doubt. Know the ground and know the sky, and you may make your victory complete.

## The Buyer

14. The buyer has his own map of the six grounds, and it is nearly the seller's map turned over.

15. On accessible ground, the clean trend, the buyer may follow. But options on a calm trend are cheap for a reason, and the buyer must be content with the trend's pace.

16. Temporising ground — the hour before the number — is where the buyer is most tempted and most often charged. The option is richest just before the event and falls the moment the event is known. He who buys into the spike pays for the information and receives the crush.

17. Narrow passes are the buyer's ground only if he arrived first: bought the move days before the event, while the rent was cheap, and sold into the spike.

18. Positions at a great distance — far expiries — are the buyer's friend. They charge less rent per day and give the move time to arrive. A near expiry on a slow thesis is a fuse cut too short.

19. *Fish in troubled waters* (第20计 混水摸鱼). In a high-volatility market the water is already muddy. Protection and lottery tickets are bought in the same hour, and both are marked up.

20. Premium is dearest when buying feels most urgent. To reach for a long single leg in that water is to buy the alarm at its peak. Fish with the spread: the strike that is sold hands back part of the inflated premium, and less has to be true for the same move to pay.

21. Muddy water is not the buyer's ally. It is his price, and he beats it only by paying less for it than the crowd.

22. *Shut the door to catch the thief* (第22计 关门捉贼). The plan is the door, and it shuts at entry. After that, the thief is inside terms already written.

23. Do not move the stop because the price came close. Do not widen the spread because the debit became uncomfortable. Do not extend the time stop because the thesis is "still intact". "Give it room" is the thief's phrase, spoken in the trader's voice.

24. Tightening is permitted. A smaller loss is still a loss taken by plan. Loosening is the theft. On every ground of this chapter, the door is shut before the ground is entered.

## Commentary

Terrain is a chapter about refusal. Of the six grounds, the seller fights well on one — the clean trend — and adequately on two, the range and the post-gap market, provided he changes shape to suit them. The other three are grounds to leave alone: the hour before a scheduled number, the volatility spike into an event, and a far expiry sold without a reason. A trader who sells every day has decided that all six grounds are the same, and the market will charge him tuition on the three that are not.

The entangling ground deserves the closest reading, because it is where the condor is honestly sold at the open. Everywhere else in this book the second side is a hedge, sold only when the first side is hurt, because on trending ground a scheduled condor is a bet against the trend with twice the risk. But a market that has proven itself a range — that has already turned back from both edges, more than once, on ordinary volume — has told the seller what shape to take. The rule is not "never sell both sides at once." The rule is "know why you are."

The last verse is the summary of the book so far: yourself, the enemy, the ground and the sky. The Commander's discipline; the market's ordinary flatness and exceptional direction; the six grounds and which to refuse; and time, which decays in the seller's favour every hour he is not fighting it.

For the buyer the six grounds sort differently. The event pass the seller refuses to enter before the news is the one the buyer is most likely to enter at the worst price: implied volatility peaks just before the print and collapses just after it, so the buyer who pays at the top must be right by more than the market already expected merely to break even. The disciplined buyer arrives at the pass early, while the rent is low, or not at all.

Distance favours him where it disfavours the seller. A far expiry costs more in total but less per day, and its value falls slowly while the thesis matures. The buyer's mistake is the reverse of the seller's: not distance without a reason, but nearness without one — the cheap weekly option bought on a thesis that needs a month.

The twentieth stratagem names a seventh ground the general did not need: the muddy water of a volatility shock. The fisherman in the old story stirs the mud himself. The market needs no help. Fear lifts implied volatility, and it lifts the price of every structure built on it, so the buyer who acts on urgency pays the top of both protection and speculation at once. His error there is structural rather than directional. A long single leg carries the full sensitivity to the chain's fear; a debit spread carries the difference between two, and the strike sold hands back part of the inflated price. The view can be identical. The price of admission is not. And the far bank of muddy water, where the income from selling looks easiest, belongs to the seller with his reserve and his wings. A buyer who crosses over in a panic becomes a seller without a seller's collateral or temperament.

The twenty-second is the rule that holds on all six grounds. The three forbidden moves, moving the stop, widening the spread, extending the time stop, are the same move: each takes a loss that was measured at entry and makes it unmeasured again, at the worst possible moment, when the position is already losing. They rarely arrive as a decision. They arrive as a sentence: the thesis needs one more week, the strike was too close, the date slipped. The sentence is always reasonable, which is what makes it the thief. Tightening is the one revision allowed, because it cannot enlarge the worst case. The seller keeps the same door: the cut price is written in the temple, and the terrain he finds himself on does not get a vote.

### 11. The Nine Situations

## The Seller

1. The art of war recognises nine varieties of ground. The seller's trade passes through nine, from the moment the line is chosen to the moment there is nothing left but the stop.

2. Dispersive ground is the open, when a trader fights in his own territory: full of opinion, full of capital, and most likely to spend both at once. On dispersive ground, do not fight. Count, and place one quarter.

3. Facile ground is the first hour after the line is set, when the position is shallow and nothing has been tested. On facile ground, do not halt to admire the credit. It is not yours yet.

4. Contentious ground is the level both sides want: the wall, the prior high, the round number. Do not attack it. Sell beyond it, and let the market fight for the ground you did not take.

5. Open ground is a trending, liquid tape where each side can move freely. On open ground, do not try to block the enemy's way; sell with the trend and let it pass.

6. Intersecting ground is the news, where three parties meet: the market, the event, and the crowd. On intersecting ground, join hands with your allies. The seller's allies are the wing and the fourth quarter. Do not sell here without both.

7. Serious ground is the middle of the trade, deep in the enemy's country, the credit half-earned and the line untested. On serious ground, gather in plunder: let theta pay. Do not add. Do not fidget. Do not leave early out of nerves, and do not stay late out of greed.

8. Difficult ground is the fluctuation: the tape moving toward the line without yet reaching it, the loss on the screen larger than the credit but smaller than the cut. On difficult ground, keep steadily on the march. This is what the fourth quarter was for.

9. Hemmed-in ground is the broken spread: the short strike breached, the loss at the credit, the reason for the trade gone. On hemmed-in ground, resort to stratagem — the hedge, if the move is fresh; the cut, if it is not. Do not stand still.

10. Desperate ground is the maximum loss, where only the width remains. On desperate ground, fight: cut the short leg, keep the wing, and let the wing fight for you. The seller who arrives on desperate ground with the short leg still open has already refused every earlier ground's advice.

11. The skilful tactician may be likened to the snake: strike its head and you will be attacked by its tail; strike its tail and you will be attacked by its head. The condor is the snake. Hurt one side and the other side is paid; run through one line and the wing beyond it becomes a long.

12. Throw your soldiers into positions whence there is no escape, and they will prefer death to flight. Set the stop in the temple, where there is no tape to argue with. Then, when the stop is reached, there is no decision to make, and a seller with no decision to make cannot make the wrong one.

13. It is the business of a general to be quiet and thus ensure secrecy; upright and just, and thus maintain order. It is the business of the seller to be quiet at the open, just to himself at the cut, and to keep order in the four quarters when the day turns four times.

14. At first, then, exhibit the coyness of a maiden, until the enemy gives you an opening; afterwards emulate the rapidity of a running hare, and it will be too late for the enemy to oppose you. Wait for the line to be offered at a price. Then cut, when cutting is due, faster than the tape can charge you for waiting.

## The Buyer

15. The buyer passes through the nine grounds too, and the one where he is most often lost is serious ground: deep in the trade, the catalyst not yet come, the option bleeding every day.

16. On serious ground the seller gathers plunder; the buyer pays it. Let him know the daily cost before he enters, so that the bleeding is a number and not a fear.

17. Difficult ground, for the buyer, is the move that starts and stalls. Keep steadily on the march only if the date has not passed.

18. Hemmed-in ground, for the buyer, is the event that came and failed. Resort to no stratagem. Sell what remains.

19. Desperate ground does not exist for the buyer. His maximum loss was paid on the first day. This is the buyer's one great advantage, and he loses it only by adding to a loser.

20. *Remove the ladder when the enemy has climbed* (第28计 上屋抽梯). Invite a man onto the roof, then take the ladder away. He goes forward or he leaps; retreat has been priced out.

21. The buyer builds his ladder before he climbs: a first tranche, then rungs at prices written in advance. The rungs are confirmation levels, not consolation levels. They are climbed when the thesis confirms, never because the price has fallen.

22. The whole ladder is measured against the maximum loss before the first step. An add that would breach the budget is a rung nailed outside the house.

23. Once on the roof, the ladder comes away. No add for boredom, no add for a headline, no add because the first tranche is red. Averaging down is not a ladder. It is a rope tied to the wrong beam, and it is the only road by which the buyer reaches desperate ground.

24. The enemy on the roof is not the market. It is the part of the trader that wants a second chance at a better price.

## Commentary

The nine situations are read here as the nine stages of a single trade, and the reading holds surprisingly well. The open is dispersive ground — the trader is at home, fully armed, and the danger is that he spends everything on the first opinion. The middle of a working trade is serious ground, where the only instruction is to let the plunder come in: theta pays a seller who is not wrong, hour by hour, and the discipline is simply to do nothing. Difficult ground is the fluctuation, which every position passes through before it resolves; the fourth quarter is the reserve that lets it be endured.

Hemmed-in and desperate ground are the two the seller must have already decided about. Hemmed-in is the broken line, and the answer is a stratagem chosen for the situation: a hedge into the other side if the move is young, a cut of the short leg if the move has already run. Desperate ground — the maximum loss — is where the old general says to fight, and the seller's fight is precise: buy back the short leg, keep the wing, and let the long option that the spread always contained ride the very move that broke the line. It is the difference between exiting a trade and letting the trade exit you.

"Throw your soldiers into positions whence there is no escape" is the least intuitive line in the chapter and the most important. The seller does not set his stop on the tape, where every price argues with it. He sets it in the temple, before the order, where it is an arithmetic and not a feeling. When the market reaches it, there is nothing to decide, and the absence of a decision is the whole of the discipline.

For the buyer the nine grounds lean the other way. Desperate ground, which the seller must have planned for, does not exist for him: the worst case was paid in full on the day he entered. His danger is the long middle — serious ground, where the option bleeds while the catalyst approaches, and difficult ground, where the move begins and stalls. The buyer who knows the daily rent in advance can endure both. The buyer who discovers it on the screen will either sell at the worst moment or buy more to lower his cost, and so build for himself the one ground he could never otherwise have reached.

The twenty-eighth stratagem is the buyer's answer to that danger, and it reads the general's "positions whence there is no escape" from the buyer's side. The old story removes the ladder so that the man on the roof commits to the route he chose. The buyer builds his ladder in the opposite order: it is placed before entry and taken away after. The first tranche is small because it is a step, not a verdict. The adds are written at confirmation levels, where the thesis has shown something, and they are funded from the original budget, not from a fresh one. That last clause is the roof. If the budget for the whole position is fixed before the first contract, every add draws against the same account, and a ladder that exceeds it is not commitment but a second, larger trade smuggled in under the first. The budget does not move because the first tranche is losing; that is precisely when a new rung feels most deserved.

So the two sides of the ticket arrive at the same discipline from opposite ends of the chapter. The seller sets his stop in the temple so that desperate ground holds no decision. The buyer pulls up his ladder so that desperate ground cannot be reached at all. Both have taken a choice away from the man on the tape, and both are safer for it.

### 12. Fire

## The Seller

1. There are five ways of attacking with fire. The first is to burn soldiers in their camp; the second is to burn stores; the third is to burn baggage trains; the fourth is to burn arsenals and magazines; the fifth is to hurl dropping fire amongst the enemy.

2. Volatility is fire. There are five ways the fire reaches the seller, and he must know each by its smoke.

3. The first burns the camp: the gap at the open, when the line set last night is already behind the price. There is no defence in the position; the defence was the size.

4. The second burns the stores: the slow bleed of a chopping market that crosses the line, retreats, and crosses again, charging a cut each time. This fire does not kill. It starves.

5. The third burns the baggage: the margin call, when a spread's marked loss exceeds the fourth quarter and the broker closes what the seller would have held. It is the fire the seller lights himself, by sizing for the outcome he hoped for.

6. The fourth burns the arsenal: the volatility spike that makes every strike expensive at once, so the hedge that was cheap in the morning costs half the credit in the afternoon. The seller who did not hedge when hedging was cheap has lost his arsenal to the same fire that made him need it.

7. The fifth is dropping fire: the news that arrives while the position is open, a headline in the middle of serious ground. Against this there is only the wing.

8. In order to carry out an attack with fire, the means must always be ready. In order to survive one, the means must be ready sooner: the stop set in the temple, the wing already bought, the fourth quarter untouched.

9. When fire breaks out inside the enemy's camp, respond at once with an attack from without. When volatility breaks out inside your position, respond at once: the hedge into reversal if the move is young, the cut of the short if it is not. Fire waits for no one, and the seller who waits to see if it spreads is the one it spreads through.

10. Beyond the five fires is the sixth, which burns every camp at once. Insurers do not go broke on ordinary claims. They go broke on catastrophes: the one event that sends every claim in the book on the same morning.

11. The market calls these events rare. It has delivered one every few years. On one Monday in October 1987 the index fell by a fifth in a single session. In February 2018 the market's fear gauge more than doubled in a day, and products built on its calm were closed for good. In February and March 2020 the index lost about a third in a few weeks.

12. A catastrophe is not a large ordinary day. It is a different kind of day: the gaps skip the stops, the quotes widen, the exits close, and every coast is hit together.

13. Run the storm in advance. Ask: if the index gaps five percent overnight, what does the book owe in the morning? If it falls ten percent in a week? Write the answers while the sun is shining. If the paper storm closes the account, the size is wrong today, not on the day it comes.

14. A stop is a plan for an ordinary day. In a catastrophe the market opens beyond it. Only the wing is guaranteed to be where you left it.

15. The worst storms come after the longest calm, because the calm is when everyone writes the most cover at the thinnest premium. Do not try to predict the catastrophe. Its date is unknowable. Its existence is certain.

16. Unhappy is the fate of one who tries to win his battles without cultivating the spirit of enterprise; for the result is waste of time and general stagnation. Unhappier is the seller who sells every day without cultivating the discipline of the stop; for the result is a hundred wages and one fire that takes them all.

17. Move not unless you see an advantage; use not your troops unless there is something to be gained; fight not unless the position is critical. Sell not unless the count says sell. Hedge not unless hurt. Cut not unless the line is broken — and then, cut.

18. No ruler should put troops into the field merely to gratify his own spleen; no general should fight a battle simply out of pique. No seller should sell because he is angry at the market for the last trade. Anger has never been a read.

19. A kingdom that has once been destroyed can never come again into being; nor can the dead ever be brought back to life. An account that has been halved needs a double to return, and one that has lost four-fifths needs five times itself. Every large loss does not merely subtract; it resets the base that everything afterward multiplies.

20. Hence the enlightened ruler is heedful, and the good general full of caution. This is the way to keep an account at peace and a sequence intact.

## The Buyer

21. Fire burns the seller and warms the buyer. Volatility is the buyer's ally, but only the volatility he paid for before it arrived.

22. The buyer who buys during the fire pays the fire's price. Options are dearest while the smoke is rising, and their value falls the moment it clears.

23. *Loot a burning house* (第5计 趁火打劫). The looter does not light the fire. He stands at the door and takes what the fire throws out. But he measures the fire first.

24. Divide the at-the-money straddle by the share price; that is the move the chain expects from the event. Set it against the last eight to twelve moves the stock actually made after the same event. Well above that history, with volatility high in its range, the fire is overpriced and the buyer is the one being looted. Well below it, with volatility near its lows, the event is being sold cheap. Between the two there is no trade, only a price.

25. There is a second fire, and it is not in the chain: bad news that has pushed a price below what the business can survive at. There the work is not volatility but solvency, and it is read in the filings. Two fires, two measures. If you cannot say which one you are looting, you are standing in the third, the one you lit yourself.

26. *Watch the fire from across the river* (第9计 隔岸观火). The house on the far bank is burning. The general does not cross to loot it. He lets the fire finish its argument, and counts what is left when the smoke clears.

27. A priced event is a settled argument. Watching is a position: flat has a profit and loss of zero and keeps the quiver full. "I have no view" is not the same as "there is no edge", and sometimes the edge is the refusal to pay for one.

28. Dropping fire, the news in the middle of the trade, is the buyer's one free gift. He may receive it; he must not pay for it in advance at full price.

29. The buyer's own fire is the crush: the event comes, moves the market a little, and leaves the option worth less than before. He was right about the direction and paid for a larger move than came.

30. The seller's catastrophe is the buyer's harvest, and the one fire the buyer may buy in advance at a low price, because it is the one the sellers have stopped charging for. Part V gives it a chapter.

## Commentary

The chapter on fire is the general's chapter on the weapon that does not care about skill, and for the seller that weapon is volatility. It arrives as a gap that puts the line behind the price before the first order can be placed; as a spike that makes the hedge unaffordable in the exact hour it becomes necessary; as a headline in the middle of a working trade. None of these can be predicted. All of them can be survived, and survival is arranged in advance: the size that makes a gap an ordinary expense, the wing that is always present because the spread always contains one, the reserve that keeps the broker's hand off the position.

The two fires the seller lights himself deserve naming. The margin call is size. The starving fire, the chop that charges a cut, then a re-entry, then a cut, is frequency: the chapter on manoeuvring returned as a slow burn. Neither is the market's fault, and both are ended by the same two rules that were already on the page: size so that the maximum loss is boring, and give the day a structural ceiling on how many times it can open.

The insurer's word for the sixth fire is catastrophe, and the industry learned it at great cost. When Hurricane Andrew struck Florida in 1992, the losses left several insurers insolvent. Most of them had priced their ordinary policies reasonably well. What they had not done was add up what the whole book would owe if one storm hit the whole coast in one night. The lesson the surviving industry took was not to forecast hurricanes better. It was to run the catastrophe in advance, on paper, and to refuse any size the paper storm could not survive. The option seller's catastrophes are part of the public record, and they come more often than the word *rare* suggests. In each of them, sellers who had sized for ordinary days met a day that was not ordinary, and the claims arrived together, at the open, through the stops. That is why this book insists on the wing. A stop is an instruction for a market that trades continuously; in a gap, the market opens past it and the fill comes wherever liquidity returns. The wing is not an instruction but a contract, and it is the one protection that is where you left it on the morning after. Its premium is the cost of making the catastrophe a number rather than a surprise.

The last verses are the arithmetic of ruin, stated by a general who had seen kingdoms end. Minus fifty needs plus one hundred. Minus eighty needs plus four hundred. This is not a warning about markets; it is what multiplication does to a sequence with a hole in it. It is also why the stop, the subject of nearly every chapter, is the only rule in the book with no exceptions. Every later chapter that needs this arithmetic points back here.

For the buyer, fire is the reason for the trade. A long option is a bet that volatility will arrive, and its value is a claim on the fire. But the claim must be bought before the smoke, at a price set by people who do not expect a fire. A buyer who pays during the spike buys a claim the market has already marked up, and the crush that follows the event, volatility falling as soon as the news is known, can take his profit even when the move went his way.

The two stratagems divide the buyer's fires into the one he may loot and the one he should watch. The looter's rule is a measurement that fits on the back of a card: the straddle divided by the price is the move the chain expects, and the stock's own history after the same kind of event is the move it has usually made. A wide gap either way is information; a narrow one is a fair price, and a fair price is not a trade, however strong the opinion. The second fire, the business sold below what it can be worth, is Mai's territory as Lewis describes it in *The Big Short*: no volatility screen finds it; it is read in the balance sheet. The watcher's rule is the default for everything else. In one count across thirty-eight rate decisions, the median move after the announcement was smaller than the move the day's straddle had priced, and the direction was close to a coin flip. The median is not the mean, and a few decisions produced moves far larger than their price, so the finding says that most such events are priced, not that all are. What survives is procedural. Watching costs nothing and keeps the right to act. Crossing the river costs the straddle and then the decay, and it must be justified by a mispricing written down before the event, with the number that would show it wrong.

So the same two rules hold for the buyer. Size so that losing the whole debit is boring, and give the day a ceiling on how many debits it can spend. The fire that ruins a buyer is not one catastrophe. It is the slow burn of paying, again and again, for fires that do not come.

### 13. Spies

## The Seller

1. What enables the wise sovereign and the good general to strike and conquer, and achieve things beyond the reach of ordinary men, is foreknowledge.

2. Foreknowledge cannot be elicited from spirits; it cannot be obtained inductively from experience, nor by any deductive calculation. Knowledge of the enemy's dispositions can only be obtained from other men.

3. Forecasts are spirits. The seller does not consult them. He consults the market's dispositions: what it has done, where it is leaning, what it has priced. These are obtained from the tape, not from the oracle.

4. There are five classes of spies: local spies, inward spies, converted spies, doomed spies, and surviving spies.

5. The seller has five spies, and he pays them differently.

6. Local spies are price itself: the pivots, the swings, the structure. They are cheap, they are everywhere, and they are honest. They tell you what the market has done and, by its shape, what it has decided.

7. Inward spies are volatility: the market's own officials, reporting what it fears. Implied volatility is the enemy's quartermaster telling you how much protection is being bought and at what price. Read him before every trade; he sets the wage.

8. Converted spies are positioning: the dealer's hedges, the walls of gamma, the levels where the enemy's own soldiers must buy or sell against their will. A converted spy works for the enemy and tells you where the enemy is forced to go. Of all five, he is the most valuable, and the one most traders never recruit.

9. Doomed spies are the news: information sent out to be believed, and to be acted upon by those who do not check it against the other four. Every headline is a doomed spy. Let him carry his message to the crowd.

10. Surviving spies are those who bring back news from the enemy's camp. The seller's surviving spy is his own journal: the record of every trade, the reason it was taken, the price at which it was cut or held, and what happened after. He is the only spy who has never lied, and he is the only one most traders refuse to keep.

11. Hence it is that with none in the whole army are more intimate relations maintained than with spies; none should be more liberally rewarded. Reward the journal with honesty. Write the trade down before it is over. Write the reason, not the outcome.

12. Spies cannot be usefully employed without a certain intuitive sagacity; they cannot be properly managed without benevolence and straightforwardness. Nor can a journal be usefully kept by a man who edits it to feel better.

13. The surviving spy's report fits on one line. An insurance company is judged by one number, the combined ratio: claims paid plus the costs of doing business, divided by the premium earned. Below one hundred, the company made money underwriting. Above one hundred, it paid its customers to be its customers.

14. The seller's costs are many and small: the commission on every fill, the spread between bid and ask, the slippage of a stop in a fast market. Each is a cent. Together they are often the whole margin. The cheapest cost to cut is the trade not made.

15. Keep the ratio monthly, from the journal, not from the account balance. The balance mixes luck with skill. The ratio separates premium from claims, and claims from costs.

16. A ratio below one hundred in a quiet month proves very little; the storm has not been counted yet. Judge it over a year that includes bad weather. A ratio above one hundred is not a reason to write more. It is a reason to stop and find which line is wrong: the price, the size, the selection, or the claims.

17. If one kind of policy carries the whole book's losses, stop writing that kind. Insurers drop lines of business every year; traders rarely drop a setup. The insurers are right.

18. Whether the object be to crush an army, to storm a city, or to assassinate an individual, it is always necessary to begin by finding out the names of the attendants and the sentries. Before the line is sold, find out the names: the expected move, the wall, the floor, the event, the volatility rank. These are the sentries. The trade that begins without them begins blind.

19. Hence it is only the enlightened ruler and the wise general who will use the highest intelligence of the army for purposes of spying, and thereby they achieve great results. Spies are a most important element in trading, because upon them depends the seller's ability to move at all.

20. The seller who has sold a thousand lines and written down none of them has fought a thousand battles and learned the lesson of one. The seller who has written down a hundred has a spy in every future camp.

## The Buyer

21. The buyer keeps the same five spies, and trusts the inward spy most.

22. Implied volatility tells the seller his wage and tells the buyer his price. A buyer who does not read it before every trade does not know whether he is buying a bargain or the seller's best day.

23. The converted spy tells the buyer where the dealers will be forced to chase: when the wall breaks, the enemy's own hedges push price in the buyer's direction.

24. *Let the enemy's spy sow discord* (第33计 反间计). A letter left on the desk for the spy to steal has done its work before it is read; the hand that kills is the enemy's own.

25. The option chain is the enemy's spy in the buyer's camp, reporting every day what others fear. The implied move is his price, skew is his accent, and the term structure is his calendar: next week's fear and next year's fear, priced apart.

26. An intercepted report is useful twice: for what it says, and for why someone wished it said. Stale data is a double agent. It answers with yesterday's map and charges today's price.

27. The deepest secret in the report is its shape: a smooth curve laid over an event that can only go two ways. Read where the spy's map is wrong, and let the enemy's own intelligence point the road.

28. The buyer's surviving spy is the journal too, and for every trade it holds the five sentences, written before entry and never after.

29. Without the journal the buyer remembers the one option that rose tenfold and forgets the forty that expired. With it, he can count whether the forty and the one together paid. That count is his combined ratio: the debits lost and the costs paid, set against what the few winners returned.

## Commentary

The general ends with spies because everything before, the count, the ground, the fire, depends on knowing, and knowing comes from people rather than from prayer. The seller's translation is that forecasts are the spirits the old text forbids consulting, and the market's dispositions are the men. Price tells him what has been decided. Volatility tells him what protection costs and therefore what he will be paid. Positioning tells him where the other side is forced to act against its will, which is the single most useful fact a seller can own about a line. The news tells the crowd what to think, and he lets it.

The surviving spy is the chapter's real subject. A journal that records the reason for every trade, at the time it was taken, is the only instrument that can tell a seller whether his losses came from bad ground, bad size, or the Commander's five faults. Without it he remembers his best trades and his worst, and nothing in between, and the pattern that is killing him is always in between. It must be written before the outcome is known, and it must be kept by a man willing to read it.

The insurer gives the surviving spy a single number to report. Take the premium collected on every spread over a month. Take the cost of every claim, the losses on positions that were tested and closed. Add every cost of trading: commissions, the bid-ask spread paid on entry and exit, the slippage of stops filled in fast markets. Divide claims plus costs by premium. If a month collected one thousand in premium, paid seven hundred in claims and one hundred and twenty in costs, the ratio is eighty-two: eighteen of every hundred collected stayed in the business. The costs are where most sellers quietly lose. A spread that collects a small credit and is opened, adjusted and closed several times can pay most of its premium away in tolls before the market has done anything at all. The costs never appear as one large loss, so they are rarely noticed, and the ratio is the only place they become visible. The cheapest improvement most books can make is not a better forecast but fewer round trips. And when every loss in the journal comes from the same setup, the same hour or the same event week, the seller has learned what an insurer would act on at once: drop the line.

For the buyer the inward spy matters most. Implied volatility is the price of the right he wants, and the whole of his edge, where it exists, is a gap between that price and the move that follows. The journal matters as much to him as to the seller, and for a sharper reason: a buyer's results are dominated by rare large wins, and memory keeps those and drops the long run of small losses that paid for them. Only a written count can say whether the one paid for the forty.

The thirty-third stratagem is the buyer's way of recruiting the seller's own spy. The stratagem is not bribery. It is making the other side's judgement do your work. The chain is a record of what other people paid to be afraid of, and its use is to show where that fear is priced too high or too low. Its deepest report is the one Mai read, as he described it to Schwager: a two-humped world, a court or a regulator that can only rule one way or the other, priced as one hump. The spy's map is wrong at its edges, and that is where it is read.

The last verse of the general's thirteen is the book's own sentence about itself. Thirteen chapters, in a general's order, on a single trade: count before you fight; know the cost of a campaign; win without needing to be right; be unbeatable before you look for a chance; divide your force in four; respond, do not predict; march slowly and cut fast; know the roads not to take; read the signs; refuse the wrong ground; know which of the nine situations you are in; survive fire; and keep the spy who never lies. None of it will make you right. It makes being wrong survivable, and survival is the only thing that compounds.

The buyer reads the same thirteen chapters and arrives at the mirror of the same sentence: say the five sentences before you pay; know the rent; fight only where direction and date are already decided; be small enough to lose many times; buy by a ladder, not a leap; buy what is cheap, not what is loud; march slowly and take profit fast; leave when the date passes; buy the coil, not the press; refuse the spike before the event; know that the middle of the trade is where the rent is paid; let fire come to you rather than pay for it in the blaze; and keep the journal that counts the forty losers beside the one winner.

Neither side is the right side. Each is a way of being wrong that can be survived, if it is counted. But the general did not end his career at Spies, and this book does not end here. Spies come before the choice of stance because a trader cannot choose between attack and defence until he has read the inward spy and knows what the fire costs today. Part V makes that choice.

## Part V: Attack and Defence 《攻守》

The general's thirteen chapters are done. They taught the seller to make himself unbeatable and the buyer to survive his own arithmetic. They did not answer the general's own question: when to defend, and when to attack. Seller and buyer are not two trades. They are the two banks of one river, and the option's price is the water between them. When the price of fire is high, the defender is paid to stand. When it is low, the attacker is paid to cross. The last five chapters are about reading the water.

In Part V the buyer speaks first, and the seller answers.

### 14. The Two Banks

## The Buyer

1. When an invading force crosses a river in its onward march, do not advance to meet it in mid-stream. It will be best to let half the army get across, and then deliver your attack.

2. Every option has two owners, and every premium is paid by one to the other. The seller stands on one bank and the buyer on the other, and the river between them is the price of fire.

3. The buyer's bank is low and wet. He pays first, he pays every day, and he is paid rarely. Let him know the ground he stands on before he calls it his.

4. Each side forages on the other. The seller lives on the buyer's need for protection and his love of direction. The buyer lives on the seller's comfort: the long calm after which protection is priced as if nothing will ever happen again.

5. The line of battle is not a strike. It is a number: the move the option charges for, set against the move the market goes on to deliver. Above the line, the defender is paid. Below it, the attacker is.

6. Implied volatility is the quartermaster's price for the march. Realised volatility is the road. When the quartermaster charges for a calmer road than the one the army is on, the attacker has his opening. When he charges for a rougher one, the attacker is paying the defender's wage.

7. Most days, in most names, the quartermaster charges for the rougher road. That gap is the seller's wage and therefore the buyer's tax. The buyer who forgets it crosses the river at the deepest point.

8. Measure the road before you cross it. The road is measured in daily moves, not in feelings about them. Square each day's move, take the average, take the root: that is the pace. Multiply it by the square root of the trading days in a year, about sixteen, and it is in the same units as the quartermaster's price.

9. Ten days are a sounding, not a survey. The road measured yesterday is the road behind the army, and the river can rise or fall the morning after the buyer pays. The measurement does not say he will be paid. It says whether he is paying the seller's wage or the seller is paying his.

10. Wait until half the army is across. The time to attack is not when the river is loudest but when the seller has committed to the calm, priced it, and grown comfortable on the far bank.

11. The buyer of a spread also sells. Every debit spread is a long option and a short one, and the short one is a small piece of the seller's trade bought into the buyer's ticket. He is never wholly on one bank.

12. A trader chooses a bank for a price, not for a career. "I am a buyer" is a sentence about identity. "Today the fire is cheap" is a sentence about the river, and only the second can be checked.

## The Seller

13. The seller reads the same river from the high bank. He is paid when the quartermaster's price is above the road, and he must check that it is before he collects.

14. The seller's spread is itself a short option and a long one. Every seller in this book has been a buyer since Chapter 4, through the wing. He stands on his bank with one foot in the water.

15. When the river runs below the line, when implied sits beneath what the market is delivering, the seller is not being paid to stand. He is paying to stand. On that day his bank is the low one, whatever he calls himself.

16. The defender who never measures the river defends by habit. Habit is not a stance. It is the absence of one, and the market charges it the attacker's price.

17. Let half the enemy across, and then strike: the seller's version is to let the panic buyers cross first, paying the spike, and to write his cover after they have, at the price they set.

18. Two banks, one river, one price. A trader who knows which bank he is on today, and why, has already done most of the general's work.

## Commentary

The general's thirteen chapters are done. They taught the seller to make himself unbeatable and the buyer to survive his own arithmetic. They did not answer the general's own question, the one he set in the chapter on the unbeatable position: when to defend, and when to attack. Part V answers it. Seller and buyer are not two trades. They are the two banks of one river, and the option's price is the water between them. When the price of fire is high, the defender is paid to stand. When it is low, the attacker is paid to cross. The last five chapters are about reading the water, and in them the buyer speaks first, reversing the order of the thirteen, so that the structure of the book makes its argument.

Every option is a transfer. Before costs, what one side gains on a contract the other loses, and both pay the house for the privilege: the spread, the commission, the slippage. So the question "is buying or selling better" has no answer in general. It has an answer only at a price. At most prices, on most days, the answer has been the seller, because buyers of protection have usually paid more than the moves they were protected against turned out to cost. That gap is a habit of the people who need insurance, not a law. When the habit breaks, after a long calm has lulled the sellers into pricing tomorrow as if it were yesterday, the answer turns.

The line of battle can be checked with a pencil. Take ten days of a stock near 100 whose daily moves, in percent, were: +1.2, −0.8, +1.5, −1.9, +0.4, −1.1, +2.0, −0.6, +1.3, −1.0. Square each one: 1.44, 0.64, 2.25, 3.61, 0.16, 1.21, 4.00, 0.36, 1.69, 1.00. They add up to 16.36. The average is about 1.64, and its square root is about 1.28. That is the pace of the road: the stock has been moving about 1.28% on a typical day. Multiply by the square root of 252 trading days, which is just under 16, and the pace is about 20% a year. That is realised volatility, the road.

Now read the quartermaster. Suppose the options on the same stock, a month out, are priced at an implied volatility of 16%. Divide by 16 and the option is charging for a daily move of about 1%. The road has been delivering 1.28%. Over twenty trading days the difference compounds into the price of a straddle: the square root of 20 over 252 is about 0.28, so the move the option charges for is about 100 × 0.16 × 0.28, or 4.5, and the at-the-money straddle costs roughly 0.8 of that, about 3.6. At the road's pace of 20%, the same arithmetic gives a typical move of about 5.7 and a straddle worth about 4.6. The quartermaster is charging for a road about a fifth calmer than the one the army is on. On this river, today, the buyer is on the high bank.

Three honest limits belong in the same breath. First, ten days is a sounding. A real comparison uses more days, and more than one window, and asks whether the last ten were ordinary for this name or a storm that has already passed. Second, the road is the past. Realised volatility is a spy who reports what has happened, and a market moving fast can go quiet the morning after the buyer pays. Third, the comparison is between like and like: an option that spans an earnings date is charging for that date, and a road measured on ordinary days will make it look dear when it is only accurate. None of this makes the measurement useless. It makes it a measurement rather than a prophecy, which is the only kind of edge this book recognises.

Reverse the numbers and the same pencil gives the seller's wage. If the stock has been moving at 12% a year and the option charges 16%, the quartermaster is charging for a road a third rougher than the one being marched. That is the ordinary state of the river, and it is why the seller's half of this book was written first. The buyer who pays the average price, on average, pays it.

"Let half the army get across" is the general's advice on timing at a river, and it reads well from both banks. The attacker does not cross when the water is loudest, during the spike, when everyone who is late is bidding for the same right. He crosses when the seller has committed himself to the calm and is standing comfortable on the far bank, half his army over. The defender has his own version. He lets the panic buyers cross first, paying the spike, and writes his cover after they have, at the price their fear set.

The last verses end the false choice that the first edition left open. A trader who says "I am a seller" or "I am a buyer" has described himself, not the river. Every spread in this book is both: the seller's credit spread is a short option with a long wing behind it, and the buyer's debit spread is a long option that sells its far wing to pay for itself. The question is never which bank to be born on. It is which bank the price puts you on today, and whether you have measured it. The next four chapters take the four answers in turn: when to attack, why most attacks fail, how to buy for defence, and when to change sides.

### 15. When to Attack

## The Buyer

1. Security against defeat implies defensive tactics; ability to defeat the enemy means taking the offensive.

2. The seller has read the first half of that sentence for thirteen chapters. The buyer holds the second. Neither half is a whole army.

3. Standing on the defensive indicates insufficient strength; attacking, a superabundance of strength. The buyer's superabundance is not capital. It is price: an attack is strong only when the right to make it has been sold to him too cheaply.

4. There are five grounds on which the buyer may attack. Outside them, he is not an attacker. He is a donor.

5. The first ground is cheap fire: the option whose implied volatility sits below what the market has been delivering and the calendar is about to deliver. He does not buy movement. He buys movement for less than it costs.

6. The second ground is the dated door: a catalyst with a day on it, bought before the crowd has noticed the day, while the rent is still the rent of an ordinary week.

7. The third ground is the long night: the move the sellers price as impossible because they have not seen it for years. The far strike, cheap enough to be lost a hundred times, held for the one night that does not stay dark.

8. The fourth ground is the shield: the put bought behind a book of stock, the wing bought behind a book of short premium. Here he does not attack the market. He attacks his own ruin, and the price of that attack is a known, small, regular expense.

9. The fifth ground is the gap-proof ticket. When the enemy's fire is likely but its direction is not, the debit is the only position whose worst morning has already been paid for.

10. Rapidity is the essence of war: take advantage of the enemy's unreadiness, make your way by unexpected routes, and attack unguarded spots.

11. The seller's unreadiness is his comfort. After a long calm he prices tomorrow as if it were yesterday. That is the unguarded spot, and there is no other.

12. To see victory only when it is within the ken of the common herd is not the acme of excellence. The option the whole market is shouting about has already been priced by the shouting.

13. Do not swallow bait offered by the enemy. A large move on the screen is bait: it invites the buyer to pay for the next move at the price of the last one.

14. Measure before you attack. Read the inward spy, implied volatility, and set him beside the local spy: what price has actually travelled. If the quartermaster charges for a calmer road than the one the army is marching, attack. If he charges for a rougher one, stand aside.

15. *Toss a brick to attract jade* (第17计 抛砖引玉). A candidate is a brick. Thousands come in; the gates stand in order, liquidity, a dated catalyst, volatility, structure, risk, edge; five to ten come out at most, and some weeks none. The discarding is the work.

16. *Lure the tiger down the mountain* (第15计 调虎离山). An illiquid strike is the tiger's mountain, and the buyer who climbs to it pays the mountain's price. Open interest of some hundreds at the strike, real volume on the day, and a bid-ask that is a small fraction of the price: below lines like these, the ground belongs to the tiger. A strike that cannot be sold has not been bought.

17. *Borrow a corpse to return the soul* (第14计 借尸还魂). An old screen is a corpse that still walks, in the direction of the last data it was fed. Ask of every input when it was last measured, and whether a fresh count would move the ranking.

18. *To catch bandits, catch their king* (第18计 擒贼擒王). Fifteen positions are fifteen bandits, each small, each loud, none of them the king. One tested setup is the king; one or two independent theses are his court. Put the risk where the evidence is.

19. Choose the shape of the attack by the ground. When the size of the move is unknown, the single option. When its size is roughly known, the debit spread, which sells the far wing to pay for the near one. When only the date is known, buy both doors and let the event choose.

20. Buy no more time than the catalyst needs, and no less. Too little and the fuse is cut short. Too much and he pays rent on months his reason does not need.

21. Size the attack as a raid, not an invasion: each debit small enough to be lost whole, many times in a row, without changing the size of the next.

22. Before paying, say the five sentences, each in one breath.

23. The edge: *what is mispriced, why that can be measured, and what must happen after costs.* If he cannot name the edge as a price, he has no edge.

24. The catalyst: *this event, on this date, reprices the stock by this mechanism.* A catalyst without a date is a rumour, and a date without a mechanism is a coin.

25. The maximum loss: *my worst case is this many dollars, because the debit is this.* Spoken as a number, never as "not much".

26. The time stop: *I leave on this date if the thesis has not confirmed, regardless of price.* Chosen from the catalyst, never from comfort.

27. The breakeven win rate: *the debit divided by what the ticket pays when right; I must be right this often to break even.* The number he must beat, said before he pays.

28. When the attack succeeds, divide the spoils at once. When the date passes and it has not succeeded, leave at once. Between those two, do nothing.

29. He wins his battles by making no mistakes. The buyer's mistakes are four, and none of them is about direction: paying the spike, buying without a date, sizing as if the debit could not go to zero, and rolling a reason that has died.

## The Seller

30. The seller reads this chapter as a list of the days he must not be found on the field.

31. When his wage falls below the fire the market is delivering, he is not being paid to hold the line. He is paying to hold it. On that ground the defender should become the attacker, or leave.

32. Before a dated door, the seller who sold the quiet week sold it to the buyer who arrived early. Let him know who stands on the other side of a cheap ticket.

33. The long night is the seller's catastrophe and the buyer's harvest. Every seller who keeps a wing has bought a little of the night. Every seller who does not has sold all of it.

34. The binary event is the seller's blind spot. Where a company has two futures, the smooth curve he sells across is wrong at both ends, and the buyer who sees the two humps is buying the end of the curve the seller priced as empty.

35. Thus the skilful general is both: defended while the wage is fair, attacking when it is not, and never both on the same direction at once.

## Commentary

The general's chapter on the unbeatable position is usually read for its first half: make yourself invincible, then wait. Thirteen chapters of this book have read it that way, and for the seller that reading is right. But the general did not stop there. In the same chapter he wrote that security against defeat lies in defence, that the ability to defeat the enemy lies in attack, and that the commander skilled in attack "flashes forth from the topmost heights of heaven." He did not despise attack. He despised attack without advantage. The buyer's whole case rests on that word, and in this trade "advantage" has an exact meaning: a price.

That is why the first chapter asked the buyer for the edge before anything else, and why this chapter refuses every other kind. Conviction is not an edge; the seller on the other side of the ticket also has conviction, and he has been paid for his. A strong story is not an edge; the market has heard it. An edge, for the buyer, is a gap between what an option charges for and what is likely to come. It is either cheap fire, a dated event the market has not yet priced, or a tail the sellers have stopped charging for. Everything else in the chapter follows from refusing to attack anywhere the edge cannot be stated as a number.

**Cheap fire** can be checked on paper, as Chapter 14 showed with ten days of moves. A straddle at the money (the call and the put together) costs roughly 0.8 × price × implied volatility × the square root of the time left, in years. Take a stock at 100, implied volatility of 20% a year, and thirty days to expiry. The square root of thirty days over 365 is about 0.29. The straddle costs about 0.8 × 100 × 0.20 × 0.29, or 4.6. That is the size of move, up or down, the market is charging for. Now suppose the stock has actually been moving at 30% a year for the past month. The same formula, with 0.30 in place of 0.20, gives a typical move of about 6.9. The quartermaster is charging for a road a third calmer than the one the army is on.

That gap is the buyer's opening. It is not a promise. Realised volatility is a spy who reports the past, and a market that has been moving fast can go quiet the morning after the buyer pays. The honest point is narrower. On this ground the buyer is not paying the seller's wage; the seller is paying the buyer's. Most of the time, in most names, the gap runs the other way: implied above what is later delivered. That is the wage the seller has collected for thirteen chapters, and it is why most buyers who buy at the average price lose on average. Cheap fire is the exception, and the buyer's art is to wait for it.

**The dated door** works on time rather than on level. As a scheduled event approaches, the options that cover it grow more expensive, because more of the uncertainty in their remaining life is concentrated in a single day. The buyer who arrives early pays the rent of an ordinary week for a ticket that includes the event. If the event moves the market, he is paid. If the price of fear rises as the date approaches, he can also be paid without the move, by selling the ticket into the spike before the news, as Chapter 10 advised. The buyer who arrives late pays for the fear in full and then watches the crush take it away the minute the event is known. The two bought the same right. Only the date of purchase differed, and the date decided which of them was the seller's provisions.

**The long night** is convexity, and it is the ground most often misread. A put struck 20% below the price, on a stock at 100, may cost a tenth of a point when the market has been calm for a long time. If the stock falls to 70 before expiry, that put is worth 10, a hundred times what was paid. The breakeven win rate is one in a hundred. That is the whole of the arithmetic, and the arithmetic does not say that such a fall will come. It says what the ticket costs and what it pays. Bought every month for a year, the same ticket costs 1.20 for every 100 of stock, and in most years every one of the twelve expires worthless.

So the long night is not a raid. It is a garrison, and it must be funded for the length of the night, not for the brightness of the morning that ends it. Two honest limits belong in the same breath. First, the tail is not cheap everywhere. Puts on the broad index are, most of the time, the most expensive options on the board, because every institution that must protect a portfolio buys the same insurance. The tail is cheap only where the sellers have stopped charging for it, usually in a single name or a quiet corner after a long calm. Second, the buyer who cannot hold the garrison through a long run of expiries is worse off than one who never began, because he will abandon it in the year before it pays.

**The shield** is buying in defence, and Chapter 17 gives it a full reading. Here it is enough to say why it belongs among the grounds of attack. Insurance loses money on average by design, as it does on a house. The buyer of a shield is not attacking the market. He is attacking the one enemy that ends accounts: the large loss that resets the base, as Chapter 12 counted it. The seller should recognise this ground at once. His defined-risk spread is a short option and a long one, and the long one is a shield. He has been a buyer since Chapter 4.

**The gap-proof ticket** is the quietest ground. There are days when the fire is likely and its direction is not: a decision pending, a market coiled in a range narrowing toward a date. On such days the seller's spread is capped, but it can reach its cap overnight, in a single gap, with no hour in which to hedge. The buyer's debit cannot be enlarged by any open. If both doors are bought, he has paid in advance for not knowing which one will open. That is not an edge by itself; the straddle is priced, and the price may be fair. But when it is fair, the buyer has the better shape, because his worst morning was settled before the night began.

**The shape of the attack** follows from what is known:

- **Single option:** when only the direction is known, because its payoff grows with the move.

- **Debit spread:** when the size of the move can be roughly estimated. It sells the far strike, the part of the move he does not expect, to pay for the near one. That lowers the debit, and so the win rate he needs: pay 2 for a spread 10 wide, and he must be right one time in five, not one in ten. It gives up the long night in exchange.

- **Straddle:** when only the date is known.

No shape is an edge. Each is a way of matching what he pays for to what he knows, so that he is not charged for a guess he never made.

**The funnel** is where the five grounds are found, and the third set of the Thirty-Six Stratagems is its machinery. It begins with everything the calendar and the chain can offer and ends with a short list, through six gates applied in a fixed order so that the same candidate meets the same questions every week. Liquidity comes first, because a candidate that cannot be traded cannot be evaluated: the tiger's mountain charges its toll on the way in and again on the way out, and it charges it whether the thesis is right or wrong. The dated catalyst comes next, because a buyer of options is paying for time and needs to know what the time is for. Volatility, structure and risk narrow the expression. Edge is last, on purpose: it is the gate most easily fooled by a good story, and it is applied only to candidates that have already passed the cheaper tests. Fresh data is a condition of every gate. One short-interest screen ranked names at the top with a short ratio above 100%, because the share count in its denominator came from filings years old; recomputed with fresh counts, those names left the top three. The arithmetic was right and the body was dead. And at the end of the funnel stands one king, not fifteen bandits: one tested setup that can say its five sentences, and a court of one or two independent theses. Five call spreads in one sector are one thesis wearing five tickers.

**The five sentences** are said here in full, once, because this is the chapter in which the buyer pays. Each closes a door the hope trade would otherwise walk through. The edge closes the door of direction dressed as mispricing. The catalyst closes the door of the dateless thesis. The maximum loss closes the door of the size chosen by appetite. The time stop closes the door of the stale reason, and it is the one most often left open, because it costs nothing to promise and something to keep. The breakeven win rate closes the door of the pretty payoff: a ticket that pays ten to one needs one win in eleven, and a buyer who has never been right one time in eleven on that kind of trade has not found an edge, only a picture of one.

**The second voice** belongs in this chapter, because Jamie Mai is the clearest modern example of a buyer who attacked only on these grounds, and the clearest warning about copying him. As Lewis tells it in *The Big Short*, Mai and his partners began with a small account and a single observation: the options market priced the long future of a company as a smooth spread of outcomes, even when that future hung on one event that could only go two ways. In Lewis's telling, one of their first trades was in a lender whose fate depended on a regulator; the long-dated calls were priced as if the stock would drift, when in fact it would either recover or not. That is the binary knife of Chapter 3 and the dated door of this one. Later they bought insurance on the higher-rated slices of mortgage bonds, which the sellers of that insurance priced as if they could not fail. That is the long night. In his interview with Schwager in *Hedge Fund Market Wizards*, Mai describes the method in terms this book would recognise: risk a little to make a lot, expect most bets to lose, and buy the cheapest instrument that captures the outcome, which is sometimes not an option at all.

**The test that failed** is the counterweight, and it must be read beside him. A pre-registered study took the part of Mai's method that a screen can see, a stock whose realised volatility sat at a three-year low, and bought twelve-month calls struck 30% out of the money whenever that condition held. The rule, the exits and the measure of failure were written down before the first run, and nothing was changed after. Over 2020 to 2025 it made 580 trades. At a realistic price, about 58 cents came back for every dollar of premium paid, and about 88% of the calls expired worthless. The same calls bought on ordinary stocks did about the same, so the quiet added nothing. The test did not fail because quiet is bad. It failed because quiet was only Mai's entry condition, the place he looked. His edge was the judgement of a particular event: which court, which regulator, which balance sheet, and why the two humps were priced as one. A screen can find quiet. It cannot borrow the eye that knows which quiet is load-bearing. That clause is the whole finding, and it is why this chapter asks for the edge as a price, with a reason, every time, and never accepts a condition in its place.

The verses on **bait and the common herd** are the general's warning against the most common buy of all: the option bought after the move, on the day the move is on every screen. It feels like the safest attack, because the direction has just been shown. It is the most expensive, because everyone who saw the same screen is bidding for the same right, and the seller has raised the price of fire to match. To see victory only when the herd can see it is to pay the herd's price for it.

Last, **size and the exit**, which decide whether any of the five grounds can be held. A raid sized so that it can be lost whole many times in a row is the only kind a buyer can repeat, and Chapter 16 counts how many times in a row he should expect to lose it. The exit is the same discipline from the other end. When the attack succeeds, the option is worth mostly fire and time, and both leave quickly, so he takes part of it at once. When the date passes and the attack has not succeeded, the reason is gone, so the option is gone.

The seller's half of this chapter is short because his lesson is short. Every ground on which the buyer may attack is a day on which the seller is paid less than his risk: cheap fire, the early ticket on a dated door, the tail priced as impossible, the binary event priced as a smooth curve. On those days the defender can do three things. He can buy the wing he should have kept. He can stand aside. Or, if he has the discipline of both halves, he can cross the river and attack. He must not do two of these on the same direction at once: Chapter 7's rule still holds. A long call beside a short put on the same name is one bet in two costumes, whatever the trader calls himself.

The general wrote that the good fighter can secure himself against defeat but cannot make certain of defeating the enemy. That is the honest limit of this chapter. The five grounds do not make the buyer right. They make sure that when he is wrong, as he will be most of the time, he was wrong at a fair price, at a size he could repeat, on a ticket whose worst day he had already paid for. And when the seller has priced the river too calm, he is standing on the far bank when it floods.

### 16. The Attacker's Tax

## The Buyer

1. Poverty of the state exchequer causes an army to be maintained by contributions from a distance. Contributing to maintain an army at a distance causes the people to be impoverished.

2. The attacker's army is maintained at a distance: he pays now for a battle that comes later, if it comes. Every day of the wait is a contribution, and the contributions impoverish him whether or not the battle is won.

3. Most attacks fail. Not because buyers are foolish, but because a tax is levied on the attack before it begins, and most attackers never count it.

4. The tax has a name. The variance premium is the seller's wage, and therefore the buyer's tax: on most days and in most names, the option charges for more fire than comes.

5. It is paid in four ways, and a fifth that pays twice.

6. The first is rent. The option loses a little of what is left every day the move does not come. At a twentieth a day, half is gone in two weeks.

7. The second is paying the spike. The buyer who arrives when fear is loudest pays the fear's price, and the crush after the news takes it back even when he was right about the direction.

8. The third is the late or the dateless entry: the option bought after the move, when the herd has priced it, or bought with no date at all, so that rent is paid on a reason with no day to arrive.

9. The fourth is the oversized debit. A low win rate means long losing runs. The buyer sized for a few attempts is beaten before his door opens.

10. The fifth is the roll: the expired call replaced with next month's, at a fresh debit, on the same reason. It pays rent twice on one thesis, and the second payment is made on a reason that has already been tested and failed.

11. Each tax has a discipline against it. Against rent, the time stop and no more time than the catalyst needs. Against the spike, buying before the fear, or the spread that hands part of it back. Against the late entry, the dated door and the refusal of bait. Against size, a debit small enough to lose many times in a row. Against the roll, the rule that the reason ends the trade.

12. None of the disciplines makes buying profitable. They remove the ways of losing that have nothing to do with the market.

13. *The beauty trap* (第31计 美人计). A beauty is not found; she is prepared: the dress, the lamp, the angle of the face. So is a backtest of long options, whose few enormous winners make a decade look like a method.

14. Ask in how many rooms she has been seen. Fix the rule on one stretch of history, judge it on another, and keep a third untouched until the last day. Prefer the curve with a scar in it. A method that never lost in the sample was not tested; it was arranged.

15. *Inflict injury on oneself to win trust* (第34计 苦肉计). The stop is the buyer's wound, taken by his own hand before the enemy can take it for him. A small loss paid at the stated price buys what the market does not sell: the right to remain in the war.

16. After thirty to fifty trades, set the hit rate against the breakeven win rate. If the setup is not above that line by a comfortable margin, after costs, it is killed. The kill rule is written while the setup is still beautiful. Written afterwards, it is a eulogy.

17. *The empty fort* (第32计 空城计). In a losing run the account has nothing to show. Show nothing: fewer trades, smaller, slower. The position not taken cannot lose, needs no stop, and pays no rent.

18. *If all else fails, retreat* (第36计 走为上). When "I have to make it back" speaks, the defeat is no longer on the field. It is in the man. Revenge is a position with no stop and no catalyst. Retreat to paper, or to a size too small to hurt, until the trades can be read without shame.

19. The losing run is not bad luck. It is the arithmetic of the win rate, and the buyer who has not sized for it has not been unlucky. He has been counted.

## The Seller

20. The seller reads this chapter as the source of his wage.

21. Every tax the buyer pays is collected by someone, and on most days the someone is the seller. Rent is his wage. The spike is his best day to write. The late buyer is his favourite customer.

22. Let him also read it as the reason the wage is small. The tax is spread across many buyers, and many sellers compete to collect it. What is left after the competition is a thin margin, and the seller pays it back, in the storm, to the few buyers who were early.

23. The seller pays taxes too: the costs of every round trip, the claims that cluster, the catastrophe that comes after the longest calm. His combined ratio is the attacker's tax, less his own.

24. A seller who sees the buyer's tax and concludes that selling cannot lose has read half the ledger. The buyers pay a little most days. The seller pays a lot on a few. Both columns are real.

## Commentary

The general's chapter on the cost of war is about an army maintained at a distance, and the buyer's army is always at a distance. He pays now, in full, for a battle that may come in a week, a month, or not at all. That is the attacker's position by construction, and it carries a tax. The variance premium, the habit of option buyers paying more for protection than the moves turn out to cost, is the seller's wage when read from his bank. Read from the buyer's, it is a levy on every attack, collected before the first shot.

The four taxes were spread across the thirteen chapters of this book, one or two at a time: rent in Chapter 2, the spike in Chapters 10 and 12, the late entry in Chapters 6 and 7, size in Chapter 4. Set out together they are one argument, and the argument explains why most buyers lose without any of them being wrong about the market. A buyer can be right about direction and lose to rent, because the move came after his option expired. He can be right about direction and timing and lose to the spike, because he paid for a bigger move than came. He can be right about everything and lose to size, because the run of losses before his winner took the account below the size at which the winner could repair it. None of these is a forecasting error. Each is a tax paid without counting it.

The same five failures run through the plainer books on buying that sit on the same shelf as this one: leverage taken for courage, the right idea at the wrong time, volatility that moved against the option even when price moved for it, structures so clever that one leg undid another, and markets too thin to leave. Each is a way of losing that has nothing to do with being wrong about the stock. The last two deserve a word here. A shape is too clever when the buyer cannot say, in one breath, what each leg is for; a calendar spread bought for direction or a ratio bought for protection is a tax disguised as a trade. And a market too thin to leave taxes the buyer twice, on the way in and on the way out, at the moment he most needs to move. The tiger's mountain of Chapter 15 is that tax, and it is levied whether the thesis is right or wrong.

The roll gets its own verse because it is the tax the buyer levies on himself. The option has expired, the reason has been tested and has failed, and the buyer buys next month's at a fresh debit because the story still sounds good. It is not patience. It is paying rent twice on one house, and the second rent is paid on a house that has already burned. The rule that answers it was set in Chapter 8: when the reason is gone, the option is gone.

The losing run is the arithmetic that decides whether a buyer can survive any of this, and it belongs here, said once. Suppose a buyer's tickets pay five times their debit when right, so that he breaks even at one win in five. At that hit rate, the chance that any particular string of ten trades is all losses is 0.8 multiplied by itself ten times, which is just over a tenth. Across a hundred attempts, the chance that a run of ten straight losses turns up somewhere is better than nine in ten. It is not a disaster scenario. It is what one in five looks like. Now size it. At two percent of the account per debit, ten total losses leave about 82% of the account. At ten percent per debit, they leave about 35%, and the winner that follows is measured against that smaller base. Chapter 12 gave the rest: minus sixty-five needs nearly plus two hundred. A buyer sized at ten percent who meets the ordinary run has not been unlucky. He has been counted.

The expectancy that sits behind all of this is one line: the hit rate times the average win, minus the miss rate times the average loss, minus costs. The hit rate in that line is estimated from the buyer's own sample, which is small and uncertain, and that is why the four stratagems of the last set belong in this chapter. The beauty is the backtest that answers a narrower question than the one asked: in-sample, the data have already been seen by the person choosing the filters, so the fit is a record of the seeing. For a buyer the temptation is sharper, because a handful of enormous winners can make a decade of long calls look like a method. In one options pilot, a single spread returned 148% while the median trade lost 94%. Shown the winner alone, anyone would call the method promising. Shown the median, anyone would call it broken. Both numbers were true, and the test of the method is not whether most trades won but whether the few winners paid for all the losers, after costs, in a sample large enough to trust.

The self-inflicted wound is the stop and the kill rule together. The first injury is the stop, taken at the stated price, which keeps the account's word intact. The second is the count, after thirty to fifty trades, of the hit rate against the breakeven win rate. On a debit spread the breakeven is the debit divided by the width, so the comparison needs no opinion, only room for costs; a hit rate that only just clears the line does not clear it. The third injury is the kill. A setup traded for a year becomes part of the trader's idea of himself, and killing it feels like admitting the year was wasted, so it is granted one more trade. The rule is written at the beginning, while the setup is still a hypothesis and not an identity.

The empty fort and the retreat are the two answers to a losing run that is already under way. The empty fort is not a trick that works because the enemy is stupid. It works because the fort really is empty. When the account has no edge that meets the standard, the honest posture is no position, and the small position taken to look busy is the pretended garrison, found out at the next open. Retreat is the last of the thirty-six and the first in importance. In a drawdown the danger is not the percentage lost; it is the hurry that arrives with it, which makes positions larger, horizons shorter and stops looser all at once. There are two retreats, to paper and to minimum size, and neither leaves the market. Both leave the self that took over.

The seller reads this chapter twice. First as the source of his wage: every tax the buyer pays is collected, and on most days he collects it. Then as the reason his wage is small. The tax is spread across a crowd of buyers and competed for by a crowd of sellers, and what reaches any one seller is a thin margin, which he hands back, in the storm, to the few buyers who paid their tax early and held their tickets through the calm. Both columns of the ledger are real. The buyers pay a little most days; the seller pays a lot on a few. Neither side has found a way of being paid for nothing, and neither will.

### 17. The Wing in the Baggage Train

## The Buyer

1. An army without its baggage train is lost; without provisions it is lost; without bases of supply it is lost.

2. Not every option is bought to attack. Some are bought to make sure the army is still there to attack tomorrow. These are carried in the baggage train, and they are paid for like provisions: every day, without expecting a battle.

3. The art of war teaches us to rely not on the likelihood of the enemy's not coming, but on our own readiness to receive him; not on the chance of his not attacking, but on the fact that we have made our position unassailable.

4. There are three wings in the baggage train. The put behind a book of stock. The wing behind a book of short premium. And the far tail, bought as fire insurance for the whole account.

5. Insurance loses money on average, by design. The house that does not burn has paid its premium for nothing, every year but one. Say this plainly before buying, or the first calm year will talk you out of the policy.

6. The insurance does not buy a profit. It buys the survival of the sequence. Chapter 12 counted the hole: a large loss resets the base that everything afterward multiplies. A small, regular expense that removes the large loss pays for the sequence, not for the trade.

7. Carry as much insurance as turns your worst morning into a bad one, and no more. Cover larger than the book it protects is not insurance. It is a new position, and it must be counted as an attack.

8. Insurance is cheap where the sellers have forgotten the tail: a single name after a long calm, a corner nobody hedges. It is dear where every institution buys the same put on the same index. Buy where it is cheap if the risk is there; pay the dear price only for the risk you cannot carry.

9. Buy it in the calm. Insurance bought after the storm is expensive, and it is bought at the moment it feels most necessary, which is the moment it is priced to be least worth having.

10. Judge the insurance on the sequence, not on its own ledger line. Its cost appears every month. Its value appears once in ten years.

11. *The golden cicada sheds its shell* (第21计 金蝉脱壳). The cicada does not die when it leaves the shell. The husk stays on the branch, whole and empty, and the bird strikes it.

12. A position carried into expiry is that husk. Leave before the last days, not on them. Know what settles and how: broad index options settle in cash, some at the morning print and some at the close; options on a fund or a stock are claims on shares, and shares can be assigned.

13. A wing that expires is a wing that has stopped guarding. Roll the insurance on a schedule written in the calm, not on the morning the house is already warm.

## The Seller

14. Every serious insurer buys insurance for itself. It is called reinsurance, and it is the reason the company is still writing after the storm.

15. In a credit spread the long wing is reinsurance bought at the moment the policy is written: part of the premium is handed on, and the largest claim is capped. The seller has been a buyer since Chapter 4.

16. A wing far away is cheap reinsurance with a high attachment point. A wing near the short strike is expensive reinsurance that caps the claim sooner. Choose the cover that keeps the worst claim inside the reserve, not the cover that looks cheapest.

17. When a short option has fallen to a few cents, buy it back. The few cents are the cheapest reinsurance in the market: they close a policy that can still be claimed for its whole limit. Do not hold a nearly expired policy to collect the last penny; the last penny is paid for by the whole limit.

18. A small, permanent tail cover for the whole book, a little protection far below the market, is the insurer's catastrophe treaty. It loses money most years. It is meant to.

19. The seller who refuses to pay for his own cover keeps a little more premium every year, and gives all of it back in one.

20. Cede enough to sleep, and keep enough to earn.

21. Leave a wing in every spread. That was the seller's rule in Chapter 7. It is also a buyer's rule, and now the seller knows which one he was following.

## Commentary

Part V has so far been about buying to attack. This chapter is about buying in defence, which is the oldest and most respectable reason anyone ever bought an option. A farmer bought the right to sell his harvest at a fixed price long before anyone bought a call to bet on a stock. The baggage train is the image the general gives for it: the provisions that do not fight, that slow the march, that cost every day, and without which the army is lost.

The arithmetic is the reason to carry them, and it can be done on paper. Take a book of stock worth 100. Suppose a put struck at 90, three months out, costs 1. Carried for a year, rolled each quarter, the insurance costs about 4, and in a calm year that 4 is simply gone. Now suppose the year is not calm, and the book falls to 60. Without the put, the loss is 40, and Chapter 12's arithmetic says the book needs to rise by two-thirds to return. With the put, the loss is the 10 down to the strike plus the 1 paid for that quarter's put, about 11, and the book needs to rise by about an eighth. The insurance did not make money in that year either. It turned a hole that takes years to fill into one that takes months, and that is the whole of what it is for. The numbers are an example, not a price list. Real puts cost what the market says on the day, and the market charges most for the insurance everyone wants.

That is why where the insurance is bought matters as much as whether. Puts on the broad index are usually the dearest options on the board: every pension fund, every manager with a mandate, every dealer short the same risk is buying the same protection, and the sellers charge accordingly. The skew, the extra price charged for the downside strikes, is the sign of that crowd. Insurance is cheap in the places the crowd is not: in a single name where nobody hedges, after a long calm in which the sellers have stopped charging for the tail. Mark Spitznagel's *Safe Haven* (2021) makes a related argument at the scale of a whole portfolio: that insurance should be judged by what it does to the compound rate of the whole, not by its own line in the ledger. That is the same lesson as verse 10, told across decades rather than seasons.

How much to carry has a plain answer, and it is the same answer for buyer and seller. Carry enough to turn the worst morning into a bad one, and no more. Insurance larger than the risk it covers has stopped being insurance. It is a bet on the catastrophe, and it must be counted as one, with its own five sentences and its own size. The buyer who loads his baggage train with more puts than he has stock has not become safer. He has become an attacker on the long night, and Chapter 15 has his rules.

The insurer's chapter on reinsurance is the seller's half of the same lesson. A primary insurer writes thousands of policies, then cedes a slice of the premium to a reinsurer who pays the claims above some level. The primary keeps less in a good year and is still open in a bad one. No large insurer operates without it, and none regards its cost as wasted. The option seller already buys reinsurance on every policy: the wing. The choice of where to place it is the reinsurance buyer's choice: far and cheap, with a large claim still possible but capped; near and dear, with the claim capped early. Neither is correct in general. The right wing is the one that keeps the worst claim inside the reserve of Chapter 2.

The buy-back at a few cents is the reinsurance most sellers skip. A short option that has decayed to almost nothing still carries the full liability of the policy until it expires. Buying it back for those cents closes the liability; holding it to earn them keeps the whole limit at risk for almost no reward. Insurers cancel policies whose remaining premium no longer pays for their remaining risk, and the habit costs pennies to copy.

The twenty-first stratagem belongs here because insurance has an expiry too. A wing left to expire stops guarding the hour it expires, and a protective put carried into its last week is a husk: the shape is still on the screen, the protection is mostly gone, and an illiquid strike may be pinned against the price with nowhere to go. The settlement terms matter more for insurance than for any other position, because insurance is held for the bad day, and the bad day is when an assignment, a cash settlement at an unexpected print, or a short stock position nobody planned does the most damage. So the roll of the insurance is written in the calm, on a schedule, like a premium payment, and not decided on the morning the house is already warm.

The seller should finish this chapter with one recognition. Every defined-risk seller in this book has owned a long option on every trade: the wing. He bought it with part of his credit, he kept it after the cut, and on the day the line broke it was the only thing on the board that paid. He has been a buyer since Chapter 4. Seeing himself as a buyer of the far wing is what makes this one book and not two.

### 18. The Turn of the Wheel

## The Buyer

1. The direct and the indirect lead on to each other in turn. It is like moving in a circle: you never come to an end. Who can exhaust the possibilities of their combination?

2. Attack and defence lead on to each other in the same circle. The buyer's season ends in the seller's, and the seller's in the buyer's, and neither season is announced.

3. Just as water retains no constant shape, so in warfare there are no constant conditions. The price of fire has no constant level. The trader who keeps one stance through every season has chosen a shape for water.

4. The wheel is read, not forecast. The trader does not say what the river will do. He says what it is doing today, measured, and which bank that puts him on.

5. Long calm, with the price of fire starting to rise from a low base: the attacker's season. The sellers have priced the calm as permanent, and the first sign that it is not costs them more than it costs anyone.

6. High fire that has peaked and begun to fall: the defender's season. The buyers who arrived in the panic have paid for the storm in full, and the storm is leaving.

7. The hour before the number: neither. Whoever moves first is charged for the information the other side is about to receive for free. Stand on neither bank. Watch from across the river.

8. *Make the host and the guest exchange roles* (第30计 反客为主). The guest comes in by the side door, stays for supper, and by morning is answering the door as host.

9. In the trade the guest is the process: the calendar, the five sentences, the size, the time stop, the journal. It arrives as a visitor to one position and must end as the master of the house. When the process keeps the house, the trade is a guest. When the trade keeps the house, the process has been shown the door.

10. The turn of the wheel is not a mood. It is a decision the journal makes. Count the season: how many attacks, how many paid, what the losers cost, what the tax was. When the count says the attacker is no longer paid, the attacker turns.

11. Change sides in the temple, not on the tape. A change of stance decided while a position is losing is not a turn of the wheel. It is a revenge trade wearing the other army's uniform.

12. The one rule that does not turn: never hold both stances on the same direction. A long call and a short put on one name are one bet. A long wing and a credit sold the same way are one bet. Count the net direction, whatever the season.

## The Seller

13. The aim is not a good year. It is a company still writing policies in twenty years.

14. Insurance moves in cycles. In the soft market, premiums are thin and everyone writes. In the hard market, after the storm, premiums are rich and few can write.

15. In the soft market, write less. Thin premium is not an opportunity; it is the cheapest the risk will ever be sold, which is the reason not to sell much of it. The company that grows fastest in the soft market is the one that fails first in the hard one.

16. In the hard market, write carefully, at good prices, with the reserve the soft market taught you to keep.

17. The insurer's soft market is the buyer's season, and the insurer's hard market is the seller's. The wheel the underwriter has watched for three centuries is the same wheel this part of the book has been reading.

18. Keep the rules in writing: the size, the limit, the coasts, the reserve, the claim rule, the events excluded. Rules held in memory change with the mood. Rules written down change only by decision.

19. Review the journal on a schedule, not after a loss. A review after a loss is an argument. A review on the calendar is underwriting. Change a rule only with evidence from the journal, and never on the day it was broken.

20. Most of the work is declining business. Most of the profit is premium on policies that were never claimed. Most of the survival is the size of the ones that were.

21. A trader is measured by his best trade. An insurer is measured by his worst year, and by whether he was still open the year after.

## Commentary

The book began with a general counting in a temple, and it ends with a wheel. The general's chapter on energy has the image: the direct and the indirect leading to each other like a circle without end. The thirteen chapters read it for the seller's day, the trend spread and the hedge turning into each other four times between the open and the close. Part V reads it for the seasons. Attack and defence are the direct and the indirect of the whole trade, and they turn into each other as the price of fire rises and falls.

The three regimes are readings, not forecasts. A long calm in which implied volatility has started to rise from a low base is a river measured, not predicted: the sellers have been comfortable a long time, the price of protection is low, and the cost of being early to the end of the calm is small. That is the attacker's season, and it is the soft market of the insurer, when premiums are thin and every company writes. High volatility that has peaked and begun to fall is the reverse: the buyers who arrived in the panic paid for the storm, and the storm is leaving. That is the defender's season, and it is the insurer's hard market, when premiums are rich and only the companies that kept their reserves can write. The hour before a scheduled number is neither. On temporising ground, as Chapter 10 said, whoever moves first pays for information the other side will soon receive for free. None of these readings says what happens next. Each says which bank the price puts the trader on today, and whether the river has been measured.

The insurer's cycle and the trader's wheel are one wheel, seen by two trades over three centuries. Insurance runs in long soft stretches, when premium is cheap and competition fierce, broken by catastrophe years after which prices rise and capacity is scarce. The companies that survive the cycle are the ones that grew slowly in the soft years. The option seller who reads this book has the same instruction: write less in the long calm, when writing more feels safest; keep the reserve and the wings through it; and arrive at the hard market with something to sell. The buyer has the mirror instruction. The soft market is his season, and the reserve he kept through the seller's season, the cash not spent on the last ten ideas, is what lets him attack when it comes.

The thirtieth stratagem answers the question every trader with both stances must face: who decides when to turn? Not the trader on the tape, who has a position and a mood. The process decides. The guest who came in as a checklist must end as the host, governing every trade, or be shown the door the first time a trade is urgent. The weekly count is where the reversal is made or lost: how many trades, the expectancy after costs, the deepest drawdown, how many rules were broken. A week with a profit and three broken rules is not a good week for the process, whatever the statement says. Written beside the profit and loss, the broken rules show whether the result came from the method or from the departure. That is the journal, the surviving spy of Chapter 13, and it is the only one qualified to say whether a stance has been paid. The turn of the wheel is therefore a scheduled decision, made in the temple, on evidence, and never on the day a rule was broken.

One rule does not turn. A trader with one account and both stances must never hold both on the same direction. A long call and a short put on one name are one bet, however the legs are labelled. A long wing kept after a cut and a credit spread sold the same way are one bet. Chapter 7 said it for the seller's day and Chapter 15 said it for the buyer's attack; it is said a third time here because a trader who has learned to stand on both banks is the one most tempted to stand on both at once, and to call it balance.

The oldest insurance houses are centuries old, and none of them lasted by being right about the weather. They lasted by writing business they could price, refusing business they could not, keeping reserves through good years, paying claims through bad ones, and reviewing their ledgers on a calendar rather than in the heat of a loss. None of it predicts the market. All of it is what lets the business keep doing what this book began with: being paid to cover a risk, a thousand times, and still being open on the day it is wrong. The buyer's version of the hundred-year company is the same sentence turned around: paying for a right, a thousand times, small enough to be wrong most of them, and still present on the day the door opens.

The first edition closed on a sentence: neither side is the right side; each is a way of being wrong that can be survived, if it is counted. Part V lets the sentence be finished. Neither side is the right side. Each is a way of being wrong that can be survived, if it is counted. The seller is wrong rarely and expensively, and survives by the limit, the reserve and the wing. The buyer is wrong often and cheaply, and survives by the size, the date and the count. The price of fire decides which way of being wrong is being paid for today. The skill is knowing which one the price is paying for, standing on that bank and no other, and turning, in the temple and on the evidence, when the river does.

## Appendices

### Appendix A: The Thirty-Six Stratagems for the Buyer

*三十六计，买方读本*

The Thirty-Six Stratagems are an anonymous Chinese collection, set down in its present form late in the imperial era, of tricks played on an enemy, in six sets of six. The first edition of this book's buyer had them at full length in a companion volume, *The Counted Trade*. In this edition they are quoted through the chapters where they fit, each cited by its number, and here they stand whole: the stratagem, its image, and a few lines for the buyer. Where a chapter reads a stratagem at length, the page says so.

The enemy most often tricked is yourself. Read them in their order, for the order is the argument: count before the battle, read the other side's price, pass the funnel, hold the maximum loss in chaos, test without fooling yourself, and know when to leave.

#### Set I: Stratagems for Winning 《胜战计》

##### 1. Cross the Sea Under Camouflage · 瞒天过海 (第1计)

*The emperor feared the sea, so a general built him a house upon the water, and he crossed believing he sat on land.*

1. The hope trade enters the account dressed as conviction and furnished like a plan. The camouflage is aimed at the passenger, and the passenger is the buyer.

2. The test of the floor is five sentences said aloud before the order: the edge, the catalyst, the maximum loss, the time stop, the breakeven win rate.

3. Say them where another can hear, or write them where the later self will read. A sentence that cannot be said is a wall painted on water.

4. Conviction belongs to the rules and the thesis, never to the size. A size that grows with feeling is the sea showing through the paint.

*In this book:* Chapter 1; the five sentences in full in Chapter 15.

##### 2. Besiege Wei to Rescue Zhao · 围魏救赵 (第2计)

*When Wei besieged Handan, the relief rode not to Handan but to Daliang, the enemy's own capital, and the siege broke behind it.*

1. The price is Handan: loud, visible, and not where the trade is won. The mispricing is Daliang.

2. An edge is one sentence in three parts: I expect this mispricing, because of this measurable reason, and after costs this structure wins if this condition holds.

3. "I think it goes up" is a direction with a feeling attached. Feelings carry no mechanism.

4. After costs is not an afterthought. Commission, spread and decay are the road the army must march, and an edge that dies on the road was never an edge.

5. Where an outcome can only go two ways, the chain may still price one smooth curve. Besiege the curve, and the direction rescues itself.

*In this book:* Chapter 3.

##### 3. Kill With a Borrowed Knife · 借刀杀人 (第3计)

*A knife is borrowed, not forged. The hand supplies the direction; another man's weight supplies the blow.*

1. The buyer's knife is dated: an event on a day, a mechanism that reprices the stock, and a chain that has not yet charged for either.

2. A catalyst without a date is a rumour. A date without a mechanism is a coin.

3. Keep one calendar of the next forty-five days. Company events, macro dates, legal and regulatory decisions, mechanical events like an index rebalance. What falls outside it is another trade, entered early.

4. If the date passes and the price has not moved, the knife was the wrong knife, and the trade is wrong even if the story is still good.

5. No dated mechanism, no catalyst. No catalyst, no trade. Time without an event is rent paid to nobody.

*In this book:* Chapter 3.

##### 4. Wait at Leisure for the Exhausted Enemy · 以逸待劳 (第4计)

*The general does not chase. He holds the pass, keeps his men in the shade, and lets the enemy's march do half the work.*

1. For the buyer the march is time, and it tires the buyer, not the enemy. Every day held, the debit is eaten by an opponent who never sleeps.

2. Patience has a deadline: I leave on this date if the thesis has not confirmed, regardless of price.

3. Buy the time the thesis needs, or set the stop the time allows. Short time and a loose stop is the one combination with no defence.

4. The date is the day after the last event that could confirm the thesis, or the day the money must go elsewhere, whichever comes first. It is not chosen for comfort.

*In this book:* Chapter 2.

##### 5. Loot a Burning House · 趁火打劫 (第5计)

*The looter does not light the fire. He stands at the door and takes what the fire throws out.*

1. The fire is fear of a dated event, and fear has a price. Divide the at-the-money straddle by the share price: that is the move the chain expects.

2. Set it against the last eight to twelve moves the stock made after the same event. Far above that history, with volatility high in its range, the buyer is the one being looted. Far below it, with volatility near its lows, the event is being sold cheap.

3. Between the two there is no trade. A fire measured at the market's own estimate is not a fire. It is a price.

4. The second fire is bad news that has pushed a business below what it can survive at. It is measured in the filings, not the chain.

5. Two fires, two measures. If you cannot say which one you are looting, you are standing in the third, the one you lit yourself.

*In this book:* Chapter 12.

##### 6. Clamour in the East, Strike in the West · 声东击西 (第6计)

*Drums in the east, the army crosses in the west. The noise is not a lie about the war. It is a lie about the place.*

1. The edge at one level is not the edge at another. A finding about stocks is a finding about stocks until it has been tested in the option that will carry it.

2. The option adds a clock, a spread and a price of fear the stock never had. A strong stock result can become a losing options trade without any error in the stock result.

3. Ask where the edge lands before choosing the instrument. Sometimes it is the option. Sometimes it is the shares, and the option only adds a clock the thesis does not need.

4. A backtest in one instrument, presented as a strategy in another, is a feint played on yourself.

*In this book:* Chapter 5.

#### Set II: Stratagems for Confronting the Enemy 《敌战计》

##### 7. Create Something From Nothing · 无中生有 (第7计)

*Straw men went over the wall at night. The besiegers loosed at them until the quivers were empty, and by dawn the garrison had arrows.*

1. A backtest turned until it rises is a straw man at dusk. Nothing was found. Something was made.

2. Write before you look: the rule, the instrument, the entry, the exits, the size, and the result that would prove the rule false. Date the page. Do not edit it.

3. Count the trials. Ten filters tried and one kept is one kept out of ten, and it carries the luck of the nine discarded.

4. Pre-registration does not create an edge. It makes the absence of one visible, which is worth more than most edges.

5. He who can kill his own rule on the day the test fails has gained what the rule could never give him: a method.

*In this book:* Chapter 6; the failed pre-registered test in Chapter 15.

##### 8. Secretly Cross at Chencang · 暗渡陈仓 (第8计)

*The plank road was rebuilt in plain sight while the army crossed at Chencang, on a road nobody watched.*

1. An earnings date is a plank road. The crowd repairs it for weeks and on the morning after looks only at the gap.

2. The gap is the noise. The drift that follows, as estimates and guidance are slowly revised, is the road.

3. Before the print, the option carries the event inside its price. After the print, that premium is gone, and the same directional thesis is bought cheaper.

4. A debit spread a few weeks out, the near strike bought and the far one sold, pays for the road and not again for the gate.

5. Measure the drift in the name, over its own past reports, before trusting it. A pattern that is famous is also crowded.

*In this book:* Chapter 6.

##### 9. Watch the Fire From Across the River · 隔岸观火 (第9计)

*The house on the far bank is burning. The general does not cross to loot it. He lets the fire finish its argument.*

1. An event is a fire, and the straddle is the price of standing near it. When the price of standing near is greater than the heat, the far bank is the better post.

2. A priced event is a settled argument. The fear is already in the premium.

3. Watching is a position. Flat has a profit and loss of zero and keeps the quiver full.

4. "I have no view" is not the same as "there is no edge." Sometimes the edge is the refusal to pay for one.

5. The exception is an event measured as mispriced, with the number that would show it wrong written down before the event. The default is the far bank.

*In this book:* Chapter 12.

##### 10. Hide a Dagger Behind a Smile · 笑里藏刀 (第10计)

*The host smiles, fills the cup, and speaks of old friendship. The blade is under the sleeve.*

1. The far weekly smiles: a few cents buy a fortune at a strike the price almost never reaches. The smile is the price. The dagger is the probability.

2. Count the smile: the spread paid in and out, and the rent charged every day the far strike stays far.

3. The far strike must be crossed twice, once by the stock and once by the clock. The first is unlikely. The second is certain.

4. An option whose delta is two in a hundred has, by the market's own rough reckoning, about one chance in fifty of finishing in the money, and needs more than that to pay.

5. The buyer who wants the tail must buy time with it, or a nearer strike, or nothing.

*In this book:* Chapter 6.

##### 11. Sacrifice the Plum for the Peach · 李代桃僵 (第11计)

*A worm came to the peach root, and the plum tree beside it took the bite. The peach lived.*

1. The far strike is the plum. Sell it, and the near strike is bought cheaper; the debit that remains is the whole risk.

2. Buy the 100 call, sell the 110, pay 3: maximum loss 3, maximum gain 7, breakeven 103, and a breakeven win rate of the debit over the width, three in ten.

3. A long call at ten to one needs one win in eleven, and something near a doubling. The spread needs three in ten, and a three-point move.

4. Know which tree you are in. If the thesis is a doubling, the spread caps it. If the thesis is a modest move, the single leg is a donation.

5. For the seller the plum is the short leg cut on the rule, and the peach is the account, guarded by the wing he keeps.

*In this book:* Chapter 8.

##### 12. Take the Goat in Passing · 顺手牵羊 (第12计)

*The column passed the village at dusk. A goat stood by the road, and a soldier led it away without breaking step.*

1. The seller's goat is the volatility premium. Most days it stands by his road: implied above realised, no event on the calendar.

2. The buyer who pays for calm feeds the seller. Calm is what the seller sells, and calm is usually priced.

3. The goat is the buyer's only when it is untended: after a crush, implied below realised, with a dated event still ahead.

4. Quiet is not cheap. A stock at a three-year low in realised volatility is a description, not a signal, and a mechanical test of buying that quiet failed.

5. Take it in passing, small, on the way. A goat is not hunted for a season.

*In this book:* Chapter 6; the test in Chapter 15.

#### Set III: Stratagems for Attack 《攻战计》

##### 13. Beat the Grass to Startle the Snake · 打草惊蛇 (第13计)

*The beater strikes the grass, not the snake, and the rustle tells him more than any map.*

1. A paper trade is grass beaten at no price. Nothing is at stake, and the ground still answers.

2. Thirty to fifty forward paper trades on one setup, each recorded before money touches it: forward, not fitted to the past.

3. The first probe with money is the smallest position the market will carry. A probe that costs a real share of the budget is a position wearing a probe's coat.

4. What the beating shows is the ground: whether the strike fills, how wide the spread sits, whether the move arrives on the day.

5. One setup, one patch of grass, until its habits are known.

*In this book:* Chapter 9.

##### 14. Borrow a Corpse to Return the Soul · 借尸还魂 (第14计)

*The dead man is carried home so that the soul may enter the house it knew. The body is borrowed; only the soul must be living.*

1. An old screen is a corpse that still walks, in the direction of the last data it was fed.

2. A ratio is only as fresh as its oldest input. A short ratio built on a share count from years-old filings can rank a name as crowded when it is not.

3. Ask of every input: when was this last measured, by whom, and would a fresh count move the ranking?

4. A backtest fed on restated data is a soul returning to the wrong body, and it walks convincingly.

*In this book:* Chapter 15.

##### 15. Lure the Tiger Down the Mountain · 调虎离山 (第15计)

*The tiger cannot be fought on its mountain. It is drawn down to ground chosen before the bait is set.*

1. An illiquid strike is the tiger's mountain. The buyer who climbs to it pays the mountain's price, and the mountain keeps the difference.

2. Count the ground before the thesis: open interest at the strike, volume on the day, and the width of the bid-ask against the mid.

3. A wide spread is a tax charged before the thesis begins, on the way in and again on the way out, right or wrong.

4. Limit orders at mid. Price is the only term the buyer can still choose, and the chased fill is a loss taken in advance.

5. A strike that cannot be sold has not been bought. Better a small position on open ground than a large one in a cave.

*In this book:* Chapters 15 and 16.

##### 16. To Catch, First Let Go · 欲擒故纵 (第16计)

*The net is opened on one side, not from mercy, but so that the quarry runs into the side that is closed.*

1. A plan made at entry has targets in it. A target named after the price has moved is a wish with a number attached.

2. Release part at the first target: the trade can no longer cost the whole debit, and what has come back is the right to choose again.

3. Release the rest on a date, not on a feeling. The remainder is the only part that can pay for the losers.

4. Sell everything at the first target and the buyer has bought a wage. Sell nothing and he holds a lottery ticket with a thesis attached.

5. Write both lines before entry: the price at which part is released, and the date at which all of it is.

*In this book:* Chapter 7.

##### 17. Toss a Brick to Attract Jade · 抛砖引玉 (第17计)

*The brick is thrown to bring back jade. It is worth little, and that is why it can be thrown.*

1. A candidate is a brick. Most are discarded, and the discarding is the work.

2. The gates stand in order: liquidity, a dated catalyst, volatility, structure, risk, edge. Each removes, and none is skipped.

3. Liquidity first, because nothing else can be priced without it. Edge last, because it is the gate that fails quietly.

4. Five to ten leave the funnel, at most. Some weeks none, and the funnel is still working. A funnel that passes fifteen has a gate left open.

*In this book:* Chapter 15.

##### 18. To Catch Bandits, Catch Their King · 擒贼擒王 (第18计)

*A band of thieves is not broken by chasing every man. Take the chief, and the band loses its head and its plan.*

1. Fifteen positions are fifteen bandits: each small, each loud, none of them the king.

2. One tested setup is the king; one or two independent theses are his court.

3. Independence is not a label. Two call spreads in the same sector are one thesis wearing two tickers.

4. Test the king: the entry, the exit, the failure. The untested bandits will lose on their own.

5. Attention is the scarce capital, and it does not diversify. The account that watches fifteen things watches nothing.

*In this book:* Chapter 15.

#### Set IV: Stratagems for Confused Situations 《混战计》

##### 19. Remove the Firewood From Under the Pot · 釜底抽薪 (第19计)

*To still a boiling pot, no one ladles the water. A man draws the firewood from beneath it, and the boil dies of itself.*

1. A loss is the boiling; the size that feeds it is the firewood. To argue with the price after entry is to ladle.

2. Before the first order, write the worst case in one sentence: my worst case is this many dollars, because the debit is this.

3. Divide the risk budget by the worst case of one unit, and round down. If one contract is over the budget, take a narrower spread, a smaller underlying, or no trade. Never round up to one.

4. Risk one to make ten is a constraint before it is a hope. The one must be small enough to lose ten times in a row and still count the eleventh.

*In this book:* Chapter 4.

##### 20. Fish in Troubled Waters · 混水摸鱼 (第20计)

*The fisherman who cannot see the bottom stirs the mud, and the fish rise gasping.*

1. In a high-volatility market the water is already muddy. Protection and lottery tickets are bought in the same hour, and both are marked up.

2. Premium is dearest when buying feels most urgent. The crowd is not paying for information; it is paying for its own alarm.

3. A long single leg in that water buys the alarm at its peak. A debit spread hands part of the inflated premium back through the strike it sells.

4. The far bank of muddy water, where income looks easiest, is the seller's ground and needs a seller's reserve. The buyer does not cross in a panic.

*In this book:* Chapter 10.

##### 21. The Golden Cicada Sheds Its Shell · 金蝉脱壳 (第21计)

*The cicada leaves the husk whole and empty on the branch, and the bird strikes the husk.*

1. A position carried into expiry is that husk. The shape is still on the screen; the life has gone out of it.

2. Leave before the last days, not on them. The final week is where an illiquid short leg is pinned against a strike with nowhere to go.

3. Know what settles, and how. Index options settle in cash, at a morning print or a close. Options on a fund or a stock are claims on shares, and shares can be assigned.

4. The trader who writes the exit at entry keeps the choice of the shell. The one who waits for expiry hands that choice to the clearing house.

*In this book:* Chapter 17.

##### 22. Shut the Door to Catch the Thief · 关门捉贼 (第22计)

*When the thief is in the house, shut the door. Do not chase him into the fields, where the ground knows him better than it knows you.*

1. The plan is the door, and it shuts at entry. After that the thief is inside terms already written.

2. Do not move the stop because the price came close. Do not widen the spread because the debit became uncomfortable. Do not extend the time stop because the thesis is "still intact."

3. "Give it room" is the thief's phrase, spoken in the trader's voice.

4. Tightening is permitted. Loosening is the theft. A plan that may be revised under loss is a preference with a schedule.

*In this book:* Chapter 10.

##### 23. Befriend the Distant, Attack the Near · 远交近攻 (第23计)

*The far state is courted with a treaty; the near field is taken acre by acre.*

1. Five bullish call spreads in five technology names look like five distant states. They answer to one throne.

2. When one rate, one index or one fear moves all five, the five are one bet. Give that one bet one risk budget, not five.

3. Count the heat: what all open trades lose together, not what each loses alone. Keep it to a small, fixed share of the account, written in advance.

4. The distant ally is the trade that shares no catalyst with the rest: another driver, another date, another mechanism. It is a different reason, not a different ticker.

*In this book:* Chapter 5, where the insurer calls it accumulation.

##### 24. Borrow the Road to Conquer Guo · 假道伐虢 (第24计)

*Jin borrowed the road through Yu to reach Guo. Guo fell, and on the march home Yu fell with it.*

1. Single-stock options and index options are two wars. They share a screen and a vocabulary, not a method.

2. The single name asks: which company event, whose guidance, what skew, what borrow. The index asks: which macro date, what term structure, what premium for variance, how the contract settles.

3. An edge tested on one name does not become an index rule by being applied to more names.

4. Two calendars, two sets of filters, two tests, two journals. March each army on its own road and borrow nothing but the discipline.

*In this book:* the seller's index spreads of Parts I to IV and the buyer's single-name attacks of Part V are two such roads.

#### Set V: Stratagems for Gaining Ground 《并战计》

##### 25. Replace the Beams With Rotten Timbers · 偷梁换柱 (第25计)

*Swap the beams for rotten timber, paint them true, and the roof still sits, until the first snow.*

1. A hit rate is put where a win rate belongs. That a stock doubled within a quarter is a fact about the stock, not the record of a stopped option trade.

2. A hit rate counts arrivals. A trade counts exits: the premium paid, the date of the stop, the price at which the spread can be sold.

3. The second rotten beam is a rule that was never a rule. "Buy cheap convexity when the stock is quiet" sounds like a method. It is one man's judgement, compressed into a sentence by someone else.

4. When the beam is judgement, no filter cut from it will carry the same load. The test of Mai's quiet, in Chapter 15, is that beam removed.

*In this book:* Chapter 15.

##### 26. Point at the Mulberry, Curse the Locust · 指桑骂槐 (第26计)

*A man points at the mulberry and curses it, and the locust on the next branch mends its ways.*

1. A trade card names the evidence and names the absence of evidence; the reader is meant to hear both.

2. "Research, not yet proven, paper only" is not a warning stuck to the card. It is the card.

3. Say what was tested: which structure, which years, which costs, which exits, which sample. Say what was not.

4. A label that claims less than the evidence is honest. A label that claims more moves real risk onto a beam nobody has tested.

*In this book:* the rule of every chapter: no forecasts, no signals, no performance claims.

##### 27. Feign Madness but Keep Your Balance · 假痴不癫 (第27计)

*The general wears the fool's mask in camp and keeps his accounts by night.*

1. A day with no qualifying trade looks like a wasted day. It is a position, and often the right one.

2. To invent a candidate so that the day has a trade is to mistake activity for edge.

3. Record the empty day as a result: the candidates reviewed and why each was refused. Then it is evidence of work, not absence of it.

4. Folly that believes itself is no longer feigned. Wait; the count does not expire with the session.

*In this book:* Chapter 3, the buyer's declined book.

##### 28. Remove the Ladder When the Enemy Has Climbed · 上屋抽梯 (第28计)

*Invite a man onto the roof, then take the ladder away. He goes forward or he leaps.*

1. The buyer builds his ladder before he climbs: a first tranche, then rungs at prices written in advance.

2. The rungs are confirmation levels, not consolation levels. They are climbed when the thesis confirms, never because the price has fallen.

3. The whole ladder is measured against the maximum loss before the first step.

4. Once on the roof, the ladder comes away. Averaging down is not a ladder. It is a rope tied to the wrong beam.

*In this book:* Chapter 11.

##### 29. Deck the Tree With False Blossoms · 树上开花 (第29计)

*Silk blossoms are tied to a bare tree. From the road it is spring; up close it is string and dye.*

1. One spread that returned several times its debit makes a season, if it is the only one shown.

2. Look at the median, not the blossom. The middle trade is what the method usually does.

3. Look at the whole distribution: the worthless expiries, the trades never filled, the candidates the rule found and could not trade.

4. Many small losses and a few large gains is a description of a distribution, not a promise about the next trade.

5. The question is never "did most win." It is whether the few paid for all the rest, after costs, in a sample large enough to trust.

*In this book:* Chapter 16.

##### 30. Make the Host and the Guest Exchange Roles · 反客为主 (第30计)

*The guest comes in by the side door, stays for supper, and by morning is answering the door as host.*

1. The guest is the process: the calendar, the five sentences, the size, the time stop, the journal. It must end as master of the house.

2. The journal records facts, not verdicts. "The stop was moved" is a fact. "The trader is a fool" is a verdict, and a verdict teaches nothing.

3. Count the week: the number of trades, the expectancy after costs, the deepest drawdown, the rules broken.

4. Expectancy is arithmetic: the hit rate times the average win, minus the miss rate times the average loss, minus costs.

5. When the process keeps the house, the trade is a guest. When the trade keeps the house, the process has been shown the door.

*In this book:* Chapter 18.

#### Set VI: Stratagems for Desperate Situations 《败战计》

##### 31. The Beauty Trap · 美人计 (第31计)

*A beauty is not found; she is prepared: the dress, the lamp, the angle of the face. So is a backtest.*

1. Test her in the room where she was found, then in a room she has never seen. Fix the rule on one period, judge it on another, and keep a third untouched to the last day.

2. The untouched period is the only honest witness. Its door opens once.

3. A rule and its data, chosen together, cannot acquit each other.

4. Prefer the curve with a scar in it. The buyer needs this more than the seller, because one beautiful run of long calls is exactly what a fitted sample produces.

*In this book:* Chapter 16.

##### 32. The Empty Fort · 空城计 (第32计)

*The gates stood open, the streets were swept, and a man sat on the wall with his lute.*

1. An empty fort cannot be dressed. A pretended garrison is found out; an open gate is not.

2. In a drawdown the account has nothing to show. Show nothing: fewer trades, smaller, slower.

3. The position not taken cannot lose. Its maximum loss is known to the cent, and it needs no stop.

4. When the options fort has no strike, no bid and no open interest, the same judgement may hold another wall, the shares themselves. The cheapest expression is not always an option.

*In this book:* Chapter 16.

##### 33. Let the Enemy's Spy Sow Discord · 反间计 (第33计)

*A letter left on the desk for the spy to steal has done its work before it is read.*

1. The option chain is the enemy's spy, reporting daily what others fear: the implied move, the skew, the term structure, the price of borrow.

2. An intercepted report is useful twice: for what it says, and for why someone wished it said.

3. Skew is the spy's accent; it tells you which tail he fears. The term structure is his calendar.

4. Stale data is a double agent. It answers with yesterday's map and charges today's price.

5. The deepest secret is the shape: a smooth curve laid over an event that can only go two ways. Read where the map is wrong.

*In this book:* Chapter 13.

##### 34. Inflict Injury on Oneself to Win Trust · 苦肉计 (第34计)

*A general is beaten before his own camp and goes over to the enemy with the wounds still open. He is believed because the wounds are real.*

1. The stop is the buyer's wound, taken by his own hand before the enemy can take it for him.

2. Thirty to fifty trades are the only wound that teaches. Below that, the sample is an opinion.

3. Count the hit rate against the breakeven win rate, after costs. If the setup does not clear that line by a comfortable margin, it is killed.

4. The kill rule is written while the setup is still beautiful. Written afterwards, it is a eulogy. A setup you cannot kill is the one that kills you.

*In this book:* Chapter 16.

##### 35. Chain Stratagems · 连环计 (第35计)

*The ships were lashed together so the fleet would ride steady in the swell. The steadiness was the gift, and one fire was the price.*

1. A trade is a chain: the calendar, the five sentences, the size, the alert, the log, the review. One weak link and the rest is cargo.

2. Sunday: the dated events on one calendar, the volatility ranks, the liquidity, five to ten candidates, no more.

3. The exit is written while the head is cool, not decided while the screen is red. After the trade, the log; each week, the review.

4. A chain is a routine that runs in the weeks you do not feel like running it. And a book chained to one factor is the lashed fleet: steady in calm water, burning all at once.

*In this book:* Chapter 5.

##### 36. If All Else Fails, Retreat · 走为上 (第36计)

*Of the thirty-six, the last is to march away: not the proudest, but the one that keeps the army.*

1. When "I have to make it back" speaks, the defeat is no longer on the field. It is in the man.

2. Revenge is a position with no stop and no catalyst.

3. Retreat to paper, or to a size too small to hurt, until the trades can be read without shame.

4. The account that leaves the field returns to it. The account that stays to prove something does not.

5. The last stratagem is the first: know the way out before you take the field. What comes home is the capital, the log, and the habit of returning.

*In this book:* Chapter 16.

### Appendix B: The Underwriter's Ledger

*承保人的账本*

The seller's half of this book reads the credit spread as an insurance policy, and the insurer's rules are scattered through the chapters where the general's lines call for them. Here they are on one page: thirteen rules, one for each part of an insurance company, with the chapter that reads each at length. Read the page before the first order of the day, and again on the day a rule was broken.

| # | The rule | In one sentence | Chapter |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **The policy** | Read all five terms off the ticket, premium, attachment point, limit, term and size, and say aloud: *I am paid this to cover that, the limit is the width, the term ends on this day, and I can pay the full claim.* | 1 |
| 2 | **The price of fear** | Sell only when the premium, the market's fear, is larger than the loss cost, the market's truth, as measured in your own table and not in last month's mood. | 6 |
| 3 | **Underwriting** | Most of the work is saying no: exclude the known hazard on the calendar, distrust the sudden eager buyer, and keep a book of the policies you declined. | 3 |
| 4 | **The law of large numbers** | Many small policies, each survivable, all priced; size before frequency, and never raise the size after a good month or a bad one. | 2 |
| 5 | **Accumulation** | Count the coasts, not the houses: ask what the whole book pays if the market falls five percent before the term ends, and give that number a limit. | 5 |
| 6 | **Limits and deductibles** | Every policy carries a wing; know which deductible you wrote; and remember that a limited policy written too many times is an unlimited book. | 4 |
| 7 | **Claims** | Decide the settlement before the sale, settle small and early, hedge into the condor only when hurt, and never write the next policy to make back the last claim. | 7 |
| 8 | **Reserves** | Keep a fixed share of the account unused, measured in full claims it can pay; refill it before writing again. | 2 |
| 9 | **The combined ratio** | Claims plus costs, divided by premium, kept monthly from the journal; above one hundred, stop and find which line is wrong; drop the line that carries the losses. | 13 |
| 10 | **Catastrophe** | Run the storm on paper while the sun shines; if the paper storm closes the account, the size is wrong today. | 12 |
| 11 | **Reinsurance** | The wing is cover bought with part of the premium; buy back the short at a few cents; cede enough to sleep and keep enough to earn. | 17 |
| 12 | **Float** | The credit is premium held in trust, earned by the clock; never count it, or size on it, before the term has passed. | 9 |
| 13 | **The hundred-year company** | Write less in the soft market and carefully in the hard one; keep the rules in writing; review the journal on a calendar; change a rule only on evidence, never on the day it was broken. | 18 |

## The seller's rule line

Said once, here, because the first edition and its companions said it too often:

**Size over frequency. A limit on every position. One side first. Hedge into the condor only when hurt. Cut the short leg and keep the wing. Never stack a second position on the same direction.**

## The buyer's line, for the facing page

The buyer has no ledger of thirteen. He has five sentences, said before he pays, which are given in full in Chapter 15:

**The edge, as a price. The catalyst, with a date and a mechanism. The maximum loss, as a number. The time stop, chosen from the catalyst. The breakeven win rate, the debit over what the ticket pays when right.**

And one line that both sides share:

**When the reason is gone, the trade is gone. The money follows.**

### Appendix C: Sources and Translations

*出处与译文*

**Sun Tzu.** The English quotations of *The Art of War* (《孙子兵法》) are from Lionel Giles, *Sun Tzu on the Art of War: The Oldest Military Treatise in the World* (London: Luzac, 1910), which is in the public domain. Where a verse adapts Giles to the trade, the adaptation is the author's and follows the general's line in the same verse. The chapters keep the general's thirteen titles and his order. Chinese quotations, such as 以迂为直，以患为利 in Chapter 7, follow the received text.

**The Thirty-Six Stratagems.** 《三十六计》 is an anonymous collection of proverbs and their commentary, gathered in its present form late in the imperial era and first printed in the twentieth century. Its six sets and thirty-six names are standard. The English names of the stratagems, the one-line images and every reading for the buyer are the author's own. The stratagems are cited by number in the chapters (第1计 to 第36计) and stand whole in Appendix A. The fullest earlier reading is the author's *The Counted Trade* (first edition), which this book now absorbs.

**The insurer.** The scene at Edward Lloyd's coffee house in 1688, and the practice of underwriting, reserving, reinsurance and the combined ratio, are general history and industry practice, read here against the defined-risk credit spread. The fullest earlier reading is the author's *The Options Underwriter* (first edition), which this book now absorbs. Hurricane Andrew (1992), the crash of 19 October 1987, the volatility shock of 5 February 2018 and the fall of February and March 2020 are matters of public record.

**The second voice.** Jamie Mai's practice is described as he gave it in his interview in Jack D. Schwager, *Hedge Fund Market Wizards* (Wiley, 2012), and as Michael Lewis tells it in *The Big Short* (W. W. Norton, 2010). Nothing in this book is drawn from any other source on Mai, and nothing here says what he would do today. The pre-registered test of a mechanical copy of his entry condition, in Chapter 15, is the one first recorded in *The Counted Trade*; it failed, and it is reported because it failed.

**The roundabout path.** Mark Spitznagel, *The Dao of Capital: Austrian Investing in a Distorted World* (Wiley, 2013), builds on the *Umweg*, the roundabout path. Chapter 7 credits its oldest statement, 以迂为直, to Sun Tzu's chapter on manoeuvring. Spitznagel's *Safe Haven: Investing for Financial Storms* (Wiley, 2021) is cited in Chapter 17 for the idea that insurance is judged by its effect on the whole. No text from either book is quoted.

**Arithmetic.** Every number in this book that is not a date or a citation is arithmetic set out so that it can be checked on paper: the straddle approximation (about 0.8 × price × volatility × the square root of the time left in years), the annualising of daily moves by the square root of 252, the breakeven win rate (the debit divided by what the ticket pays when right), the losing-run probabilities, and the arithmetic of recovery after a loss. The research counts quoted in Chapters 5, 12, 15 and 16 are records of tests, most of which failed. None is a performance claim, a forecast or a signal.
