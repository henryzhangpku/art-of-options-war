# The Art of Options War

*Henry Zhang*


## Before the Temple Count

Two and a half thousand years ago a general wrote thirteen short chapters on how to win without being destroyed. He was not interested in glory. He was interested in the arithmetic of survival: which battles to fight, which to refuse, how to be paid by an enemy's mistakes instead of your own courage, and why the state that wins every battle can still lose the war.

Selling options is the same problem in a different uniform. The seller is paid to hold a line. He does not need to know where the enemy will go, only where the enemy will not arrive in time. He wins most engagements and can be ruined by one. Every chapter that follows borrows the old general's structure — thirteen chapters, in his order, on his subjects — and reads it against a single instrument: the defined-risk credit spread, sold on the index, by a trader who intends to still be trading next year.

This book is education, not advice. It contains no recommendations for you personally, no signals and no promises. Options can lose money faster than stock. A defined-risk spread cannot lose more than its width, but its width can still be more than you can afford, and a run of them can still end an account. Nothing here is a performance claim; where the text names a number, the number is arithmetic you can check on paper.

Read one chapter a day. Then read them again, in the order the general chose, because the order is the argument: count before you fight, know what a campaign costs, win without needing to be right, make yourself unbeatable before you look for a chance, and — last — value information above every other weapon.


## Part I: The Count

The general began in the temple, before a single soldier moved. Whoever counts more carefully wins; whoever counts less, loses; whoever does not count at all does not deserve to be surprised.

The first three chapters are the count. What the campaign will be judged on, what it costs to keep an army in the field, and the one idea that turns the whole book: the highest skill is to win without fighting — to be paid without having to be right.


### 1. Counting in the Temple

1. The trade is a matter of vital importance to the account. It is the ground of survival or ruin. It cannot be entered on a whim.

2. Therefore, before the market opens, weigh the trade by five constants, and compare your side with the enemy's.

3. These are: the Sky, the Ground, the Commander, the Method, and the Cause.

4. The Sky is time. It is the option's expiry, the hours that remain, the decay that runs in one direction only. The Sky favours the seller, and the seller must never fight it.

5. The Ground is the terrain of prices: where the strikes stand, how wide the spread is, where the walls of dealer hedging rise and where the floors lie. The Ground decides how far the enemy must travel to reach you.

6. The Commander is the trader: his patience, his discipline, his willingness to take a small loss, his refusal to double a bet. All five constants are weighed, but the Commander is the one who fails.

7. The Method is the plan for money: how the day's risk is divided, when the hedge is sold, when the short leg is cut, when the wing is left to ride. A Method that exists only in the head does not exist.

8. The Cause is why you trade at all. If it is to be right, you will lose. If it is to be paid for standing behind a line, you may endure.

9. He who knows these five will prevail; he who does not will be surprised, and surprise is the seller's only enemy.

10. Therefore, before entering, ask: which side has the Sky? Whose Ground is easier to defend? Which Commander is calmer? Whose Method has a rule for being wrong? By these, the outcome is known.

11. All trading is based on positioning. When you are willing to sell, seem uninterested. When the market screams direction, remember that it is offering you a price for the fence, not a prophecy.

12. The seller who wins first counts in the temple. He knows his maximum loss before he knows his profit. He knows what he will do if the line is tested before it is tested. He knows the day can turn four times, and he has kept powder for each turn.

13. The seller who counts once wins once. The seller who counts every morning is still here in the afternoon, and in the year after.

## Commentary

The old text begins with counting because the temple is the only place a battle can be lost without cost. For the option seller the temple count is one sentence, said out loud before the order: *this is a five-wide spread sold for one, the maximum loss is four, and the loss at which I cut is one.* If the sentence cannot be said, the trade cannot be taken.

Of the five constants, only the Sky is reliably yours. Time decays in one direction, and a seller who does not fight it is paid every hour he is not wrong. The Ground can be chosen but not controlled. The Method can be written and then ignored. And the Commander is where every account dies: not from being wrong about direction, which is ordinary, but from refusing to be wrong at a small size.

The Cause is placed last because it is the quietest. A trader who needs to be right will hold a broken spread to prove a point. A trader who is paid to hold a line simply moves the line, or leaves the field. The rest of this book assumes the second man.


### 2. The Cost of the Campaign

1. When you take the field, know what it costs to stay there.

2. The buyer of options pays rent every day for the right to be right later. The seller collects the rent. This is the whole of the seller's advantage, and it is not small; but it is a wage, not a treasure.

3. A wage is earned by staying alive to collect it. A treasure is lost in one campaign.

4. There is no instance of an account benefiting from a prolonged war with a broken position. What was sold to expire quietly, and did not, must not be fed with hope.

5. The cost of a long siege is not the loss on the ticket. It is the capital pinned under a position that cannot win, the trades not taken while it is held, and the Commander's judgement, which rots in a siege.

6. Therefore the wise seller does not raise a second levy. He does not add to a losing spread. He does not sell a fresh line beside the one that is failing. He ends the engagement, and the cost is one collection, not four.

7. The clever general forages on the enemy. The clever seller is paid by the buyer's need for protection, which is constant, and by the buyer's love of direction, which is expensive.

8. One credit taken from the market is worth twenty predicted.

9. In war, then, let your object be a quick, quiet settlement. In a spread, let your object be to collect and leave, or to cut and leave, but in either case to leave.

10. The seller who understands the cost of the campaign never asks how much he can make. He asks how many days he can stay wrong, and prices the trade so the answer is more than one.

11. Speed has never been the enemy of the seller. Only the slow exit is.

## Commentary

The chapter on waging war is a chapter about expense, and in this trade the expense is well hidden. The credit looks like income; the risk does not appear on any statement until the morning it does. A seller who holds a failing spread believes he is being patient. In the old general's arithmetic he is feeding an army that has already lost, and the provisions come from the treasury that was supposed to fund the next hundred trades.

The number that matters is the ratio of the loss you allow to the credit you took. A five-wide spread sold for one carries a maximum loss of four. Cut at one and the campaign cost a single collection. Refuse, and it costs four — the earnings of four quiet days, spent to be stubborn on one loud one. Theta pays the seller a wage precisely because it is small and repeated; a single maximum loss is the treasure that the wage can never rebuy.

Foraging on the enemy has a plain meaning here. Someone must sell protection to a market that must hedge. Someone must sell direction to a buyer who wants it. The seller's provisions are the other side's needs, and those needs renew every session. His own provisions — his capital and his temper — do not.


### 3. Winning Without Fighting

1. In the practical art of trading, the best thing is to take the credit whole and unharmed; to fight for it against a moving market is second best.

2. To win a hundred directional battles is not the height of skill. To be paid without needing direction at all — that is the height of skill.

3. Thus the highest form of selling is to be right about where the market will *not* go. The next is to hedge when it goes. The next is to cut. The lowest is to hold to expiry and hope.

4. Ask a buyer what the market will do and he answers up, or down. He has named one door of four.

5. The market may rise far. It may rise and stall. It may fall. It may go nowhere at all. The buyer needs one door. The seller of the fence is paid by the other three.

6. Three doors of four is not three wins in four. The doors are not equal in width, and the one that hurts is wider than any that pays. The market prices them honestly. Take the three doors for what they are: a better shape to be wrong in.

7. The skilful seller subdues the enemy's direction without fighting it. He sets his line where the enemy must travel far and fast to reach it, and he is paid for every day the enemy does not.

8. He does not attack the trend. He sells beneath a rising market and above a falling one, and lets the trend defend his line for him.

9. The rule is: when the tape is calm, collect. When it moves, hedge into the move. When it runs through your line, cut the short and keep the wing. Do not besiege a moving market; a siege costs a season.

10. Know the enemy and know yourself, and in a hundred trades you will not be in danger. Know yourself but not the enemy, and for every credit you take you will give one back. Know neither, and you will be in danger in every trade.

11. To know the enemy is to know that flat is the market's ordinary state, and that direction is the exception it is famous for.

12. To know yourself is to know the exact loss at which you will leave, and to have left at that loss before.

## Commentary

The general's most famous line — that supreme excellence is to win without fighting — is the seller's whole thesis. The buyer of an option must be right three times: direction, distance and date. The seller of a spread must be right once: that price stays on his side of a line he chose. He has given away the arrow and kept the fence, and the fence is priced. He is not smarter than the buyer. He has simply refused the fight the buyer insists on.

The four doors are the honest version of this. Only a real move, far enough, in time, hurts the seller; stall, reverse and flat all pay him. But the honest half must be said in the same breath: the losing door is also the widest loss. Nothing about the shape of the bet promises a win rate, and a seller who hears "three in four" as "seventy-five percent" has already lost the count. The shape buys tractability, not edge.

Knowing yourself, in this chapter, is not a mood. It is a number. The seller who cannot say the price at which he will leave does not know himself, however calm he feels at the open, because the tape will ask him the question when he is least calm.


## Part II: Shape and Force

Chapters four to six are about shape. The old text says that the skilful fighter first makes himself invincible and then waits for the enemy to become vulnerable. Invincibility is a matter of defence and lies with you; vulnerability is a matter of the enemy and does not.

For the premium seller, that is the entire trade. The defined-risk spread is the invincible position; the hedge into reversal is the response to the enemy's move; compounding is the force that a well-shaped position releases over time.


### 4. The Unbeatable Position

1. The good fighters of old first put themselves beyond the possibility of defeat, and then waited for an opportunity to defeat the enemy.

2. To be beyond defeat lies in your own hands. To defeat the enemy lies in the enemy's. The seller can make himself unbeatable; he cannot make the market give him a move.

3. Being unbeatable is a matter of defence. The defined-risk spread is the only position in this book, because it is the only one whose worst day is printed on the ticket before the day begins.

4. Sell a five-wide spread for one, and the maximum loss is four. Written. Known. Chosen. The market cannot enlarge it. Only the seller can, by selling more.

5. He who sells naked has no shape. He has a wage on ordinary days and a debt without a ceiling on the day that is not ordinary. A position without a ceiling is not a position. It is a wager on the weather.

6. The seller who is beyond defeat does not need to know the future. He needs only to have set his line where the future must work hard to arrive, and to have made his loss survivable if it does.

7. Standing on the defensive indicates insufficient strength; attacking, a superabundance of it. The seller has never had a superabundance. He has a small, repeatable wage. Let him stand on the defensive and be content.

8. What the ancients called a clever fighter is one who not only wins, but wins with ease. The seller wins with ease when his line is far and his size is small; every trade that felt like a fight was a trade sized for a different day.

9. The consummate seller cultivates the Method and adheres to discipline. Thus it is in his power to control success.

10. The Method is: max loss first; size so that a maximum loss is an ordinary day, not a ruinous one; cut at the credit; never stack a second position on the same direction.

11. A victorious seller enters the trade having already survived it. A defeated seller enters, and then looks for a way to survive.

## Commentary

This chapter is the reason the whole book is about spreads and nothing else. The general's "invincible" has an exact translation: a position whose maximum loss is a number, not a hope. The seller of a vertical spread has read his worst day off the ticket. He may dislike that day, but he cannot be surprised by it, and surprise is the only thing in this trade that ends accounts.

"First be beyond defeat, then wait" also fixes the order of operations that most traders reverse. They find a direction they believe in, then look for a way to size it. The seller does the opposite: he fixes the size such that the printed maximum loss is a normal, boring expense, and only then asks where the line should be. The line can be wrong — that is Tuesday. The size cannot be wrong, because the size is what decides whether Wednesday happens.

The fair reading of "standing on the defensive indicates insufficient strength" is that it is true, and that is fine. The seller's edge, where it exists, is a small, structural premium for holding a line that others must cross. It is not large enough to survive being attacked with. Whoever treats it as a war chest has misread the ledger.


### 5. Force and the Four Quarters

1. The control of a large force is the same in principle as the control of a few: it is a matter of dividing it.

2. Divide the day's risk in four before the day divides it for you.

3. One quarter for the direction you took. One for the hedge you will need. One for the direction that returns. One for the fluctuation you must simply survive.

4. Full size on the first entry is not conviction. It is the absence of a plan for being wrong.

5. In all fighting the direct method is used for joining battle, and the indirect for securing victory. The direct method is the first spread, sold in the direction of the trend. The indirect is the second side, sold only when the first is hurt, which turns a bet into a condor.

6. The direct and the indirect lead on to each other in turn. It is like moving in a circle: you never come to an end. The trend read, the hedge, the trend again, the exit — the whole day is four turns of one wheel.

7. Energy is the bending of the crossbow; timing is the release of the trigger. The seller's energy is his credit already collected. His timing is the stop.

8. The quality of decision is like the well-timed swoop of a falcon which enables it to strike and destroy its victim. The stop is not a retreat. It is the swoop.

9. Compounding is the momentum of a round stone rolled down a mountain. Ten in a hundred, kept and rolled thirty times, becomes a number that no longer looks like ten.

10. Double your money and lose half, and you are exactly where you began, having paid for the ride. The account does not remember your best day. It remembers the product of all of them.

11. Therefore the good seller seeks his result from the shape of the sequence, not from any single battle. He does not demand too much of one trade. He demands that the next trade be possible.

12. Small and repeatable outruns large and rare, and it does it quietly. Boring is the product.

## Commentary

The general's chapter on energy is usually read as a chapter on momentum, and the seller's momentum is compounding. Returns multiply; they do not add. A plus-one-hundred followed by a minus-fifty is two times one half — exactly nothing, after a month of adrenaline. The same account taking a smaller figure without a reset ends the period on a number that looks like an error. This is not a claim about markets; it is what multiplication does, and it can be checked on paper in a minute.

The four quarters are the seller's division of force. The first quarter is the read: one spread, in the direction of the trend, at a quarter of what you are willing to lose today. The second is the hedge, sold only when the first side starts to hurt, which completes an iron condor instead of closing in fear or doubling in pride. The third is the re-entry when the turn comes, on better information than the open offered. The fourth buys nothing and earns nothing: it is the room to let an open position breathe against you without being closed by your broker or by yourself.

Nothing in this chapter says you will earn ten percent at any interval, ever. It says only that if you ever do have a repeatable process, what decides where you finish is not the size of your best result. It is whether the sequence stays unbroken — and the stop, the falcon's swoop, is what keeps it so.


### 6. Weak Points and Strong

1. Whoever is first in the field and awaits the enemy will be fresh for the fight; whoever comes second and has to hasten to battle will arrive exhausted.

2. The seller who sets his line while the tape is calm chooses his ground. The seller who chases a move that has already happened is sold the worst price of the day, and calls it conviction.

3. By holding out advantages to him, you can cause the enemy to approach of his own accord. The market approaches your line of its own accord; you do not go to meet it.

4. You can be sure of succeeding in your attacks if you only attack places which are undefended. The seller attacks undefended ground: the strike the market must travel far to reach, beyond the wall where dealer hedging leans against it.

5. You can ensure the safety of your defence if you only hold positions that cannot be attacked. A line far enough, with a wing behind it, cannot be attacked by an ordinary day. It can only be attacked by the exceptional one — and for that, the wing.

6. Do not repeat the tactics which have gained you one victory, but let your methods be regulated by the infinite variety of circumstances. The line that held yesterday is not the line for today. The walls move.

7. Military tactics are like water; for water in its natural course runs away from high places and hastens downwards. So in trading: avoid what is strong and strike at what is weak. Avoid the direction the crowd has paid for; sell the range the crowd has abandoned.

8. Water shapes its course according to the nature of the ground over which it flows. The seller shapes his position according to the enemy he is facing.

9. Therefore the seller does not predict. He responds. The first spread is a read. The hedge is a response. The cut is a response. The wing left to ride is a response. Nothing in the book is a forecast.

10. He who can modify his tactics in relation to his opponent and thereby succeed in winning may be called a heaven-born captain. He who can turn a directional spread into a condor, and a condor into a lone wing riding a trend, without ever adding to what is losing, may be called a seller.

11. The five elements are not always equally predominant; the four seasons make way for each other in turn. The market has its seasons too: flat is its ordinary one, and direction is a treat.

## Commentary

Weak points and strong is the chapter of the hedge. The old general's water finds the low ground; the seller's position finds the side of the market that is not moving. When the first spread — sold in the direction of the trend — begins to hurt, the answer is not to defend it and not to double it. It is to sell the other side. The bet becomes a condor, the credit doubles, and a bad morning becomes a flat afternoon. The second side is a reaction to being hurt, never a schedule; a condor sold at the open is a different trade with twice the risk and none of the information.

From the condor there are two futures. If the market goes flat, both credits are collected and the day is ordinary. If the trend resumes and runs through a line, the seller does not cut the losing vertical whole. He buys back the short leg only and keeps the wing — which is cheap, already paid for, and now the only thing in the book that profits if the move continues. Paired with the winning credit spread on the other side, it is a net long riding the very move that hurt him.

"Do not repeat the tactics that won" has a dull, exact meaning: the strikes that were safe yesterday are not information about today. The walls of dealer positioning move overnight, the expected move changes with volatility, and the line must be chosen again every morning, in the temple, before the first order.


## Part III: The March

Armies lose more men on the march than in battle. Chapters seven to nine are about movement: the cost of hurry, the rules that must bend and the rules that must not, and how to read the ground and the enemy from the signs they leave.

In the market, the march is the trading day. Most accounts are not lost to one great battle. They are lost to marching too fast, trading too often, and reading omens where there are only signs.


### 7. Manoeuvring

1. There is nothing harder than manoeuvring. The difficulty consists in turning the devious into the direct, and misfortune into gain.

2. The seller's manoeuvre is the hedge: the devious route by which a losing morning is turned into a flat afternoon. It is slow, it is unglamorous, and it is the whole of the skill.

3. If you march your army in haste to seize an advantage, you will arrive with a tenth of your force. The trader who chases every move arrives at the important one with no capital and no temper.

4. An army without its baggage train is lost. A seller without his fourth quarter — the room to sit through a fluctuation — is closed out by his own broker in the hour before the trade resolves.

5. We cannot enter into alliance until we are acquainted with the designs of our neighbours. We cannot sell a line until we are acquainted with the walls of the day, the expected move, and the event on the calendar.

6. Let your rapidity be that of the wind, your compactness that of the forest. Be rapid in cutting. Be compact in size.

7. In raiding, divide your spoils. In taking a credit, take it: the seller who waits for the last tenth of a credit is paid a tenth and risks the whole.

8. He who knows the art of the direct and the indirect will win. The direct is the trend spread. The indirect is the condor. Neither is an addition to the other; each is a substitution.

9. The seller who sells a second spread beside a losing one has not manoeuvred. He has doubled. A long wing and a credit sold with the trend are the same directional bet in two costumes; count the net direction, not the legs.

10. Never stack a second position on the same direction. Size over frequency. One exposure, hedged or cut, never repeated.

11. Do not trade non-stop. When the condor's balance breaks — when a short is cut or a hedge is sold — that is a resolution, not an invitation. An engine that re-enters every time its shape changes has no ceiling, and a trader without a ceiling has no floor.

12. A whole army may be robbed of its spirit; a commander may be robbed of his presence of mind. The tape robs the seller of presence of mind by moving quickly. His defence is that his decisions were made in the temple, slowly.

13. Do not pursue an enemy who simulates flight. Do not chase a reversal that has not confirmed. Do not attack soldiers whose temper is keen; do not sell into a panic that is still accelerating. Leave a way of escape to a surrounded enemy; leave a wing in every spread.

## Commentary

Manoeuvring is the chapter about frequency, and frequency is a risk dial that most traders do not know they are turning. The old general loses his baggage by marching too fast; the seller loses his fourth quarter by trading too often. Every additional entry in a day is a levy on the reserve that was supposed to let an open position breathe. By noon the fast marcher is full size in one direction with nothing left for the hedge, and the first ordinary fluctuation closes him out.

Two rules in this chapter are absolute. The first is never to stack on the same direction. It is easy to believe that a second credit spread, sold with the trend, is "pairing" with the wing that survived a cut — but a long wing and a spread sold in the same direction are the same bet, and pairing them is doubling on the side that has just run through a strike. Read a book by its net exposure, not its leg count. The second is the ceiling: the position's own state must limit how many times it can open, and "usually stops re-entering" is not a rule.

Rapid as the wind is about one action only: the cut. Everything else in this trade is meant to be slow — the temple count, the line chosen while the tape is calm, the hedge sold only when hurt. Speed kept for the exit, and nowhere else, is what separates manoeuvre from panic.


### 8. Variation

1. There are roads which must not be followed, armies which must not be attacked, towns which must not be besieged, positions which must not be contested, commands of the sovereign which must not be obeyed.

2. There are trades which must not be taken. There are lines which must not be defended. There are losses which must not be held. There are signals which must not be followed, however good the source. There are opinions of your own which must not be obeyed.

3. The general who thoroughly understands the advantages that accompany variation of tactics knows how to handle his troops. He who does not understand these may be well acquainted with the ground, and yet not be able to turn his knowledge to account.

4. Ground is the setup. Variation is what you do when the setup is wrong. Most traders study ground for years and variation for never.

5. The wise seller considers both advantage and disadvantage in one thought. In the advantage — the credit — he keeps the disadvantage in view: the four he can lose. In the disadvantage — the stop — he keeps the advantage in view: the sequence it protects.

6. The art of war teaches us to rely not on the likelihood of the enemy's not coming, but on our own readiness to receive him. The art of selling teaches us to rely not on the likelihood that the line holds, but on the plan for the day it does not.

7. There are five dangerous faults which may affect a general: recklessness, cowardice, a hasty temper, a delicacy of honour, and over-solicitude for his men.

8. There are five dangerous faults which may affect a seller. Recklessness, which sells too wide and too much. Cowardice, which closes a working spread at the first fluctuation and never collects. A hasty temper, which trades back at the market that hurt it. A delicacy of honour, which will not take a small loss because a small loss admits a small error. And over-solicitude for the position, which holds a broken line out of loyalty to the morning's opinion.

9. These are the five besetting sins of a seller, ruinous to the conduct of a campaign. When an account is overturned and its trader slain, the cause will surely be found among these five.

10. Of the five, the fourth is the deadliest. The trader who cannot cut does not have a strategy. He has a hostage.

11. Therefore the rule for variation is one rule: when the reason for the trade is gone, the trade is gone. Not the money — the reason. The money follows.

## Commentary

The chapter on variation is the general's warning that rules without exceptions are for men who do not intend to survive contact. The seller's exceptions are the roads not to follow: the event day when the expected move is a lie, the line that would be right if only the wall had not moved overnight, the signal that arrives from a trusted source and is wrong anyway. Knowing the setup is the ground; knowing when the setup no longer applies is the variation, and it is the harder study.

The five faults translate almost without effort, and the translation is unkind. Recklessness is size. Cowardice is the seller who never lets a credit mature. Hasty temper is revenge trading — selling back at the market within minutes of a cut, on no read at all. Delicacy of honour is the deadliest because it is invisible: it feels like conviction, and it is nothing but the refusal to be wrong at a size that would not matter. Over-solicitude is the loyalty a trader feels toward the opinion he had at 9:31, long after the market has stopped sharing it.

The single rule of variation is that the reason ends the trade, not the price. A spread sold because the trend was up, in a market whose structure has just broken to the downside, has lost its reason whether or not it has yet lost money. Wait for the money to confirm what the reason already said and you will have waited exactly as long as the market needs to charge you for it.


### 9. The March

1. Camp in high places, facing the sun. Do not climb heights in order to fight. So much for mountain warfare.

2. Sell your line on the high ground: beyond the wall where dealer hedging leans against price, on the side of the market that must climb to reach you. Do not sell into a climb to prove you can. So much for placing a strike.

3. After crossing a river, get far away from it. Do not linger near the level the market has just crossed; a level just broken is the one it comes back to test.

4. In crossing salt marshes, your sole concern should be to get over them quickly. The chop between two levels is a marsh. Do not sell a line inside it; the seller in the marsh is nibbled from both sides and paid for neither.

5. All armies prefer high ground to low, and sunny places to dark. High ground is distance from price; sunny places are calm volatility. A seller who prefers the low ground and the dark is a buyer who has not yet noticed.

6. When the enemy is close at hand and remains quiet, he is relying on the natural strength of his position. When the tape is quiet near your line, it is not friendship. It is a market gathering.

7. Movement amongst the trees of a forest shows the enemy is advancing. The rise of birds in flight is the sign of an ambuscade. The market's signs are the same: a pivot taken out, a change of character in the swing, a trailing stop that has flipped. The structure changes before the price does.

8. The seller reads the trend by its structure, not by his hope. A higher low held is a market that has not changed its mind. A higher low broken is a market that has, whatever the last candle says.

9. When the ATR trail flips, the trend has spoken. Sell puts beneath a market that ratchets up. Sell calls above a market that ratchets down. Sell neither while it is deciding.

10. Dust rising in a high column is the sign of chariots; dust low and wide, of infantry. Volume surging at a level is the sign of a wall; volume dying into a move is the sign of a move that has run out of enemy.

11. If the enemy's troops march up angrily and remain facing ours for a long time without either joining battle or taking themselves off, the situation demands great vigilance. A market that presses your line for an hour and does not break it is the most dangerous market there is, because it looks like safety.

12. If soldiers are punished before they have grown attached to you, they will not prove submissive. If you cut a spread before it has ever been allowed to work, you will learn nothing and collect nothing. Let the position breathe inside the fourth quarter; cut it at the line you chose, not at the first breath.

13. He who exercises no forethought but makes light of his opponents is sure to be captured by them.

## Commentary

The army on the march is the chapter about reading signs, and the seller's signs are structural. Direction is not read from a feeling or a headline but from the market's own pivots: whether the last swing low held or broke, whether the character of the swing has changed, whether a trailing stop that ratchets with volatility has flipped from one side of price to the other. This is the read that decides which side is sold. Bull sells puts. Bear sells calls. Undecided sells nothing and waits, which is also a position.

High ground and river crossings have exact counterparts. The high ground is distance from price, placed beyond the level where dealer hedging leans — the wall that pins a rising market or the floor that catches a falling one. The river is a level just crossed, and the general's advice to get far from it is the seller's rule never to sell the strike the market has just broken through; it returns to test what it broke. The marsh is the chop between two levels, and no line inside it is defensible.

The sign that matters most is the quiet one. A market pressing against your line without breaking it is not evidence the line holds; it is the enemy standing close at hand and remaining quiet, relying on the strength of his position. Vigilance here means having already decided what the cut price is, so that when the quiet ends you are executing a decision rather than making one.


## Part IV: Ground and Fire

The last four chapters are the hardest ground. Terrain that must not be fought on. The nine situations an army can find itself in, and what each demands. Fire, the weapon that destroys faster than any sword. And finally spies — because the general who knows wins before he moves.

For the seller: the regimes to refuse, the stages of a trade from first entry to the desperate ground where only the stop remains, volatility as fire, and the journal as the only spy who has never lied to you.


### 10. Terrain

1. We may distinguish six kinds of terrain: accessible ground, entangling ground, temporising ground, narrow passes, precipitous heights, and positions at a great distance from the enemy.

2. The market has six kinds of ground, and the seller must know which he is standing on before he sells a line.

3. Accessible ground is the clean trend: pivots holding, structure intact, volatility calm. On accessible ground, sell in the direction of the trend, beneath it or above it, and let the trend defend you.

4. Entangling ground can be abandoned but is hard to re-occupy. It is the range that looks like a trend for an hour. The seller who sells a directional line here is trapped when the range reasserts; the condor was the right shape from the start, and only on this ground is it sold without being hurt first.

5. Temporising ground is when neither side gains by making the first move. It is the hour before the number, the day before the decision. On temporising ground, do not sell. Whoever moves first is charged for the information the other side is about to receive for free.

6. Narrow passes are the event days: the print, the meeting, the expiry of the giant. If you can occupy them first — sell after the event, into the collapse of volatility — do so. If the enemy holds the pass — sell before the event, into the spike — do not follow him in, however rich the credit looks.

7. Precipitous heights are the gap: the market that has opened far from where it closed. Occupy the sunny side — sell the range the gap has made and the market must now travel far to unmake — and do not chase the gap itself.

8. Positions at a great distance are far expiries. The credit is larger, the wage is slower, and the enemy has more days to arrive. The seller who does not know why he is far from the enemy will find that distance is not safety; it is time, and time is the market's ally too when the line is far off.

9. These six are the principles connected with ground. The general who has attained a responsible post must be careful to study them.

10. When troops flee, are insubordinate, collapse, are disorganised, or are routed, it must be due to natural causes in the general. So with the seller: when a spread is chased, held, doubled, or abandoned in fear, the cause is not the ground. It is the Commander, who did not know which ground he was on.

11. The natural formation of the ground is the soldier's best ally; but a power of estimating the adversary, of controlling the forces of victory, and of shrewdly calculating difficulties, dangers and distances, constitutes the test of a great general.

12. If fighting is sure to result in victory, then you must fight, even though the ruler forbid it. If the count says sell and the mood says wait, sell. If the count says wait and the credit tempts you, wait. The credit is the ruler; the count is the general.

13. Know the enemy and know yourself, and your victory will not stand in doubt. Know the ground and know the sky, and you may make your victory complete.

## Commentary

Terrain is a chapter about refusal. Of the six grounds, the seller fights well on one — the clean trend — and adequately on two, the range and the post-gap market, provided he changes shape to suit them. The other three are grounds to leave alone: the hour before a scheduled number, the volatility spike into an event, and a far expiry sold without a reason. A trader who sells every day has decided that all six grounds are the same, and the market will charge him tuition on the three that are not.

The entangling ground deserves the closest reading, because it is where the condor is honestly sold at the open. Everywhere else in this book the second side is a hedge, sold only when the first side is hurt, because on trending ground a scheduled condor is a bet against the trend with twice the risk. But a market that has proven itself a range — that has already turned back from both edges, more than once, on ordinary volume — has told the seller what shape to take. The rule is not "never sell both sides at once." The rule is "know why you are."

The last verse is the summary of the book so far: yourself, the enemy, the ground and the sky. The Commander's discipline; the market's ordinary flatness and exceptional direction; the six grounds and which to refuse; and time, which decays in the seller's favour every hour he is not fighting it.


### 11. The Nine Situations

1. The art of war recognises nine varieties of ground. The seller's trade passes through nine, from the moment the line is chosen to the moment there is nothing left but the stop.

2. Dispersive ground is the open, when a trader fights in his own territory: full of opinion, full of capital, and most likely to spend both at once. On dispersive ground, do not fight. Count, and place one quarter.

3. Facile ground is the first hour after the line is set, when the position is shallow and nothing has been tested. On facile ground, do not halt to admire the credit. It is not yours yet.

4. Contentious ground is the level both sides want: the wall, the prior high, the round number. Do not attack it. Sell beyond it, and let the market fight for the ground you did not take.

5. Open ground is a trending, liquid tape where each side can move freely. On open ground, do not try to block the enemy's way; sell with the trend and let it pass.

6. Intersecting ground is the news, where three parties meet: the market, the event, and the crowd. On intersecting ground, join hands with your allies. The seller's allies are the wing and the fourth quarter. Do not sell here without both.

7. Serious ground is the middle of the trade, deep in the enemy's country, the credit half-earned and the line untested. On serious ground, gather in plunder: let theta pay. Do not add. Do not fidget. Do not leave early out of nerves, and do not stay late out of greed.

8. Difficult ground is the fluctuation: the tape moving toward the line without yet reaching it, the loss on the screen larger than the credit but smaller than the cut. On difficult ground, keep steadily on the march. This is what the fourth quarter was for.

9. Hemmed-in ground is the broken spread: the short strike breached, the loss at the credit, the reason for the trade gone. On hemmed-in ground, resort to stratagem — the hedge, if the move is fresh; the cut, if it is not. Do not stand still.

10. Desperate ground is the maximum loss, where only the width remains. On desperate ground, fight: cut the short leg, keep the wing, and let the wing fight for you. The seller who arrives on desperate ground with the short leg still open has already refused every earlier ground's advice.

11. The skilful tactician may be likened to the snake: strike its head and you will be attacked by its tail; strike its tail and you will be attacked by its head. The condor is the snake. Hurt one side and the other side is paid; run through one line and the wing beyond it becomes a long.

12. Throw your soldiers into positions whence there is no escape, and they will prefer death to flight. Set the stop in the temple, where there is no tape to argue with. Then, when the stop is reached, there is no decision to make, and a seller with no decision to make cannot make the wrong one.

13. It is the business of a general to be quiet and thus ensure secrecy; upright and just, and thus maintain order. It is the business of the seller to be quiet at the open, just to himself at the cut, and to keep order in the four quarters when the day turns four times.

14. At first, then, exhibit the coyness of a maiden, until the enemy gives you an opening; afterwards emulate the rapidity of a running hare, and it will be too late for the enemy to oppose you. Wait for the line to be offered at a price. Then cut, when cutting is due, faster than the tape can charge you for waiting.

## Commentary

The nine situations are read here as the nine stages of a single trade, and the reading holds surprisingly well. The open is dispersive ground — the trader is at home, fully armed, and the danger is that he spends everything on the first opinion. The middle of a working trade is serious ground, where the only instruction is to let the plunder come in: theta pays a seller who is not wrong, hour by hour, and the discipline is simply to do nothing. Difficult ground is the fluctuation, which every position passes through before it resolves; the fourth quarter is the reserve that lets it be endured.

Hemmed-in and desperate ground are the two the seller must have already decided about. Hemmed-in is the broken line, and the answer is a stratagem chosen for the situation: a hedge into the other side if the move is young, a cut of the short leg if the move has already run. Desperate ground — the maximum loss — is where the old general says to fight, and the seller's fight is precise: buy back the short leg, keep the wing, and let the long option that the spread always contained ride the very move that broke the line. It is the difference between exiting a trade and letting the trade exit you.

"Throw your soldiers into positions whence there is no escape" is the least intuitive line in the chapter and the most important. The seller does not set his stop on the tape, where every price argues with it. He sets it in the temple, before the order, where it is an arithmetic and not a feeling. When the market reaches it, there is nothing to decide, and the absence of a decision is the whole of the discipline.


### 12. Fire

1. There are five ways of attacking with fire. The first is to burn soldiers in their camp; the second is to burn stores; the third is to burn baggage trains; the fourth is to burn arsenals and magazines; the fifth is to hurl dropping fire amongst the enemy.

2. Volatility is fire. There are five ways the fire reaches the seller, and he must know each by its smoke.

3. The first burns the camp: the gap at the open, when the line set last night is already behind the price. There is no defence in the position; the defence was the size.

4. The second burns the stores: the slow bleed of a chopping market that crosses the line, retreats, and crosses again, charging a cut each time. This fire does not kill. It starves.

5. The third burns the baggage: the margin call, when a spread's marked loss exceeds the fourth quarter and the broker closes what the seller would have held. It is the fire the seller lights himself, by sizing for the outcome he hoped for.

6. The fourth burns the arsenal: the volatility spike that makes every strike expensive at once, so the hedge that was cheap in the morning costs half the credit in the afternoon. The seller who did not hedge when hedging was cheap has lost his arsenal to the same fire that made him need it.

7. The fifth is dropping fire: the news that arrives while the position is open, a headline in the middle of serious ground. Against this there is only the wing.

8. In order to carry out an attack with fire, the means must always be ready. In order to survive one, the means must be ready sooner: the stop set in the temple, the wing already bought, the fourth quarter untouched.

9. When fire breaks out inside the enemy's camp, respond at once with an attack from without. When volatility breaks out inside your position, respond at once: the hedge into reversal if the move is young, the cut of the short if it is not. Fire waits for no one, and the seller who waits to see if it spreads is the one it spreads through.

10. Unhappy is the fate of one who tries to win his battles without cultivating the spirit of enterprise; for the result is waste of time and general stagnation. Unhappier is the seller who sells every day without cultivating the discipline of the stop; for the result is a hundred wages and one fire that takes them all.

11. Move not unless you see an advantage; use not your troops unless there is something to be gained; fight not unless the position is critical. Sell not unless the count says sell. Hedge not unless hurt. Cut not unless the line is broken — and then, cut.

12. No ruler should put troops into the field merely to gratify his own spleen; no general should fight a battle simply out of pique. No seller should sell because he is angry at the market for the last trade. Anger has never been a read.

13. A kingdom that has once been destroyed can never come again into being; nor can the dead ever be brought back to life. An account that has been halved needs a double to return, and one that has lost four-fifths needs five times itself. Every large loss does not merely subtract; it resets the base that everything afterward multiplies.

14. Hence the enlightened ruler is heedful, and the good general full of caution. This is the way to keep an account at peace and a sequence intact.

## Commentary

The chapter on fire is the general's chapter on the weapon that does not care about skill, and for the seller that weapon is volatility. It arrives as a gap that puts the line behind the price before the first order can be placed; as a spike that makes the hedge unaffordable in the exact hour it becomes necessary; as a headline in the middle of a working trade. None of these can be predicted. All of them can be survived, and survival is arranged in advance: the size that makes a gap an ordinary expense, the wing that is always present because the spread always contains one, the reserve that keeps the broker's hand off the position.

The two fires the seller lights himself deserve naming. The margin call is size. The starving fire — the chop that charges a cut, then a re-entry, then a cut — is frequency, the chapter on manoeuvring returned as a slow burn. Neither is the market's fault, and both are ended by the same two rules that were already on the page: size so that the maximum loss is boring, and give the day a structural ceiling on how many times it can open.

The last verses are the arithmetic of ruin, stated by a general who had seen kingdoms end. Minus fifty needs plus one hundred. Minus eighty needs plus four hundred. This is not a warning about markets; it is what multiplication does to a sequence with a hole in it. It is also why the stop — the subject of nearly every chapter — is the only rule in the book with no exceptions.


### 13. Spies

1. What enables the wise sovereign and the good general to strike and conquer, and achieve things beyond the reach of ordinary men, is foreknowledge.

2. Foreknowledge cannot be elicited from spirits; it cannot be obtained inductively from experience, nor by any deductive calculation. Knowledge of the enemy's dispositions can only be obtained from other men.

3. Forecasts are spirits. The seller does not consult them. He consults the market's dispositions — what it has done, where it is leaning, what it has priced — and these are obtained from the tape, not from the oracle.

4. There are five classes of spies: local spies, inward spies, converted spies, doomed spies, and surviving spies.

5. The seller has five spies, and he pays them differently.

6. Local spies are price itself: the pivots, the swings, the structure. They are cheap, they are everywhere, and they are honest. They tell you what the market has done and, by its shape, what it has decided.

7. Inward spies are volatility: the market's own officials, reporting what it fears. Implied volatility is the enemy's quartermaster telling you how much protection is being bought and at what price. Read him before every trade; he sets the wage.

8. Converted spies are positioning: the dealer's hedges, the walls of gamma, the levels where the enemy's own soldiers must buy or sell against their will. A converted spy works for the enemy and tells you where the enemy is forced to go. Of all five, he is the most valuable, and the one most traders never recruit.

9. Doomed spies are the news: information sent out to be believed, and to be acted upon by those who do not check it against the other four. Every headline is a doomed spy. Let him carry his message to the buyers.

10. Surviving spies are those who bring back news from the enemy's camp. The seller's surviving spy is his own journal: the record of every trade, the reason it was taken, the price at which it was cut or held, and what happened after. He is the only spy who has never lied, and he is the only one most traders refuse to keep.

11. Hence it is that with none in the whole army are more intimate relations maintained than with spies; none should be more liberally rewarded. Reward the journal with honesty. Write the trade down before it is over. Write the reason, not the outcome.

12. Spies cannot be usefully employed without a certain intuitive sagacity; they cannot be properly managed without benevolence and straightforwardness. Nor can a journal be usefully kept by a man who edits it to feel better.

13. Whether the object be to crush an army, to storm a city, or to assassinate an individual, it is always necessary to begin by finding out the names of the attendants and the sentries. Before the line is sold, find out the names: the expected move, the wall, the floor, the event, the volatility rank. These are the sentries. The trade that begins without them begins blind.

14. Hence it is only the enlightened ruler and the wise general who will use the highest intelligence of the army for purposes of spying, and thereby they achieve great results. Spies are a most important element in trading, because upon them depends the seller's ability to move at all.

15. The seller who has sold a thousand lines and written down none of them has fought a thousand battles and learned the lesson of one. The seller who has written down a hundred has a spy in every future camp.

## Commentary

The general ends with spies because everything before — the count, the ground, the fire — depends on knowing, and knowing comes from people rather than from prayer. The seller's translation is that forecasts are the spirits the old text forbids consulting, and the market's dispositions are the men. Price tells him what has been decided. Volatility tells him what protection costs and therefore what he will be paid. Positioning tells him where the other side is forced to act against its will, which is the single most useful fact a seller can own about a line. The news tells the buyers what to think, and he lets it.

The surviving spy is the chapter's real subject. A journal that records the reason for every trade, at the time it was taken, is the only instrument that can tell a seller whether his losses came from bad ground, bad size, or the Commander's five faults. Without it he remembers his best trades and his worst, and nothing in between, and the pattern that is killing him is always in between. It must be written before the outcome is known, and it must be kept by a man willing to read it.

The last verse is the book's own sentence about itself. Thirteen chapters, in a general's order, on a single trade: count before you fight; know the cost of a campaign; win without needing to be right; be unbeatable before you look for a chance; divide your force in four; respond, do not predict; march slowly and cut fast; know the roads not to take; read the signs; refuse the wrong ground; know which of the nine situations you are in; survive fire; and keep the spy who never lies. None of it will make you right. It makes being wrong survivable, and survival is the only thing that compounds.
