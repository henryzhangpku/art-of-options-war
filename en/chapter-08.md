# 8. Variation

*Part III: The March*

1. There are roads which must not be followed, armies which must not be attacked, towns which must not be besieged, positions which must not be contested, commands of the sovereign which must not be obeyed.

2. There are trades which must not be taken. There are lines which must not be defended. There are losses which must not be held. There are signals which must not be followed, however good the source. There are opinions of your own which must not be obeyed.

3. The general who thoroughly understands the advantages that accompany variation of tactics knows how to handle his troops. He who does not understand these may be well acquainted with the ground, and yet not be able to turn his knowledge to account.

4. Ground is the setup. Variation is what you do when the setup is wrong. Most traders study ground for years and variation for never.

5. The wise seller considers both advantage and disadvantage in one thought. In the advantage — the credit — he keeps the disadvantage in view: the four he can lose. In the disadvantage — the stop — he keeps the advantage in view: the sequence it protects.

6. The art of war teaches us to rely not on the likelihood of the enemy's not coming, but on our own readiness to receive him. The art of selling teaches us to rely not on the likelihood that the line holds, but on the plan for the day it does not.

7. There are five dangerous faults which may affect a general: recklessness, cowardice, a hasty temper, a delicacy of honour, and over-solicitude for his men.

8. There are five dangerous faults which may affect a seller. Recklessness, which sells too wide and too much. Cowardice, which closes a working spread at the first fluctuation and never collects. A hasty temper, which trades back at the market that hurt it. A delicacy of honour, which will not take a small loss because a small loss admits a small error. And over-solicitude for the position, which holds a broken line out of loyalty to the morning's opinion.

9. These are the five besetting sins of a seller, ruinous to the conduct of a campaign. When an account is overturned and its trader slain, the cause will surely be found among these five.

10. Of the five, the fourth is the deadliest. The trader who cannot cut does not have a strategy. He has a hostage.

11. Therefore the rule for variation is one rule: when the reason for the trade is gone, the trade is gone. Not the money — the reason. The money follows.

## Commentary

The chapter on variation is the general's warning that rules without exceptions are for men who do not intend to survive contact. The seller's exceptions are the roads not to follow: the event day when the expected move is a lie, the line that would be right if only the wall had not moved overnight, the signal that arrives from a trusted source and is wrong anyway. Knowing the setup is the ground; knowing when the setup no longer applies is the variation, and it is the harder study.

The five faults translate almost without effort, and the translation is unkind. Recklessness is size. Cowardice is the seller who never lets a credit mature. Hasty temper is revenge trading — selling back at the market within minutes of a cut, on no read at all. Delicacy of honour is the deadliest because it is invisible: it feels like conviction, and it is nothing but the refusal to be wrong at a size that would not matter. Over-solicitude is the loyalty a trader feels toward the opinion he had at 9:31, long after the market has stopped sharing it.

The single rule of variation is that the reason ends the trade, not the price. A spread sold because the trend was up, in a market whose structure has just broken to the downside, has lost its reason whether or not it has yet lost money. Wait for the money to confirm what the reason already said and you will have waited exactly as long as the market needs to charge you for it.
