# 9. The March

*Part III: The March*

## The Seller

1. Camp in high places, facing the sun. Do not climb heights in order to fight. So much for mountain warfare.

2. Sell your line on the high ground: beyond the wall where dealer hedging leans against price, on the side of the market that must climb to reach you. Do not sell into a climb to prove you can. So much for placing a strike.

3. After crossing a river, get far away from it. Do not linger near the level the market has just crossed; a level just broken is the one it comes back to test.

4. In crossing salt marshes, your sole concern should be to get over them quickly. The chop between two levels is a marsh. Do not sell a line inside it; the seller in the marsh is nibbled from both sides and paid for neither.

5. All armies prefer high ground to low, and sunny places to dark. For the seller, high ground is distance from price and sunny places are calm volatility. The low ground and the dark belong to the other bank of the river, where the buyer camps on purpose.

6. When the enemy is close at hand and remains quiet, he is relying on the natural strength of his position. When the tape is quiet near your line, it is not friendship. It is a market gathering.

7. Movement amongst the trees of a forest shows the enemy is advancing. The rise of birds in flight is the sign of an ambuscade. The market's signs are the same: a pivot taken out, a change of character in the swing, a trailing stop that has flipped. The structure changes before the price does.

8. The seller reads the trend by its structure, not by his hope. A higher low held is a market that has not changed its mind. A higher low broken is a market that has, whatever the last candle says.

9. When the ATR trail flips, the trend has spoken. Sell puts beneath a market that ratchets up. Sell calls above a market that ratchets down. Sell neither while it is deciding.

10. Dust rising in a high column is the sign of chariots; dust low and wide, of infantry. Volume surging at a level is the sign of a wall; volume dying into a move is the sign of a move that has run out of enemy.

11. If the enemy's troops march up angrily and remain facing ours for a long time without either joining battle or taking themselves off, the situation demands great vigilance. A market that presses your line for an hour and does not break it is the most dangerous market there is, because it looks like safety.

12. If soldiers are punished before they have grown attached to you, they will not prove submissive. If you cut a spread before it has ever been allowed to work, you will learn nothing and collect nothing. Let the position breathe inside the fourth quarter; cut it at the line you chose, not at the first breath.

13. The seller marches by the clock. He is paid first and pays claims later. The money held in between is float, the oldest advantage of the insurer.

14. When a spread is sold, the credit arrives that morning. It is not yet profit. It is premium held in trust for a policy that has not ended, and it is earned only as the term passes. The decay of an option is the insurer's earning, hour by hour, of money he already holds.

15. Do not count unearned premium as income, and do not size the next policy on it. Float is not capital.

16. The last days of a term earn the most premium and carry the sharpest claims. Near the end, a policy is all reward or all claim within hours. Decide in the temple which policies are held to the end and which are closed before it; in the last afternoon there is no time left to decide.

17. He who exercises no forethought but makes light of his opponents is sure to be captured by them.

## The Buyer

18. The buyer camps in the low places and waits for the sun. His ground is calm volatility that is about to stop being calm.

19. The buyer reads the same signs as the seller and acts on the opposite ones. A pivot broken is the seller's warning and the buyer's invitation.

20. When the ATR trail flips, the buyer may follow it: calls behind a market that has turned up, puts behind one that has turned down. Neither while it is deciding; deciding is where rent is paid for nothing.

21. Volume dying into a quiet range is the sign of a coiled market. That is the buyer's high ground: the move nobody is paying for yet.

22. A market that presses a level for an hour without breaking it is the seller's danger and the buyer's patience. He does not buy the press. He buys the break, small, with his stop at the level.

23. *Beat the grass to startle the snake* (第13计 打草惊蛇). The beater strikes the grass, not the snake. The rustle tells him where the snake was, and the snake, startled, tells him more than any map.

24. A paper trade is grass beaten at no price. Thirty to fifty forward paper trades on one setup, each written down before money touches it, will show the ground: whether the strike fills, how wide the spread sits at the open, whether the move arrives on the day.

25. The first probe with money is the smallest position the market will carry. A probe that costs a real share of the risk budget is not a probe. It is a position wearing a probe's coat.

26. The seller's float is the buyer's bill. Every day the seller earns a little of what he holds, the buyer pays a little of what he paid. The buyer marches by the catalyst; the clock only charges him.

## Commentary

The army on the march is the chapter about reading signs, and the seller's signs are structural. Direction is not read from a feeling or a headline but from the market's own pivots: whether the last swing low held or broke, whether the character of the swing has changed, whether a trailing stop that ratchets with volatility has flipped from one side of price to the other. This is the read that decides which side is sold. Bull sells puts. Bear sells calls. Undecided sells nothing and waits, which is also a position.

High ground and river crossings have exact counterparts. The high ground is distance from price, placed beyond the level where dealer hedging leans: the wall that pins a rising market or the floor that catches a falling one. The river is a level just crossed, and the general's advice to get far from it is the seller's rule never to sell the strike the market has just broken through; it returns to test what it broke. The marsh is the chop between two levels, and no line inside it is defensible. The low ground is not a mistake; it is a different camp. The first edition called a seller on the low ground a buyer who had not noticed. Better to say it plainly: the low ground is where the buyer camps on purpose, and a seller found there has wandered into the other army's country.

The sign that matters most is the quiet one. A market pressing against your line without breaking it is not evidence the line holds; it is the enemy standing close at hand and remaining quiet, relying on the strength of his position. Vigilance here means having already decided what the cut price is, so that when the quiet ends you are executing a decision rather than making one.

The insurer marches by a different sign, the clock. Float is why great insurers have often been great investors: they hold large sums of other people's premium for long stretches before the claims come due. The seller's float is small and short, a credit held for days or weeks, but the principle and the trap are the same. The credit sitting in the account looks like profit. Until the policy expires, it is a liability with a balance attached. A spread sold for one with ten days to expiry has not made one on the first day; it has made whatever the clock has released, and the rest is held against a claim. Treating the whole credit as earned is how a book ends up sizing new policies on money it may have to give back. And because decay quickens near expiry, the final days release the most premium at the same moment that price near the strike swings the claim hardest. The seller is paid by the clock. The trader wants to be paid by the move. They are different trades.

The buyer reads the same signs from the other bank of the river. The quiet market the seller treats as safety, the buyer treats as stored energy: realised volatility falling, volume drying up, a range narrowing toward a dated event. That is where options are cheapest, and where a buyer pays the least rent for the most possible movement.

The thirteenth stratagem is usually read as a warning not to alert the enemy. For the buyer it is better read as a method. The snake is the market's behaviour around a setup, and the only way to see it is to disturb the grass and watch. The disturbance must be cheap, or it is not observation but commitment. Paper is the cheapest grass, and thirty to fifty forward trades is not a magic number: it is large enough that a single lucky week does not become a method, and small enough that a working setup is not buried under a decade of waiting. The first money is a probe at minimum size. What must not happen is the probe growing before the count is complete. A probe that becomes a position has stopped asking questions.

The signs of a turning trend are the same signs for both. When the structure breaks, the higher low lost and the trail flipped, the seller moves his line and the buyer takes his entry. Neither acts while the market is deciding. The seller is not paid there, and the buyer pays rent there for nothing.
