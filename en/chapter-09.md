# 9. The March

*Part III: The March*

1. Camp in high places, facing the sun. Do not climb heights in order to fight. So much for mountain warfare.

2. Sell your line on the high ground: beyond the wall where dealer hedging leans against price, on the side of the market that must climb to reach you. Do not sell into a climb to prove you can. So much for placing a strike.

3. After crossing a river, get far away from it. Do not linger near the level the market has just crossed; a level just broken is the one it comes back to test.

4. In crossing salt marshes, your sole concern should be to get over them quickly. The chop between two levels is a marsh. Do not sell a line inside it; the seller in the marsh is nibbled from both sides and paid for neither.

5. All armies prefer high ground to low, and sunny places to dark. High ground is distance from price; sunny places are calm volatility. A seller who prefers the low ground and the dark is a buyer who has not yet noticed.

6. When the enemy is close at hand and remains quiet, he is relying on the natural strength of his position. When the tape is quiet near your line, it is not friendship. It is a market gathering.

7. Movement amongst the trees of a forest shows the enemy is advancing. The rise of birds in flight is the sign of an ambuscade. The market's signs are the same: a pivot taken out, a change of character in the swing, a trailing stop that has flipped. The structure changes before the price does.

8. The seller reads the trend by its structure, not by his hope. A higher low held is a market that has not changed its mind. A higher low broken is a market that has, whatever the last candle says.

9. When the ATR trail flips, the trend has spoken. Sell puts beneath a market that ratchets up. Sell calls above a market that ratchets down. Sell neither while it is deciding.

10. Dust rising in a high column is the sign of chariots; dust low and wide, of infantry. Volume surging at a level is the sign of a wall; volume dying into a move is the sign of a move that has run out of enemy.

11. If the enemy's troops march up angrily and remain facing ours for a long time without either joining battle or taking themselves off, the situation demands great vigilance. A market that presses your line for an hour and does not break it is the most dangerous market there is, because it looks like safety.

12. If soldiers are punished before they have grown attached to you, they will not prove submissive. If you cut a spread before it has ever been allowed to work, you will learn nothing and collect nothing. Let the position breathe inside the fourth quarter; cut it at the line you chose, not at the first breath.

13. He who exercises no forethought but makes light of his opponents is sure to be captured by them.

## Commentary

The army on the march is the chapter about reading signs, and the seller's signs are structural. Direction is not read from a feeling or a headline but from the market's own pivots: whether the last swing low held or broke, whether the character of the swing has changed, whether a trailing stop that ratchets with volatility has flipped from one side of price to the other. This is the read that decides which side is sold. Bull sells puts. Bear sells calls. Undecided sells nothing and waits, which is also a position.

High ground and river crossings have exact counterparts. The high ground is distance from price, placed beyond the level where dealer hedging leans — the wall that pins a rising market or the floor that catches a falling one. The river is a level just crossed, and the general's advice to get far from it is the seller's rule never to sell the strike the market has just broken through; it returns to test what it broke. The marsh is the chop between two levels, and no line inside it is defensible.

The sign that matters most is the quiet one. A market pressing against your line without breaking it is not evidence the line holds; it is the enemy standing close at hand and remaining quiet, relying on the strength of his position. Vigilance here means having already decided what the cut price is, so that when the quiet ends you are executing a decision rather than making one.
