# 14. The Two Banks

*Part V: Attack and Defence*

## The Buyer

1. When an invading force crosses a river in its onward march, do not advance to meet it in mid-stream. It will be best to let half the army get across, and then deliver your attack.

2. Every option has two owners, and every premium is paid by one to the other. The seller stands on one bank and the buyer on the other, and the river between them is the price of fire.

3. The buyer's bank is low and wet. He pays first, he pays every day, and he is paid rarely. Let him know the ground he stands on before he calls it his.

4. Each side forages on the other. The seller lives on the buyer's need for protection and his love of direction. The buyer lives on the seller's comfort: the long calm after which protection is priced as if nothing will ever happen again.

5. The line of battle is not a strike. It is a number: the move the option charges for, set against the move the market goes on to deliver. Above the line, the defender is paid. Below it, the attacker is.

6. Implied volatility is the quartermaster's price for the march. Realised volatility is the road. When the quartermaster charges for a calmer road than the one the army is on, the attacker has his opening. When he charges for a rougher one, the attacker is paying the defender's wage.

7. Most days, in most names, the quartermaster charges for the rougher road. That gap is the seller's wage and therefore the buyer's tax. The buyer who forgets it crosses the river at the deepest point.

8. Measure the road before you cross it. The road is measured in daily moves, not in feelings about them. Square each day's move, take the average, take the root: that is the pace. Multiply it by the square root of the trading days in a year, about sixteen, and it is in the same units as the quartermaster's price.

9. Ten days are a sounding, not a survey. The road measured yesterday is the road behind the army, and the river can rise or fall the morning after the buyer pays. The measurement does not say he will be paid. It says whether he is paying the seller's wage or the seller is paying his.

10. Wait until half the army is across. The time to attack is not when the river is loudest but when the seller has committed to the calm, priced it, and grown comfortable on the far bank.

11. The buyer of a spread also sells. Every debit spread is a long option and a short one, and the short one is a small piece of the seller's trade bought into the buyer's ticket. He is never wholly on one bank.

12. A trader chooses a bank for a price, not for a career. "I am a buyer" is a sentence about identity. "Today the fire is cheap" is a sentence about the river, and only the second can be checked.

## The Seller

13. The seller reads the same river from the high bank. He is paid when the quartermaster's price is above the road, and he must check that it is before he collects.

14. The seller's spread is itself a short option and a long one. Every seller in this book has been a buyer since Chapter 4, through the wing. He stands on his bank with one foot in the water.

15. When the river runs below the line, when implied sits beneath what the market is delivering, the seller is not being paid to stand. He is paying to stand. On that day his bank is the low one, whatever he calls himself.

16. The defender who never measures the river defends by habit. Habit is not a stance. It is the absence of one, and the market charges it the attacker's price.

17. Let half the enemy across, and then strike: the seller's version is to let the panic buyers cross first, paying the spike, and to write his cover after they have, at the price they set.

18. Two banks, one river, one price. A trader who knows which bank he is on today, and why, has already done most of the general's work.

## Commentary

The general's thirteen chapters are done. They taught the seller to make himself unbeatable and the buyer to survive his own arithmetic. They did not answer the general's own question, the one he set in the chapter on the unbeatable position: when to defend, and when to attack. Part V answers it. Seller and buyer are not two trades. They are the two banks of one river, and the option's price is the water between them. When the price of fire is high, the defender is paid to stand. When it is low, the attacker is paid to cross. The last five chapters are about reading the water, and in them the buyer speaks first, reversing the order of the thirteen, so that the structure of the book makes its argument.

Every option is a transfer. Before costs, what one side gains on a contract the other loses, and both pay the house for the privilege: the spread, the commission, the slippage. So the question "is buying or selling better" has no answer in general. It has an answer only at a price. At most prices, on most days, the answer has been the seller, because buyers of protection have usually paid more than the moves they were protected against turned out to cost. That gap is a habit of the people who need insurance, not a law. When the habit breaks, after a long calm has lulled the sellers into pricing tomorrow as if it were yesterday, the answer turns.

The line of battle can be checked with a pencil. Take ten days of a stock near 100 whose daily moves, in percent, were: +1.2, −0.8, +1.5, −1.9, +0.4, −1.1, +2.0, −0.6, +1.3, −1.0. Square each one: 1.44, 0.64, 2.25, 3.61, 0.16, 1.21, 4.00, 0.36, 1.69, 1.00. They add up to 16.36. The average is about 1.64, and its square root is about 1.28. That is the pace of the road: the stock has been moving about 1.28% on a typical day. Multiply by the square root of 252 trading days, which is just under 16, and the pace is about 20% a year. That is realised volatility, the road.

Now read the quartermaster. Suppose the options on the same stock, a month out, are priced at an implied volatility of 16%. Divide by 16 and the option is charging for a daily move of about 1%. The road has been delivering 1.28%. Over twenty trading days the difference compounds into the price of a straddle: the square root of 20 over 252 is about 0.28, so the move the option charges for is about 100 × 0.16 × 0.28, or 4.5, and the at-the-money straddle costs roughly 0.8 of that, about 3.6. At the road's pace of 20%, the same arithmetic gives a typical move of about 5.7 and a straddle worth about 4.6. The quartermaster is charging for a road about a fifth calmer than the one the army is on. On this river, today, the buyer is on the high bank.

Three honest limits belong in the same breath. First, ten days is a sounding. A real comparison uses more days, and more than one window, and asks whether the last ten were ordinary for this name or a storm that has already passed. Second, the road is the past. Realised volatility is a spy who reports what has happened, and a market moving fast can go quiet the morning after the buyer pays. Third, the comparison is between like and like: an option that spans an earnings date is charging for that date, and a road measured on ordinary days will make it look dear when it is only accurate. None of this makes the measurement useless. It makes it a measurement rather than a prophecy, which is the only kind of edge this book recognises.

Reverse the numbers and the same pencil gives the seller's wage. If the stock has been moving at 12% a year and the option charges 16%, the quartermaster is charging for a road a third rougher than the one being marched. That is the ordinary state of the river, and it is why the seller's half of this book was written first. The buyer who pays the average price, on average, pays it.

"Let half the army get across" is the general's advice on timing at a river, and it reads well from both banks. The attacker does not cross when the water is loudest, during the spike, when everyone who is late is bidding for the same right. He crosses when the seller has committed himself to the calm and is standing comfortable on the far bank, half his army over. The defender has his own version. He lets the panic buyers cross first, paying the spike, and writes his cover after they have, at the price their fear set.

The last verses end the false choice that the first edition left open. A trader who says "I am a seller" or "I am a buyer" has described himself, not the river. Every spread in this book is both: the seller's credit spread is a short option with a long wing behind it, and the buyer's debit spread is a long option that sells its far wing to pay for itself. The question is never which bank to be born on. It is which bank the price puts you on today, and whether you have measured it. The next four chapters take the four answers in turn: when to attack, why most attacks fail, how to buy for defence, and when to change sides.
