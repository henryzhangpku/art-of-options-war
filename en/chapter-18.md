# 18. The Turn of the Wheel

*Part V: Attack and Defence*

## The Buyer

1. The direct and the indirect lead on to each other in turn. It is like moving in a circle: you never come to an end. Who can exhaust the possibilities of their combination?

2. Attack and defence lead on to each other in the same circle. The buyer's season ends in the seller's, and the seller's in the buyer's, and neither season is announced.

3. Just as water retains no constant shape, so in warfare there are no constant conditions. The price of fire has no constant level. The trader who keeps one stance through every season has chosen a shape for water.

4. The wheel is read, not forecast. The trader does not say what the river will do. He says what it is doing today, measured, and which bank that puts him on.

5. Long calm, with the price of fire starting to rise from a low base: the attacker's season. The sellers have priced the calm as permanent, and the first sign that it is not costs them more than it costs anyone.

6. High fire that has peaked and begun to fall: the defender's season. The buyers who arrived in the panic have paid for the storm in full, and the storm is leaving.

7. The hour before the number: neither. Whoever moves first is charged for the information the other side is about to receive for free. Stand on neither bank. Watch from across the river.

8. *Make the host and the guest exchange roles* (第30计 反客为主). The guest comes in by the side door, stays for supper, and by morning is answering the door as host.

9. In the trade the guest is the process: the calendar, the five sentences, the size, the time stop, the journal. It arrives as a visitor to one position and must end as the master of the house. When the process keeps the house, the trade is a guest. When the trade keeps the house, the process has been shown the door.

10. The turn of the wheel is not a mood. It is a decision the journal makes. Count the season: how many attacks, how many paid, what the losers cost, what the tax was. When the count says the attacker is no longer paid, the attacker turns.

11. Change sides in the temple, not on the tape. A change of stance decided while a position is losing is not a turn of the wheel. It is a revenge trade wearing the other army's uniform.

12. The one rule that does not turn: never hold both stances on the same direction. A long call and a short put on one name are one bet. A long wing and a credit sold the same way are one bet. Count the net direction, whatever the season.

## The Seller

13. The aim is not a good year. It is a company still writing policies in twenty years.

14. Insurance moves in cycles. In the soft market, premiums are thin and everyone writes. In the hard market, after the storm, premiums are rich and few can write.

15. In the soft market, write less. Thin premium is not an opportunity; it is the cheapest the risk will ever be sold, which is the reason not to sell much of it. The company that grows fastest in the soft market is the one that fails first in the hard one.

16. In the hard market, write carefully, at good prices, with the reserve the soft market taught you to keep.

17. The insurer's soft market is the buyer's season, and the insurer's hard market is the seller's. The wheel the underwriter has watched for three centuries is the same wheel this part of the book has been reading.

18. Keep the rules in writing: the size, the limit, the coasts, the reserve, the claim rule, the events excluded. Rules held in memory change with the mood. Rules written down change only by decision.

19. Review the journal on a schedule, not after a loss. A review after a loss is an argument. A review on the calendar is underwriting. Change a rule only with evidence from the journal, and never on the day it was broken.

20. Most of the work is declining business. Most of the profit is premium on policies that were never claimed. Most of the survival is the size of the ones that were.

21. A trader is measured by his best trade. An insurer is measured by his worst year, and by whether he was still open the year after.

## Commentary

The book began with a general counting in a temple, and it ends with a wheel. The general's chapter on energy has the image: the direct and the indirect leading to each other like a circle without end. The thirteen chapters read it for the seller's day, the trend spread and the hedge turning into each other four times between the open and the close. Part V reads it for the seasons. Attack and defence are the direct and the indirect of the whole trade, and they turn into each other as the price of fire rises and falls.

The three regimes are readings, not forecasts. A long calm in which implied volatility has started to rise from a low base is a river measured, not predicted: the sellers have been comfortable a long time, the price of protection is low, and the cost of being early to the end of the calm is small. That is the attacker's season, and it is the soft market of the insurer, when premiums are thin and every company writes. High volatility that has peaked and begun to fall is the reverse: the buyers who arrived in the panic paid for the storm, and the storm is leaving. That is the defender's season, and it is the insurer's hard market, when premiums are rich and only the companies that kept their reserves can write. The hour before a scheduled number is neither. On temporising ground, as Chapter 10 said, whoever moves first pays for information the other side will soon receive for free. None of these readings says what happens next. Each says which bank the price puts the trader on today, and whether the river has been measured.

The insurer's cycle and the trader's wheel are one wheel, seen by two trades over three centuries. Insurance runs in long soft stretches, when premium is cheap and competition fierce, broken by catastrophe years after which prices rise and capacity is scarce. The companies that survive the cycle are the ones that grew slowly in the soft years. The option seller who reads this book has the same instruction: write less in the long calm, when writing more feels safest; keep the reserve and the wings through it; and arrive at the hard market with something to sell. The buyer has the mirror instruction. The soft market is his season, and the reserve he kept through the seller's season, the cash not spent on the last ten ideas, is what lets him attack when it comes.

The thirtieth stratagem answers the question every trader with both stances must face: who decides when to turn? Not the trader on the tape, who has a position and a mood. The process decides. The guest who came in as a checklist must end as the host, governing every trade, or be shown the door the first time a trade is urgent. The weekly count is where the reversal is made or lost: how many trades, the expectancy after costs, the deepest drawdown, how many rules were broken. A week with a profit and three broken rules is not a good week for the process, whatever the statement says. Written beside the profit and loss, the broken rules show whether the result came from the method or from the departure. That is the journal, the surviving spy of Chapter 13, and it is the only one qualified to say whether a stance has been paid. The turn of the wheel is therefore a scheduled decision, made in the temple, on evidence, and never on the day a rule was broken.

One rule does not turn. A trader with one account and both stances must never hold both on the same direction. A long call and a short put on one name are one bet, however the legs are labelled. A long wing kept after a cut and a credit spread sold the same way are one bet. Chapter 7 said it for the seller's day and Chapter 15 said it for the buyer's attack; it is said a third time here because a trader who has learned to stand on both banks is the one most tempted to stand on both at once, and to call it balance.

The oldest insurance houses are centuries old, and none of them lasted by being right about the weather. They lasted by writing business they could price, refusing business they could not, keeping reserves through good years, paying claims through bad ones, and reviewing their ledgers on a calendar rather than in the heat of a loss. None of it predicts the market. All of it is what lets the business keep doing what this book began with: being paid to cover a risk, a thousand times, and still being open on the day it is wrong. The buyer's version of the hundred-year company is the same sentence turned around: paying for a right, a thousand times, small enough to be wrong most of them, and still present on the day the door opens.

The first edition closed on a sentence: neither side is the right side; each is a way of being wrong that can be survived, if it is counted. Part V lets the sentence be finished. Neither side is the right side. Each is a way of being wrong that can be survived, if it is counted. The seller is wrong rarely and expensively, and survives by the limit, the reserve and the wing. The buyer is wrong often and cheaply, and survives by the size, the date and the count. The price of fire decides which way of being wrong is being paid for today. The skill is knowing which one the price is paying for, standing on that bank and no other, and turning, in the temple and on the evidence, when the river does.
