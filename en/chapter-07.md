# 7. Manoeuvring

*Part III: The March*

1. There is nothing harder than manoeuvring. The difficulty consists in turning the devious into the direct, and misfortune into gain.

2. The seller's manoeuvre is the hedge: the devious route by which a losing morning is turned into a flat afternoon. It is slow, it is unglamorous, and it is the whole of the skill.

3. If you march your army in haste to seize an advantage, you will arrive with a tenth of your force. The trader who chases every move arrives at the important one with no capital and no temper.

4. An army without its baggage train is lost. A seller without his fourth quarter — the room to sit through a fluctuation — is closed out by his own broker in the hour before the trade resolves.

5. We cannot enter into alliance until we are acquainted with the designs of our neighbours. We cannot sell a line until we are acquainted with the walls of the day, the expected move, and the event on the calendar.

6. Let your rapidity be that of the wind, your compactness that of the forest. Be rapid in cutting. Be compact in size.

7. In raiding, divide your spoils. In taking a credit, take it: the seller who waits for the last tenth of a credit is paid a tenth and risks the whole.

8. He who knows the art of the direct and the indirect will win. The direct is the trend spread. The indirect is the condor. Neither is an addition to the other; each is a substitution.

9. The seller who sells a second spread beside a losing one has not manoeuvred. He has doubled. A long wing and a credit sold with the trend are the same directional bet in two costumes; count the net direction, not the legs.

10. Never stack a second position on the same direction. Size over frequency. One exposure, hedged or cut, never repeated.

11. Do not trade non-stop. When the condor's balance breaks — when a short is cut or a hedge is sold — that is a resolution, not an invitation. An engine that re-enters every time its shape changes has no ceiling, and a trader without a ceiling has no floor.

12. A whole army may be robbed of its spirit; a commander may be robbed of his presence of mind. The tape robs the seller of presence of mind by moving quickly. His defence is that his decisions were made in the temple, slowly.

13. Do not pursue an enemy who simulates flight. Do not chase a reversal that has not confirmed. Do not attack soldiers whose temper is keen; do not sell into a panic that is still accelerating. Leave a way of escape to a surrounded enemy; leave a wing in every spread.

## Commentary

Manoeuvring is the chapter about frequency, and frequency is a risk dial that most traders do not know they are turning. The old general loses his baggage by marching too fast; the seller loses his fourth quarter by trading too often. Every additional entry in a day is a levy on the reserve that was supposed to let an open position breathe. By noon the fast marcher is full size in one direction with nothing left for the hedge, and the first ordinary fluctuation closes him out.

Two rules in this chapter are absolute. The first is never to stack on the same direction. It is easy to believe that a second credit spread, sold with the trend, is "pairing" with the wing that survived a cut — but a long wing and a spread sold in the same direction are the same bet, and pairing them is doubling on the side that has just run through a strike. Read a book by its net exposure, not its leg count. The second is the ceiling: the position's own state must limit how many times it can open, and "usually stops re-entering" is not a rule.

Rapid as the wind is about one action only: the cut. Everything else in this trade is meant to be slow — the temple count, the line chosen while the tape is calm, the hedge sold only when hurt. Speed kept for the exit, and nowhere else, is what separates manoeuvre from panic.
