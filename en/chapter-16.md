# 16. The Attacker's Tax

*Part V: Attack and Defence*

## The Buyer

1. Poverty of the state exchequer causes an army to be maintained by contributions from a distance. Contributing to maintain an army at a distance causes the people to be impoverished.

2. The attacker's army is maintained at a distance: he pays now for a battle that comes later, if it comes. Every day of the wait is a contribution, and the contributions impoverish him whether or not the battle is won.

3. Most attacks fail. Not because buyers are foolish, but because a tax is levied on the attack before it begins, and most attackers never count it.

4. The tax has a name: the variance premium, the seller's wage of Chapter 6, read from the buyer's bank.

5. It is paid in four ways, and a fifth that pays twice.

6. The first is rent. The option loses a little of what is left every day the move does not come. At a twentieth a day, half is gone in two weeks.

7. The second is paying the spike. The buyer who arrives when fear is loudest pays the fear's price, and the crush after the news takes it back even when he was right about the direction.

8. The third is the late or the dateless entry: the option bought after the move, when the herd has priced it, or bought with no date at all, so that rent is paid on a reason with no day to arrive.

9. The fourth is the oversized debit. A low win rate means long losing runs. The buyer sized for a few attempts is beaten before his door opens.

10. The fifth is the roll: the expired call replaced with next month's, at a fresh debit, on the same reason. It pays rent twice on one thesis, and the second payment is made on a reason that has already been tested and failed.

11. Each tax has a discipline against it. Against rent, the time stop and no more time than the catalyst needs. Against the spike, buying before the fear, or the spread that hands part of it back. Against the late entry, the dated door and the refusal of bait. Against size, a debit small enough to lose many times in a row. Against the roll, the rule that the reason ends the trade.

12. None of the disciplines makes buying profitable. They remove the ways of losing that have nothing to do with the market.

13. *The beauty trap* (第31计 美人计). A beauty is not found; she is prepared: the dress, the lamp, the angle of the face. So is a backtest of long options, whose few enormous winners make a decade look like a method.

14. Ask in how many rooms she has been seen. Fix the rule on one stretch of history, judge it on another, and keep a third untouched until the last day. Prefer the curve with a scar in it. A method that never lost in the sample was not tested; it was arranged.

15. *Inflict injury on oneself to win trust* (第34计 苦肉计). The stop is the buyer's wound, taken by his own hand before the enemy can take it for him. A small loss paid at the stated price buys what the market does not sell: the right to remain in the war.

16. After thirty to fifty trades, set the hit rate against the breakeven win rate. If the setup is not above that line by a comfortable margin, after costs, it is killed. The kill rule is written while the setup is still beautiful. Written afterwards, it is a eulogy.

17. *The empty fort* (第32计 空城计). In a losing run the account has nothing to show. Show nothing: fewer trades, smaller, slower. The position not taken cannot lose, needs no stop, and pays no rent.

18. *If all else fails, retreat* (第36计 走为上). When "I have to make it back" speaks, the defeat is no longer on the field. It is in the man. Revenge is a position with no stop and no catalyst. Retreat to paper, or to a size too small to hurt, until the trades can be read without shame.

19. The losing run is not bad luck. It is the arithmetic of the win rate, and the buyer who has not sized for it has not been unlucky. He has been counted.

## The Seller

20. The seller reads this chapter as the source of his wage.

21. Every tax the buyer pays is collected by someone, and on most days the someone is the seller. Rent is his wage. The spike is his best day to write. The late buyer is his favourite customer.

22. Let him also read it as the reason the wage is small. The tax is spread across many buyers, and many sellers compete to collect it. What is left after the competition is a thin margin, and the seller pays it back, in the storm, to the few buyers who were early.

23. The seller pays taxes too: the costs of every round trip, the claims that cluster, the catastrophe that comes after the longest calm. His combined ratio is the attacker's tax, less his own.

24. A seller who sees the buyer's tax and concludes that selling cannot lose has read half the ledger. The buyers pay a little most days. The seller pays a lot on a few. Both columns are real.

## Commentary

The general's chapter on the cost of war is about an army maintained at a distance, and the buyer's army is always at a distance. He pays now, in full, for a battle that may come in a week, a month, or not at all. That is the attacker's position by construction, and it carries a tax. The variance premium, which Chapter 6 counted as the seller's wage, reads from the buyer's bank as a levy on every attack, collected before the first shot.

The four taxes were spread across the thirteen chapters of this book, one or two at a time: rent in Chapter 2, the spike in Chapters 10 and 12, the late entry in Chapters 6 and 7, size in Chapter 4. Set out together they are one argument, and the argument explains why most buyers lose without any of them being wrong about the market. A buyer can be right about direction and lose to rent, because the move came after his option expired. He can be right about direction and timing and lose to the spike, because he paid for a bigger move than came. He can be right about everything and lose to size, because the run of losses before his winner took the account below the size at which the winner could repair it. None of these is a forecasting error. Each is a tax paid without counting it.

The same five failures run through the plainer books on buying that sit on the same shelf as this one: leverage taken for courage, the right idea at the wrong time, volatility that moved against the option even when price moved for it, structures so clever that one leg undid another, and markets too thin to leave. Each is a way of losing that has nothing to do with being wrong about the stock. The last two deserve a word here. A shape is too clever when the buyer cannot say, in one breath, what each leg is for; a calendar spread bought for direction or a ratio bought for protection is a tax disguised as a trade. And a market too thin to leave taxes the buyer twice, on the way in and on the way out, at the moment he most needs to move. The tiger's mountain of Chapter 15 is that tax, and it is levied whether the thesis is right or wrong.

The roll gets its own verse because it is the tax the buyer levies on himself. The option has expired, the reason has been tested and has failed, and the buyer buys next month's at a fresh debit because the story still sounds good. It is not patience. It is paying rent twice on one house, and the second rent is paid on a house that has already burned. The rule that answers it was set in Chapter 8: when the reason is gone, the option is gone.

The losing run is the arithmetic that decides whether a buyer can survive any of this, and it belongs here, said once. Suppose a buyer's tickets pay five times their debit when right, so that he breaks even at one win in five. At that hit rate, the chance that any particular string of ten trades is all losses is 0.8 multiplied by itself ten times, which is just over a tenth. Across a hundred attempts, the chance that a run of ten straight losses turns up somewhere is better than nine in ten. It is not a disaster scenario. It is what one in five looks like. Now size it. At two percent of the account per debit, ten total losses leave about 82% of the account. At ten percent per debit, they leave about 35%, and the winner that follows is measured against that smaller base. Chapter 12 gave the rest: minus sixty-five needs nearly plus two hundred. A buyer sized at ten percent who meets the ordinary run has not been unlucky. He has been counted.

The expectancy that sits behind all of this is one line: the hit rate times the average win, minus the miss rate times the average loss, minus costs. The hit rate in that line is estimated from the buyer's own sample, which is small and uncertain, and that is why the four stratagems of the last set belong in this chapter. The beauty is the backtest that answers a narrower question than the one asked: in-sample, the data have already been seen by the person choosing the filters, so the fit is a record of the seeing. For a buyer the temptation is sharper, because a handful of enormous winners can make a decade of long calls look like a method. In one options pilot, a single spread returned 148% while the median trade lost 94%. Shown the winner alone, anyone would call the method promising. Shown the median, anyone would call it broken. Both numbers were true, and the test of the method is not whether most trades won but whether the few winners paid for all the losers, after costs, in a sample large enough to trust.

The self-inflicted wound is the stop and the kill rule together. The first injury is the stop, taken at the stated price, which keeps the account's word intact. The second is the count, after thirty to fifty trades, of the hit rate against the breakeven win rate. On a debit spread the breakeven is the debit divided by the width, so the comparison needs no opinion, only room for costs; a hit rate that only just clears the line does not clear it. The third injury is the kill. A setup traded for a year becomes part of the trader's idea of himself, and killing it feels like admitting the year was wasted, so it is granted one more trade. The rule is written at the beginning, while the setup is still a hypothesis and not an identity.

The empty fort and the retreat are the two answers to a losing run that is already under way. The empty fort is not a trick that works because the enemy is stupid. It works because the fort really is empty. When the account has no edge that meets the standard, the honest posture is no position, and the small position taken to look busy is the pretended garrison, found out at the next open. Retreat is the last of the thirty-six and the first in importance. In a drawdown the danger is not the percentage lost; it is the hurry that arrives with it, which makes positions larger, horizons shorter and stops looser all at once. There are two retreats, to paper and to minimum size, and neither leaves the market. Both leave the self that took over.

The seller reads this chapter twice. First as the source of his wage: every tax the buyer pays is collected, and on most days he collects it. Then as the reason his wage is small. The tax is spread across a crowd of buyers and competed for by a crowd of sellers, and what reaches any one seller is a thin margin, which he hands back, in the storm, to the few buyers who paid their tax early and held their tickets through the calm. Both columns of the ledger are real. The buyers pay a little most days; the seller pays a lot on a few. Neither side has found a way of being paid for nothing, and neither will.
