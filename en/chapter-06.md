# 6. Weak Points and Strong

*Part II: Shape and Force*

## The Seller

1. Whoever is first in the field and awaits the enemy will be fresh for the fight; whoever comes second and has to hasten to battle will arrive exhausted.

2. The seller who sets his line while the tape is calm chooses his ground. The seller who chases a move that has already happened is sold the worst price of the day, and calls it conviction.

3. By holding out advantages to him, you can cause the enemy to approach of his own accord. The market approaches your line of its own accord; you do not go to meet it.

4. You can be sure of succeeding in your attacks if you only attack places which are undefended. The seller attacks undefended ground: the strike the market must travel far to reach, beyond the wall where dealer hedging leans against it.

5. You can ensure the safety of your defence if you only hold positions that cannot be attacked. A line far enough, with a wing behind it, cannot be attacked by an ordinary day. It can only be attacked by the exceptional one, and for that, the wing.

6. Do not repeat the tactics which have gained you one victory, but let your methods be regulated by the infinite variety of circumstances. The line that held yesterday is not the line for today. The walls move.

7. Military tactics are like water; for water in its natural course runs away from high places and hastens downwards. So in trading: avoid what is strong and strike at what is weak. Avoid the direction the crowd has paid for; sell the range the crowd has abandoned.

8. Water shapes its course according to the nature of the ground over which it flows. The seller shapes his position according to the enemy he is facing.

9. Weak and strong have a price. Every policy has two: the premium the buyer pays, and the loss cost, which is what the policy will pay out on average. The business lives in the gap between them.

10. The premium is the market's fear. In an option it is called implied volatility: the size of move the buyers are paying to be covered against. The loss cost is the market's truth: the move that actually comes, realised volatility, measured after the fact.

11. When fear is larger than truth, the seller is paid for writing. When truth is larger than fear, every policy written is a gift to the buyer.

12. Fear is usually larger than truth, because people pay more to sleep than the storm will cost them. That is not a law of nature. It is a habit of buyers, and habits break.

13. Keep your own table. An insurer prices from his own claims history, not from the salesman's brochure. The seller's table is a record of how far the market actually travelled over the terms he writes, and how often it reached the strikes he sells.

14. If one week in ten the market reached the strike, and the average claim when it did was three, the loss cost is three tenths. A premium of three tenths is a policy written for nothing. A premium of a half is a business.

15. Never price a policy from the premium alone. A rich premium on a risk that is about to happen is not rich. It is accurate. And when premium is thin, the right size is small, and the right number is often none.

16. Therefore the seller does not predict. He responds. The first spread is a read. The hedge is a response. The cut is a response. The wing left to ride is a response. Nothing in the book is a forecast.

17. He who can modify his tactics in relation to his opponent and thereby succeed in winning may be called a heaven-born captain. He who can turn a directional spread into a condor, and a condor into a lone wing riding a trend, without ever adding to what is losing, may be called a seller.

18. The five elements are not always equally predominant; the four seasons make way for each other in turn. The market has its seasons too: flat is its ordinary one, and direction is a treat.

## The Buyer

19. The buyer, too, should be first in the field. Buy while the tape is calm and the rent is cheap; do not buy after the move, when everyone who is late is bidding for the same right.

20. Attack undefended places. The buyer attacks the move the sellers have stopped defending: the option whose implied volatility sits below what the calendar is likely to deliver.

21. Avoid what is strong. The strong place for the buyer is the expensive option, whose price already contains the move he expects.

22. *Take the goat in passing* (第12计 顺手牵羊). The column passes the village at dusk; a goat stands by the road; a soldier leads it away without breaking step. The seller's goat is the volatility premium, and most days it stands by his road, not the buyer's.

23. The goat is the buyer's only when it is untended: after a crush, when implied sits below realised and a dated event still lies ahead. Quiet is not cheap. A stock at a three-year low in realised volatility is a description, not a signal.

24. *Hide a dagger behind a smile* (第10计 笑里藏刀). The far weekly smiles: a few cents on the screen buy a fortune at the far strike. The smile is the price. The dagger is the probability.

25. The far strike must be crossed twice, once by the stock and once by the clock. The first is unlikely. The second is certain. Cheapness is not a gift. It is a quote for a thing that almost never happens, made by people who have counted.

26. *Secretly cross at Chencang* (第8计 暗渡陈仓). The crowd repairs the plank road of the earnings date in plain sight and looks only at the gap. The drift that follows is the road nobody watches, and it is bought cheaper after the print, when the fear has left the price.

27. *Create something from nothing* (第7计 无中生有). Straw men on the wall drew the besiegers' arrows. A backtest turned until it rises is a straw man at dusk. Write the rule, the exits and the test that would kill it before you look, and date the page.

28. Water runs away from high places. The buyer's water runs toward cheap volatility and away from rich.

29. The trader who held the wing left by a seller's cut was already a buyer, whether or not he called himself one. That wing is the one long option in this book bought for nothing, because the credit paid for it.

30. Therefore the buyer does not predict either. He prices. The question is not whether the market will move, but whether it will move more than the option's price says.

## Commentary

Weak points and strong is the chapter of the hedge. The old general's water finds the low ground; the seller's position finds the side of the market that is not moving. When the first spread, sold in the direction of the trend, begins to hurt, the answer is not to defend it and not to double it. It is to sell the other side. The bet becomes a condor, the credit doubles, and a bad morning becomes a flat afternoon. The second side is a reaction to being hurt, never a schedule; a condor sold at the open is a different trade with twice the risk and none of the information.

From the condor there are two futures. If the market goes flat, both credits are collected and the day is ordinary. If the trend resumes and runs through a line, the seller does not cut the losing vertical whole. He buys back the short leg only and keeps the wing, which is cheap, already paid for, and now the only thing in the book that profits if the move continues. Paired with the winning credit spread on the other side, it is a net long riding the very move that hurt him.

"Do not repeat the tactics that won" has a dull, exact meaning: the strikes that were safe yesterday are not information about today. The walls of dealer positioning move overnight, the expected move changes with volatility, and the line must be chosen again every morning, in the temple, before the first order.

The insurer gives "weak and strong" a number. Every insurer in history has been in one business: collect slightly more in premium than will be paid out in claims, across enough policies for the difference to become reliable. The option seller's version of that margin has a name, the gap between implied and realised volatility, and a long-documented habit: over long stretches, buyers of index protection have usually paid more than the moves they were protected against turned out to cost. The habit is the seller's wage. It is not guaranteed, and it is thinnest exactly when the market feels calmest.

The table in verse 14 is the actuarial sum in its simplest form, and it can be redone with your own numbers. If the market reached the strike one week in ten, and the average loss on the spread when it did was three, each policy costs three tenths on average. Sell it for three tenths and you have built a machine that pays your commissions. Sell it for a half and the margin is two tenths a policy, before costs. The danger is that the table is made of the past. A calm month makes every premium look generous, and a seller who prices from the last month alone writes his cheapest policies just before his most expensive claims. The cure is a long table, an honest one, and a willingness to write nothing.

For the buyer, weak and strong are measured in the same price from the other side. The weak point is the option priced for a calmer future than the calendar promises; the strong point is the one priced for exactly the move he expects, or more. Buying the strong point is not a mistake about direction. It is paying full price for a right the seller has already charged for, and it is the most common way to be right about the market and still lose money.

The Counted Trade's second set of stratagems is a single lesson about reading the other side's price, and four of them belong here. The goat is the variance premium: most days it is the seller's, and the buyer who pays for calm is buying fair-weather insurance from someone who knows the weather. The exception has a shape, not a slogan: implied below realised, after a crush, with a dated event still ahead. The dagger is the far weekly, cheap in dollars because it is unlikely, not because its volatility is cheap; the smile on the volatility surface is the honest name of that cheapness. Chencang is the quiet road after the event, where the same directional thesis is bought without paying for the gate. And the straw men are the backtest that was turned until it rose. A pre-registered test does not create an edge. It makes the absence of one visible, which is worth more than most edges, and Chapter 15 records one such test that failed.

The wing that survives the seller's cut is where the two halves of this book meet. It was bought as insurance, paid for by the credit, and on the day the line breaks it becomes the only long option on the board that cost nothing to hold. A seller who has learned to let that wing ride has learned the buyer's whole art in one position: a small, paid-for right, held only while the move that justifies it is still under way.
