# 6. Weak Points and Strong

*Part II: Shape and Force*

1. Whoever is first in the field and awaits the enemy will be fresh for the fight; whoever comes second and has to hasten to battle will arrive exhausted.

2. The seller who sets his line while the tape is calm chooses his ground. The seller who chases a move that has already happened is sold the worst price of the day, and calls it conviction.

3. By holding out advantages to him, you can cause the enemy to approach of his own accord. The market approaches your line of its own accord; you do not go to meet it.

4. You can be sure of succeeding in your attacks if you only attack places which are undefended. The seller attacks undefended ground: the strike the market must travel far to reach, beyond the wall where dealer hedging leans against it.

5. You can ensure the safety of your defence if you only hold positions that cannot be attacked. A line far enough, with a wing behind it, cannot be attacked by an ordinary day. It can only be attacked by the exceptional one — and for that, the wing.

6. Do not repeat the tactics which have gained you one victory, but let your methods be regulated by the infinite variety of circumstances. The line that held yesterday is not the line for today. The walls move.

7. Military tactics are like water; for water in its natural course runs away from high places and hastens downwards. So in trading: avoid what is strong and strike at what is weak. Avoid the direction the crowd has paid for; sell the range the crowd has abandoned.

8. Water shapes its course according to the nature of the ground over which it flows. The seller shapes his position according to the enemy he is facing.

9. Therefore the seller does not predict. He responds. The first spread is a read. The hedge is a response. The cut is a response. The wing left to ride is a response. Nothing in the book is a forecast.

10. He who can modify his tactics in relation to his opponent and thereby succeed in winning may be called a heaven-born captain. He who can turn a directional spread into a condor, and a condor into a lone wing riding a trend, without ever adding to what is losing, may be called a seller.

11. The five elements are not always equally predominant; the four seasons make way for each other in turn. The market has its seasons too: flat is its ordinary one, and direction is a treat.

## Commentary

Weak points and strong is the chapter of the hedge. The old general's water finds the low ground; the seller's position finds the side of the market that is not moving. When the first spread — sold in the direction of the trend — begins to hurt, the answer is not to defend it and not to double it. It is to sell the other side. The bet becomes a condor, the credit doubles, and a bad morning becomes a flat afternoon. The second side is a reaction to being hurt, never a schedule; a condor sold at the open is a different trade with twice the risk and none of the information.

From the condor there are two futures. If the market goes flat, both credits are collected and the day is ordinary. If the trend resumes and runs through a line, the seller does not cut the losing vertical whole. He buys back the short leg only and keeps the wing — which is cheap, already paid for, and now the only thing in the book that profits if the move continues. Paired with the winning credit spread on the other side, it is a net long riding the very move that hurt him.

"Do not repeat the tactics that won" has a dull, exact meaning: the strikes that were safe yesterday are not information about today. The walls of dealer positioning move overnight, the expected move changes with volatility, and the line must be chosen again every morning, in the temple, before the first order.
